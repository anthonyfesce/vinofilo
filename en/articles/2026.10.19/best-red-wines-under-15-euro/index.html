The best red wines under 15 euro, a map by appellation

From Montepulciano d’Abruzzo to Cirò, from Barbera d’Asti to the Beaujolais crus, the red appellations that give the most when the budget is 15 euro.

19 October 2026 · 5 min read

Anyone who types “best red wines under 15 euro” usually wants a name to copy into their phone. The trouble is that names change, vintages too, prices even more so, and a list of bottles ages worse than a rosé forgotten in the car. What does stay stable is geography. There are appellations where 15 euro buys the best the area can do, and others where the same sum barely buys you through the door. Learning to tell them apart is worth more than any ranking.

An honest preamble on prices. Price bands vary from shop to shop and from year to year, and for many appellations there are no reliable public surveys by style. So the reasoning here is about positioning, not price tags: where the 15-euro threshold is comfortable, where it is borderline, where it is an illusion.

The younger sibling with nothing to envy

The smartest move with a fixed budget is to look for the “second wine” of the great areas. The textbook case is Rosso di Montalcino. Same territory as Brunello, same grape, the pure Sangiovese required by the production rules, but with an obligation to wait only until 1 September of the year after the harvest instead of the years of ageing demanded of its big brother. It has been a DOC since 1983 and is no fallback: many estates use it to convey the character of the vintage without the trick of long oak ageing.

That said, Rosso di Montalcino sits on the edge of our threshold, not inside it. When the trade press compiles its selections of the best, it tends to set the ceiling at 20 euro, a sign that you can find them under 15, but you have to look. Much the same goes for Ripasso della Valpolicella, a DOC since 2009: a Valpolicella refermented on the pomace left over from Amarone, with more body and more alcohol than plain Valpolicella. It is the cheapest way to get close to the area’s style, provided you know that versions differ greatly from one another and the price swings quite a bit.

The Centre-South, where 15 euro is serious money

If there is one territory where 15 euro stops being a limit and becomes a luxury, it is central and southern Italy. Montepulciano d’Abruzzo, a DOC since 1968, requires at least 85% of the grape of the same name and has a Riserva version with at least two years of ageing, nine months of it in wood. It is one of the most widespread Italian reds on the shelves, and the basic version is not the one that needs a full budget: with 15 euro you can aim for the Riserva, or for the more ambitious subzone, Colline Teramane, promoted to DOCG in 2003. It is the difference between buying a region’s everyday wine and buying its Sunday wine.

The same reasoning applies to Cirò, in Calabria, built on Gaglioppo: a red with firm tannin and a colour often paler than one expects from the South, which gives the lie to every cliché about southern wines being all jam. In the trade press’s selections of supermarket wines at around 15 euro it appears in its Classico Superiore version, which is the one to look for. Alongside it, in the same price band, you often find Cesanese del Lazio, the most underrated grape around Rome, and Aglianico del Molise in its Riserva version, a way to drink Aglianico without paying for the name of the Vulture or Taurasi.

In Tuscany, Morellino di Scansano is the Maremma’s answer to Chianti: at least 85% Sangiovese, in the province of Grosseto, a DOC since 1978 and a DOCG from the 2007 vintage. A warmer climate, more generous fruit, less austerity. For those who find inland Sangiovese too angular, it is the right way in.

The North of light reds and border grapes

In the North the rule is different: the names to avoid are the famous ones, because at 15 euro a Barolo or an Amarone does not exist in any form worth having. It pays instead to look at what those same hills produce away from the spotlight. Barbera d’Asti, a DOCG since 2008 with at least 90% Barbera, is perhaps Piedmont’s most solid bargain: high acidity, soft tannin, a wine that stands up to everyday cooking without asking permission.

Further north, Alto Piemonte offers Nebbiolo at prices that would raise a smile in Barolo: Colline Novaresi is one of the names to bear in mind, with reds tauter and less muscular than those of the Langhe. And for lovers of reds to drink cool, Alto Adige has Lago di Caldaro, made from Schiava: pale colour, little tannin, no pretensions to greatness. It is the kind of wine a certain snobbery regards as minor, and which at the summer table almost always wins.

In Sardinia, finally, Mandrolisai brings together Bovale, Cannonau and Monica in a red from the centre of the island that hardly anyone knows outside the region. And that is exactly why it costs what it costs.

Across the border, three safe bets

France is not just an out-of-reach Bordeaux and Burgundy. In Beaujolais the ten crus, from Saint-Amour to Moulin-à-Vent by way of Fleurie, Morgon and Brouilly, are hillside Gamays that share only the grape with Beaujolais Nouveau. Often the word Beaujolais does not even appear on the label: there is just the name of the village, and that is what to look for.

In the Rhône valley, Côtes du Rhône Villages requires at least 50% Grenache and a share of Syrah or Mourvèdre, with some villages allowed to add their own name to the label. It is the step above generic Côtes du Rhône, and the jump in quality usually outstrips the jump in price.

In Spain, Rioja Crianza is a guarantee of method more than of style: the regulations require at least two years of ageing, one of them in oak casks, before release. Tempranillo, wood, a certain roundness. It is not the most thrilling Rioja, but it rarely disappoints anyone looking for a rounded red.

The right threshold is where the area gives its best

My argument is simple, and perhaps unpopular with label collectors: there is no such thing as a good red under 15 euro in the absolute, there is an appellation where 15 euro is enough to reach the top. A Montepulciano d’Abruzzo Riserva or an ambitious Barbera d’Asti at that price is the best of its area. A wine from a famous appellation at the same price is almost always the worst of its own. Choosing well, on a fixed budget, means giving up the chase for the name and accepting to buy the top of the class at a less famous school.
