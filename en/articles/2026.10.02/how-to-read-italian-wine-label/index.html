How to read an Italian wine label without being seduced by it

DOCG, DOC, IGT, Riserva, Classico, vintage and QR code: what an Italian wine label really tells you, and what it keeps quiet about quality.

2 October 2026 · 5 min read

A wine label is a legal document dressed up as a business card. Every word printed on it has been authorised, regulated and sometimes haggled over for years between consortia and ministries; and yet most of us look at it the way we look at a book cover, hunting for a familiar name or a reassuring design. It is worth learning to read one properly, if only to discover how little it actually promises.

The pyramid: DOCG, DOC, IGT, Vino

The Italian system, overhauled by the Testo Unico del vino (Law 238 of 2016, the consolidated wine act), works in tiers of constraint. At the top sit the DOCGs, denominazioni di origine controllata e garantita (controlled and guaranteed designations of origin); just below them, the DOCs; then the IGTs, indicazioni geografiche tipiche (typical geographical indications); and at the base, plain “Vino”, with no protected geographical reference at all.

The difference between the rungs is not a mark out of ten, it is a rulebook, the disciplinare. DOC and DOCG impose a defined zone, permitted grape varieties, maximum yields per hectare, sometimes minimum ageing periods and cellar practices. DOCG adds tighter controls and the numbered strip around the bottle’s neck; to earn it, a designation must have been a DOC for at least seven years. IGT allows far more freedom over grapes and styles, asking above all for a link to a broad territory.

Here lies the first misconception to dismantle. Some of Italy’s most coveted reds were born as IGTs, or even as “vino da tavola”, table wine, precisely because their producers wanted to use grapes or blends the local rulebook did not allow. The pyramid measures how faithfully a wine obeys the rules of its place, not how good it is.

Riserva, Superiore, Classico: three words that sound like praise

These are the terms most likely to persuade you to spend a little more, so it pays to know what they mean, literally.

Riserva indicates a longer minimum ageing period than the basic version. By law that means at least two years for reds and one for whites; for sparkling wines, one year for the tank method and three for wines refermented in bottle. Many rulebooks demand more. But “aged for longer” is not the same as “better”: an aromatic white kept in the cellar past its moment loses precisely what made it interesting.

Superiore signals stricter rules in vineyard and cellar: a yield per hectare at least ten per cent lower and a higher minimum alcohol level. Lower yields tend to give more concentrated grapes, and that is a reasonable clue. It remains a clue.

Classico is the most misunderstood term of all. It is not a verdict on style or quality: it means the grapes come from the oldest zone of the designation, the historic core around which the area later expanded. The most famous case is Chianti Classico, whose zone was mapped out as early as the 1930s. To say “Classico” is to say “this is where it all began”. It is geography, often rather good geography, but it guarantees nothing about what ends up in your glass.

Alongside these you may find the name of a vigna, a single vineyard: it is allowed only if that plot is vinified separately and declared as such. Of all the words on the label, it is perhaps the most honest, because it narrows the field rather than widening it.

The vintage, and who put the wine in the bottle

The vintage is the year of the harvest, not the year of bottling. For designated wines, at least 85 per cent of the lot must come from the declared year. That margin surprises anyone who imagines absolute purity, but it is the European norm, and it gives wineries a modicum of flexibility.

More interesting, and far less read, is the small-print line stating who bottled the wine. “Integralmente prodotto” (wholly produced) is the strictest formula: grapes from the estate’s own vineyards, vinified and bottled on the estate. “Imbottigliato all’origine” (estate bottled) means the producer is also the bottler, with a majority of its own grapes or wine. Phrases such as “imbottigliato da” or “imbottigliato per” (bottled by, bottled for) followed by a name may instead point to a wine bought in and sold under a brand, which is no flaw in itself, but it is a different story from the one the label’s design invites you to imagine.

If I had to recommend a single habit, it would be this: turn the bottle round and read that line before the big fancy name on the front.

Ingredients and calories: the European novelty

Since 8 December 2023, under EU Regulation 2021/2117, wine has ceased to be the exception among foodstuffs. Bottles produced from that date must carry a nutrition declaration and a list of ingredients, including additives and processing aids such as sulphites. Wines produced earlier can stay on sale until stocks run out.

Space on a label is limited, however, and the legislator allowed a way out: the bottle must show the energy value and the allergens; everything else can be entrusted to an electronic label reached via a QR code. That page has strict rules: it may not contain promotional material, nor collect or track the data of whoever consults it. It is worth scanning one at least once. You will find the list is almost always short, that sulphites are everywhere, and that the word “grapes” at the top of the list has a certain effect, when you stop to think about it.

What the label will never tell you

Now to the point. None of this information measures quality. The label does not tell you whether the grapes were picked by hand or by machine, whether the producer selected them bunch by bunch, how much new oak was used, or whether the bottle was stored well in the months between cellar and shelf. It does not tell you whether the wine is at its peak, still climbing or already in decline. Above all, it does not tell you whether you will like it.

Mediocre DOCGs exist, and there are plenty of them. Extraordinary IGTs exist too, and they are often the wines into which a producer has poured the most courage. The neck strip guarantees origin and compliance with a rulebook, not the talent of whoever applied it.

A label should be read the way you read a stranger’s CV: useful for understanding where they come from and the path they have taken, useless for knowing what they are really like. The name that matters, in the end, is almost always the producer’s, and the only way to get to know it is to open, taste, remember. The rules exist so that we are not deceived. Taste, on the other hand, is something you build for yourself.
