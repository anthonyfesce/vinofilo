Wine headaches: sulfites, histamine or alcohol to blame?

Red or white, sulfites, histamine, quercetin and alcohol: what research really says about wine headaches, the morning after and hangover remedies, with no miracle cures.

27 November 2026 · 4 min read

It is one of the most searched questions about wine, and also one of the worst answered. Whoever asks it usually already has a culprit in mind: sulfites, red wine, “chemical” wine as opposed to natural. The truth is less comfortable. Headaches linked to wine have several possible causes, the main one is almost always alcohol itself, and for several of the most popular theories the evidence is weak. One caveat applies to everything that follows: this is an article about wine, not a consultation. Anyone with frequent, severe or unusual headaches, or who suffers from migraine, should talk to their doctor.

The first suspect: alcohol

Wine is an alcoholic drink, and alcohol, in sufficient quantity, causes headaches on its own. It acts on the blood vessels, disrupts sleep, increases urine output and therefore fluid loss, and its first breakdown product in the body, acetaldehyde, is a toxic substance the body has to get rid of quickly. The classic “morning after” headache is part of the picture of a hangover, and its intensity follows above all one variable: how much you drank.

There is a detail that concerns wine closely. In 2010 a Brown University study, published in Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research, compared bourbon and vodka at equal alcohol levels: those who had drunk the spirit richer in congeners, the substances other than ethanol formed during fermentation and ageing, reported worse hangovers the next day. Wine too contains substances other than ethanol, but the study was not about wine, and the main effect remained that of alcohol.

Sulfites, the scapegoat

They are the most accused, and probably the least guilty. As we explained when discussing the words “contains sulfites”, there is a real sensitivity to these substances, which the US FDA estimates at about one person in a hundred, more frequent among asthmatics; but the reactions described are mainly respiratory, not the classic headache. And there is a clue that gives pause: by law whites may contain more sulfites than reds, yet the most common complaint concerns reds. Anyone with a confirmed sensitivity must of course take it into account and follow their doctor’s advice.

Histamine: a suspicion without solid evidence

Histamine is a substance found in many fermented foods, wine included, and its content varies greatly from bottle to bottle. That is why for decades it has been blamed for so-called wine intolerances. Controlled studies, however, do not bear this out. In 2001 a group from the hospital in Nancy published in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology a double-blind trial on 16 people who described themselves as wine-intolerant: drinking a wine low in histamine and one rich in it, their reactions did not change. A 2003 review in Annals of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology reached a similar conclusion for biogenic amines in general.

This does not rule out individual cases. The literature describes patients with a reduced ability to break down histamine who react to foods and wines rich in it, as in a case published in 1996 in International Archives of Allergy and Immunology. But these are conditions to be diagnosed with a doctor, not self-diagnosed by looking at the colour of the wine.

Red wine and the quercetin lead

Why, then, do so many people say they are fine with white and suffer with red? In 2023 researchers at the University of California, Davis, put forward a new hypothesis in Scientific Reports. Red wine contains far more quercetin, a flavonoid from the skins, than white wine and other alcoholic drinks. Once in the blood, one of its derivatives inhibits, in the laboratory, the enzyme ALDH2, the one that disposes of acetaldehyde. If the enzyme slows down, acetaldehyde builds up, and in some people it might trigger a headache. It is the same mechanism that explains the flushing and discomfort of people who carry a low-activity variant of the enzyme.

It is an interesting hypothesis, but that is all it is: the authors themselves write that evidence in humans is needed.

And white wine? Those who say they suffer more with white have, at present, no dedicated scientific explanation. Quantity may count, the speed at which a cool, light wine is drunk, drinking on an empty stomach at aperitif time. But these are possibilities, not research findings.

The morning after, and the remedies

Here the science is clear, and a little disappointing. A systematic review published in 2022 in Addiction by researchers at King’s College London examined 21 controlled studies of hangover remedies: the evidence in favour of any substance was found to be of very low quality. No supplement, extract or pill has convincingly been shown to prevent or cure a hangover.

What remains is common sense, not medicine: drink less, drink more slowly, eat, alternate wine with water. As for over-the-counter headache medicines, especially in combination with alcohol, the only person to listen to is your doctor or pharmacist.

Our conclusion

Wine headaches are real, but hunting for a single culprit is almost always a shortcut. Blaming sulfites, histamine or “industrial” wine is reassuring, because it suggests that changing labels is enough. More often the answer lies in the number of glasses. That said, if a specific wine makes you feel unwell even in modest amounts, it is not an obsession: it is useful information to take to your doctor, together with what you ate and how much you slept.
