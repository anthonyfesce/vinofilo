It was meant to be a modest publicity stunt, a way to get people talking about an English wine merchant and his Paris wine school in the year of America’s bicentennial. It became the most quoted tasting in history, with a film, a place in a museum and fifty years of argument. The Judgment of Paris, on 24 May 1976, did not prove that Californian wine was better than French. It proved something more uncomfortable: that blind, with no label in sight, nobody can be sure.

## An Englishman in Paris

Steven Spurrier was a British wine merchant who had put down roots in Paris, where he had founded the Académie du Vin, a wine school aimed mainly at foreigners. Helping him organise the event was Patricia Gallagher, an American. The idea was simple: to mark 200 years of United States independence, pit some of California’s finest wines against great French names, in front of a French jury.

There were two flights. On one side, Californian Cabernet Sauvignon against red Bordeaux; on the other, Californian Chardonnay against white Burgundy. Ten wines per flight, served blind, each marked out of 20 with no shared scoring framework.

The panel numbered eleven, but only the nine French judges’ scores counted. And they were not just any nine: among them were Aubert de Villaine, one of Burgundy’s best-known figures, Odette Kahn, Raymond Oliver, Jean-Claude Vrinat and Christian Vannequé. People from Michelin-starred restaurants, wine magazines and great cellars. Which is precisely why nobody expected surprises.

## The verdict

Among the whites, the winner was the 1973 Chardonnay from Chateau Montelena in the Napa Valley, made by Mike Grgich. Second came a 1973 Meursault Charmes from Roulot, third another Californian, Chalone 1974.

Among the reds, first place went to the 1973 Cabernet Sauvignon from Stag's Leap Wine Cellars, Warren Winiarski’s wine. Close behind came Château Mouton-Rothschild 1970 and Château Haut-Brion 1970, then Château Montrose 1970. Fifth was Ridge Monte Bello 1971, a name that would come back.

Only one journalist was there: George M. Taber of *Time*. He published the story straight away, and the event went down in history under a name that plays on the Greek myth of the Judgement of Paris. The French press reacted with disdain; *Le Figaro* called the results “laughable”. In California, by contrast, those few lines landed like an honorary degree.

## The numbers, under the microscope

Half a century on, it is worth telling the less celebrated part of the story too. The economists Orley Ashenfelter and Richard Quandt reanalysed the scores statistically and concluded that only the top two wines and the last one in each flight genuinely stood apart; the other seven were, in practice, indistinguishable. A more recent reading, by Richard Ballantyne MW, argues that there were arithmetic errors in the white totals and that, counting only the nine French judges, Montelena and Roulot’s Meursault would have finished level. This is no small detail, and it deserves mention: the legend has a few arithmetical cracks.

But the substance stands. France’s most qualified palates, tasting blind, had placed Californian wines on a par with the best of Bordeaux and Burgundy. And the rematches confirmed the trend. In 1978, in San Francisco, Stag's Leap 1973 again came top among the reds. In 2006, for the 30th anniversary, the reds were tasted again in parallel in Napa and in London: Ridge Monte Bello 1971 won on both continents, while the best-placed Bordeaux, Mouton-Rothschild 1970, finished sixth. Anyone who feared that Californian wines could not age got a fairly emphatic answer.

## What it changed, Italy included

The first effect was on California. According to the Napa Valley’s tourism body, the valley had between 60 and 70 wineries in the 1970s and has more than 500 today; not all of that is down to Paris, of course, but Paris brought credibility and international attention. Bottles of both winning wines entered the collections of the Smithsonian Institution, and in 2008 the story, heavily fictionalised, became a film, *Bottle Shock*, with Alan Rickman as Spurrier.

The second effect is subtler, and concerns every country that was not France. The Judgment of Paris legitimised an idea: that a great wine can be born wherever there is the right soil, a suitable climate and someone working with rigour. For an Italian reader the parallel is obvious. In 1978, two years later, Sassicaia won a blind tasting organised by *Decanter* against 33 other Bordeaux-style blends from around the world. It was the moment coastal Tuscany, and with it ambitious Italian wine, joined the international conversation. The road had been opened, unwittingly, by an English merchant in Paris.

Spurrier died in March 2021, aged 79. In 2026, for the 50th anniversary, the tasting was re-created privately in San Francisco: proof that the legend, cracks and all, still makes news.

## Why blind tasting remains the only honest test

The most enduring lesson of that afternoon is not about California. It is about those of us who drink. The label, the name, the price and the reputation weigh on our judgement far more than we like to admit, even when the judges are a country’s finest palates. Take all that away, and what remains is the wine in the glass.

My view is that the Judgment of Paris matters less as a ranking and far more as a method. It does not tell us who is best, because nine people on one afternoon cannot. It tells us that wine’s hierarchies are human constructions, useful but fragile, and that every so often someone has the right, perhaps the duty, to put them to the test by covering up the bottles.
