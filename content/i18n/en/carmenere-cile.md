For almost a century Chile made Merlot that was not Merlot. The vines grew, the bottles shipped, drinkers enjoyed them, and nobody knew that among the rows of the Central Valley survived a grape that its homeland, Bordeaux, had given up for lost. When a French ampelographer identified it in 1994, Chile discovered it had not a mistake to correct but an identity. It is one of the finest stories in modern wine, and it has an Italian chapter that few people know.

## A name from the colour of the leaves

The name Carmenère comes from the French *carmin*, crimson: it refers to the deep red the leaves turn in autumn before they fall. In nineteenth-century Bordeaux it was one of the grapes of the Médoc, alongside Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Merlot, Malbec and Petit Verdot, the family that still defines the style of Bordeaux reds. Genetic studies place it firmly within that family, with links to Merlot and the Cabernets, although sources do not entirely agree on some details of its pedigree.

Then came phylloxera, the tiny American insect that devastated Europe’s vineyards in the second half of the nineteenth century. Carmenère suffered more than most. When the Bordelais replanted, grafting onto American rootstock, they chose more regular, reliable grapes, and Carmenère, which ripens late and behaves badly in cool springs, all but disappeared. For decades it was considered extinct.

## The journey to Chile

But someone had already taken it elsewhere. In the nineteenth century Chilean landowners, enamoured of French models, imported cuttings from Bordeaux; according to the most widely cited accounts, in the 1850s cuttings that included Carmenère were planted in the valleys around Santiago. Chile had, and still has, a rare advantage: phylloxera never arrived, kept out by formidable natural barriers (the desert to the north, the Andes to the east, the ocean to the west, the ice to the south) and by a dry growing season. The vines could grow on their own roots.

In the vineyard, however, Carmenère became mixed up with Merlot, which it closely resembles. The two grapes were planted together, vinified together and sold as Merlot. People knew that certain plants behaved differently, ripening later, and some regarded them as a local clone, sometimes called “Merlot Peumal”. But nobody had put a name to the difference.

## 1994 and the discovery

The name came from Jean-Michel Boursiquot, a French ampelographer, a scholar who identifies vine varieties by the shape of their leaves, bunches and shoots. In 1994, on a visit to Chile, he recognised in those supposed Merlot vines the Carmenère that had vanished from Bordeaux. A few years later, in 1998, the Chilean ministry of agriculture officially recognised Carmenère as a distinct variety.

For the Chilean industry it was at once an embarrassment and an opportunity. An embarrassment, because part of the Merlot exported until then was not what the label said. An opportunity, because Chile now had a grape that no other country grew on a large scale. Argentina had Malbec, another Bordeaux exile turned national flag; Chile could have Carmenère. According to 2009 figures, the country had more than 8,800 hectares of it, the largest area in the world: a dated number by now, but it gives the order of magnitude.

In the glass, when ripe, Carmenère gives dark-coloured reds, soft in tannin, with black fruit, spice and a green note of bell pepper and herbs that is both its signature and its risk. Picked too early, that note takes over and the wine tastes green. Much of Chile’s work over the past thirty years has consisted precisely in learning when to harvest it and where to plant it.

## The Italian chapter

Here the story takes an unexpected turn. Carmenère had not survived only in Chile. In north-east Italy, above all in Veneto and Friuli-Venezia Giulia, the regions facing the Adriatic between Venice and the Slovenian border, many Bordeaux varieties had long been established. In those vineyards, what was called “Cabernet Franc” was in many cases, at least in part, Carmenère.

The best-documented episode involves Ca' del Bosco, a winery in Franciacorta, the Lombard area known for traditional-method sparkling wine. In 1990 the estate bought vines it believed were Cabernet Franc; they turned out to be Carmenère, and the wine made from them was christened Carmenero. For an international reader the anecdote is instructive: even in a country with an ampelographic culture as old as Italy’s, a grape’s identity could stay hidden under the wrong name for generations.

Today Italian Carmenère is a small presence, tied mainly to the reds of the north-east, where Bordeaux varieties have long lived alongside native ones. How much room individual DOC rules give it today is a detail worth checking case by case. It lacks Chile’s critical mass, but it tells the same story from another angle: a grape that survives not because someone protects it, but because nobody knows it is there.

## An identity not to be wasted

My view is that Carmenère is the perfect example of how a scientific discovery can become a heritage, but only if those who receive it know what to do with it. Chile has had thirty years to decide whether to make Carmenère a mass-market red or a wine of place, and the best answer has come from those who treated it as a demanding grape, not as an export label. As for Italy, it would be a shame to leave it in a corner: a vine that came through phylloxera under a false name deserves at least to be called by its own.
