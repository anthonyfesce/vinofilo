Say Friuli and anyone with a passing acquaintance with wine thinks of whites: Friulano, Ribolla, the great blends of the Collio. It is a fair reflex, and one we have indulged ourselves. But on the hills between Cividale, Prepotto and Rosazzo there is another story, rougher and less often told, of red grapes that at one point the law simply treated as non-existent. That you can buy them today is down to a handful of stubborn growers, a prize dreamt up by a family of distillers and a few vineyards planted outside the rules.

## Refosco, the elder brother

The most widespread and least endangered is Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso, named after the stalk that turns red as the grapes ripen. It is grown across much of Friuli’s denominations, from the Colli Orientali to the Grave, from Aquileia to Latisana, and it spills over into eastern Veneto with Lison Pramaggiore. Across the Slovenian border, around Koper, the local name for the family is Terrano.

Refosco, in any case, is not one grape but a clan: in the Colli Orientali alone there are at least six, not all of them close relatives. Refosco Nostrano, for instance, is a different variety that merely shares the name, and has its own sub-denomination tied to the village of Faedis. DNA analysis, on the other hand, has turned up an unexpected link: Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso is a parent of Marzemino, the Trentino red name-checked in Mozart’s Don Giovanni.

Ancient legends cling to its history too, such as the one making it a relative of the wine beloved of Livia, wife of Augustus. These are tales to be taken for what they are. The wine itself is more concrete: deep colour, high acidity, notes of plum and almond, a late-ripening grape that needs sunshine and copes well with autumn rain.

## Schioppettino, the grape that was planted in secret

Schioppettino was widespread in the upper Judrio valley until the First World War, then all but vanished. In 1977 Mario Soldati, who travelled Italy writing about its wines, found just three or four producers still making it. The problem was not only fashion: the variety was not on the authorised list, and planting it meant breaking the law.

That is exactly what Paolo Rapuzzi did at Cialla, a hamlet of Prepotto, in the early 1970s, planting a vineyard from cuttings taken from old vines. His first vintage, the 1975, was lost in the 1976 Friuli earthquake before anyone had even tasted it. But the gesture counted. In 1975 Benito and Giannola Nonino, a family of Friulian distillers, had founded the Risit d'Âur Prize, literally the “golden vine cutting”, with the stated aim of winning official recognition for Ribolla, Schioppettino, Pignolo and Tazzelenghe. The first award went to the Rapuzzis.

The pressure worked. According to the Nonino family’s account, a European Community regulation authorised the cultivation of Schioppettino, Pignolo, Tazzelenghe and Ribolla Gialla in the province of Udine from March 1978, and in 1983 these varieties moved onto the recommended list. Schioppettino remains tied to a few places, above all Prepotto, which has its own sub-zone within the Friuli Colli Orientali DOC. The wine has high acidity, fine tannins and a peppery finish that puts it closer to certain northern Rhône reds than to the Merlots of the plain.

## Pignolo, the hardest of them all

If Schioppettino is the elegant red, Pignolo is the one that never asks permission. Small berries, thick skins, low and irregular yields, and tannins so concentrated that critics compare them to Sagrantino’s. It was prized as early as the 14th century, and sources record that its wine fetched more than most others in the region. Then came the long slide: listed in the Associazione Agraria Friulana’s catalogue in 1863, dropped a few years later, left out of the replanting that followed phylloxera and oidium, and finally omitted from a 1970 European regulation that effectively made it illegal.

The rescue runs through the Abbey of Rosazzo, where just two vines had survived into the 1970s. In 1979 Walter Filiputti took over the abbey’s vineyard and within three years had turned those two plants into a planting of around 2,200 vines; from 1981 the Zamò family joined in managing it. Meanwhile Girolamo Dorigo, intrigued by something he read in a book in 1969, had already put in some 1,500 vines, he too outside the rules. Nor is that the only line of survival: as early as 1947 Domenico Casasola, whose family had once tended the abbey’s vines, passed cuttings to Luigi Moschioni.

Estimates of the area vary: around 80 hectares across Friuli according to the reference work “Wine Grapes”, a mere 44 in the Colli Orientali vineyard register for 2024. Tiny numbers either way. The DOC rules require at least three years’ ageing, but many producers wait a good deal longer.

## Tazzelenghe, the tongue-cutter

The name says it all: in Friulian it means “tongue-cutter”, a nod to acidity and tannin that take no prisoners. Tazzelenghe was described by Pietro di Maniago in 1823, shown at the Friulian Agricultural Society in 1863 and entered in Italy’s National Register of Grape Varieties in 1977. Genetic analysis in 2005 identified it as Refosco del Botton and found a parent-offspring relationship with Refosco di Faedis: the Refosco clan, once again, comes back.

Around 2000 there were some 7 hectares left, between Buttrio, Cividale and the Rosazzo valleys; by 2016 sources put the figure at about 45. It is permitted in the Friuli Colli Orientali DOC, and those who make it do so more out of conviction than calculation.

## A lesson that reaches beyond Friuli

My view is that these four reds tell you better than any white what “native” really means. Not a wine-list buzzword, but the outcome of deliberate choices: someone planting in secret, a family knocking on the doors of officialdom, an abbey keeping two vines alive. They are not easy wines, and not all of them will become famous. But the day we stop looking only for whites in Friuli, we will find that the region had its red answer ready all along. It just needed someone with the nerve to disobey.
