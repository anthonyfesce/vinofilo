There is an Italian wine that millions of people order every evening in London, New York or Toronto without wondering where it comes from. They don’t choose it for the region or the producer: they choose it because it is “a Pinot Grigio”, the way you might ask for “a coffee”. No other Italian white has become a generic noun in quite this way. It is an extraordinary success and, at the same time, a small problem, because a wine everyone knows is also a wine few people really look at.

## The numbers behind a phenomenon

Let’s start with a correction: Pinot Grigio is not Italy’s most exported wine overall. That title belongs to Prosecco. But Pinot Grigio is right behind it, second in Italy’s export rankings and first among still whites, and in the UK it sits firmly among the three best-selling grapes alongside Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc.

Its production heartland is the north-east, what Italians call the *Triveneto*: Veneto, Friuli Venezia Giulia and Trentino. Around 27,000 hectares of Pinot Grigio are planted here, roughly 85% of Italy’s output of the variety and, according to the producers’ consortium, about 40% of the world’s. In 2024 the Delle Venezie DOC alone bottled some 225 million bottles of still Pinot Grigio, plus small volumes of frizzante and sparkling. Almost all of it leaves the country: trade sources put the export share close to 95%. North America alone accounts for 43% of sales, the UK for about a quarter of bottles, Germany for a tenth.

Put simply, Italian Pinot Grigio is first and foremost a wine conceived and sold for people who aren’t Italian.

## It used to be pink: Ramato

The loveliest paradox in this story is that Pinot Grigio was not originally a white wine at all. The grape’s skin is not green: its berries are greyish-pink, hence the name *grigio*, “grey”. It is a colour mutation within the great Pinot family, closely related to Pinot Nero, and if the skins are left in contact with the juice they release a hue somewhere between copper and salmon pink.

In Friuli this was simply how it was made. It was called, and still is, *ramato*, from *rame*, “copper”, for that metallic glint in the glass. Skin contact can last anywhere from a day to two weeks, and the resulting wine has more aroma, more texture and a light tannic grip. Until the 1960s this was the prevailing style. Then history took another turn, and Ramato became a niche, now being rediscovered by drinkers looking for a Pinot Grigio with more personality and by fans of skin-contact wines (we covered them in our piece on orange wines, Ramato’s close cousins).

## The 1961 crossroads and the American boom

The turning point has a date. In 1961 the Santa Margherita winery, founded by Count Gaetano Marzotto, began vinifying Pinot Grigio as a white wine, separating the juice from the skins straight away. The result was pale, dry, light and fresh: the opposite of Ramato. When this style reached the United States it found an audience ready for an easy-to-grasp white, with no oak and no sharp edges, and an Italian name that sounded elegant. Other producers followed, and within a couple of decades “Pinot Grigio” had become almost a synonym for Italian white wine in America.

Success came at a price. Enormous demand pushed many growers to plant everywhere and crop heavily, with high yields and ever more neutral wines. Pinot Grigio became the wine snob’s favourite target: water with a label, people said. It was an unfair verdict on the grape, though not always on the bottles.

## The Delle Venezie DOC: bringing order

For readers outside Italy, a quick primer. In Italy a DOC (*Denominazione di Origine Controllata*) is a defined zone with written rules on grapes, yields and methods; an IGT is a broader, more permissive category. For decades much of the north-east’s Pinot Grigio was released as IGT delle Venezie, with plenty of room for manoeuvre.

In 2017 that changed. The Delle Venezie DOC was created, the first major Italian interregional appellation devoted to a single grape: it covers Veneto, Friuli Venezia Giulia and the province of Trento, and requires at least 85% Pinot Grigio. The old IGT was renamed Trevenezie. Maximum yields came down, and a consortium oversees compliance and traceability. In 2021 a rosé version was introduced as well, in effect giving the Ramato style an official home.

It was a smart move: protecting a name that had gone global before it became entirely anonymous. It should be said, though, that the Delle Venezie DOC is above all a guarantee of origin and standards, not of excellence. Anyone after a Pinot Grigio with character would do well to look at the smaller appellations too: Collio and Colli Orientali in Friuli, and Alto Adige, where the grape, on the right soils and at the right altitudes, gives wines of quite another depth.

## Same grape, different wine: Alsace Pinot Gris

To see how much Pinot Grigio depends on style rather than on the grape, you only need to cross the Alps. In Alsace the same variety is called Pinot Gris and is considered one of the region’s four noble grapes. There it is picked later, at full ripeness: the wines are more golden and richer, with aromas of ripe pear, honey and spice, sometimes noticeable residual sugar, and in late-harvest versions they become true sweet wines. It was once even called “Tokay d’Alsace”, a name later dropped to avoid confusion with Hungarian Tokaji.

Pinot Grigio and Pinot Gris are the same grape with two philosophies. The first, in its most widespread form, aims for freshness and lightness; the second for body and complexity. In between sit Friuli’s Ramato wines and the more ambitious Pinot Grigio from Alto Adige, proof that the distance between the two worlds is shorter than the labels suggest. Beyond Europe, for that matter, producers use one name or the other precisely to signal which style they have chosen.

## A wine worth a second look

My view is that Pinot Grigio has paid for its success with its reputation, and that the time has come to give some of it back. The grape is not neutral: it was made neutral by high yields and by a market that asked only for freshness. Taste it as a Ramato, or from a good Friulian or Alto Adige hillside, and you find a wine with colour, aroma and structure. Italy’s next step is not to sell more Pinot Grigio, but to convince the world that it is worth asking where it comes from.
