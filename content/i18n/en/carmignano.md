Less than twenty kilometres from Florence, on the slopes of Montalbano, lies a denomination that beat one of the most retold revolutions in Italian wine by a couple of decades, and that almost nobody mentions when the story is told. While in the Maremma and Chianti Cabernet was becoming the emblem of rebellion against the rulebook, in Carmignano it was already written into law. It is a small story, quite literally, and that is precisely why it is worth telling.

## Three centuries of boundaries

On 24 September 1716 Grand Duke Cosimo III de' Medici signed the edict that drew the boundaries of four Tuscan wine zones: Chianti, Pomino, Valdarno di Sopra and, indeed, Carmignano. We have already covered it in our piece on Chianti, and it is one of the documents most often cited by anyone tracing the origins of the very idea of an appellation. For Carmignano it meant early protection: the name could not be used for wines made elsewhere.

Its reputation, however, predates the edict. The historical section of today's production rules recalls that a fourteenth-century chronicler was already praising the wines “of Carmignano and Artimino” as excellent. And the Medici's mark on the land is still visible: at Artimino stands the family's grand sixteenth-century villa, one of the places that lend this corner of the province an almost courtly air.

The twentieth century was less kind. In the 1930s, when a commission began to bring order to Italy's wine zones, Carmignano was folded into Chianti Montalbano. For a few decades the wine of one of the Medici zones sat under someone else's umbrella.

## The Cabernet that arrived early

This is where it gets interesting. In Carmignano, Sangiovese was being blended with Cabernet as early as the nineteenth century, long before anyone coined the term Super Tuscan. Some accounts trace Cabernet in the area all the way back to the Medici era, but the documents are thin on this point, and it is best treated as tradition rather than established fact.

The modern chapter is on firmer ground. In the 1960s the Capezzana estate, run by the Contini Bonacossi family, reintroduced Cabernet to its vineyards; several sources report that the cuttings came from Château Lafite. When Carmignano won its DOC in 1975, finally breaking away from Chianti, its rules provided for Cabernet in the blend: according to the most widely cited accounts, it was the first Italian denomination to do so.

The contrast with its neighbours says it all. In those same years Tuscany's most famous Cabernet-based reds, the ones we explored in our piece on Bolgheri and the Super Tuscans, were released as humble table wines precisely because the rules made no room for them. In Carmignano, by contrast, the Bordeaux grape was already regarded as part of local tradition. DOCG status followed in 1990, applying from the 1988 vintage.

## What the rules say

Today's regulations explain the style well. Carmignano DOCG must contain at least 50% Sangiovese, up to 20% Canaiolo Nero and between 10 and 20% Cabernet Franc and/or Cabernet Sauvignon. Small amounts of white grapes are also allowed (Trebbiano Toscano, Canaiolo Bianco and Malvasia del Chianti, together no more than 10%), as are other authorised red varieties.

The zone covers just two communes in the province of Prato, Carmignano and Poggio a Caiano, with hillside vineyards that may not exceed 400 metres. Maximum yield is 8 tonnes per hectare. The wine cannot be released before 1 June of the second year after the harvest and must spend at least 8 months in oak or chestnut casks; the Riserva requires at least 12 months in wood and may not be released before 29 September of the third year.

Alongside the DOCG there are other labels: Barco Reale di Carmignano, a DOC for younger, more approachable reds, and the Carmignano DOC, now used for rosato and Vin Santo, including Occhio di Pernice.

## Small indeed, but how small?

You will often read that Carmignano is Tuscany's smallest DOCG. That claim should be taken with a pinch of salt: sources agree on a vineyard area of just over a hundred hectares and output in the order of a few hundred thousand bottles a year, but Tuscany has other very small DOCGs, and we found no up-to-date, figures-in-hand comparison. Better to say it is one of the smallest, which is remarkable enough for a zone with three hundred years of legal history.

Size has concrete consequences. Producers are few, and two large historic estates control the bulk of the vineyards. Which means the image of the whole denomination rests heavily on a handful of cellars, for better and for worse.

## Climate and style

The land has its own logic. The hills are low and open towards the plain, but the nearby mountains cool the nights. It is a setting in which grapes ripen early, and Cabernet, which elsewhere in Tuscany struggles or overdoes it, here finds a balance with Sangiovese. The typical result is a red rounder than a Chianti Classico, with Tuscan fruit and a little extra backbone, but without the international stamp of many Bordeaux-style blends from the coast.

## A lesson in modesty

My view is that Carmignano is one of Italy's most underrated denominations, and that the reasons have almost nothing to do with the wine. It is too small to make noise, too close to Florence to seem exotic, too serious to chase fashions. And yet it settled early, calmly and by law, the question half of Tuscany spent twenty years fighting over: whether Cabernet could stand beside Sangiovese without betraying it. Carmignano's answer was yes, as long as it stayed in the minority. It was not a bad answer then, and it is not a bad one now.
