Some grapes need a press office; others had Mozart. Marzemino belongs to the second group, and in Trentino they know it perfectly well: for more than two centuries that toast on stage has been the best calling card of a red that, otherwise, hardly anyone outside the Vallagarina could pronounce. Nosiola had no librettist. It had something rarer: a handful of stubborn families who went on hanging bunches in their attics long after it had stopped making sense to anyone. Two Trentino grapes, two opposite stories, and a province we usually talk about only for its sparkling wine.

## A toast before hell

Prague, 29 October 1787. At the theatre now known as the Estates Theatre, *Don Giovanni* opens, with music by Mozart and a libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte. In the second-act finale the protagonist dines alone, waited on by Leporello, while a little band plays fashionable opera tunes. At one point he orders: “Versa il vino! Eccellente marzimino!” (“Pour the wine! Excellent Marzemino!”). Shortly afterwards the stone Commendatore will arrive, and dinner will end in hell.

It is one of the most famous wine references in the history of music, and one of the most mysterious. Why Marzemino? The libretto does not say, and as far as we know there is no direct testimony from Da Ponte about the choice. One can recall that the librettist was from the Veneto, born in Ceneda, and that in the eighteenth century Marzemino was a wine known across the north-east under Venetian influence; the rest is conjecture, and should be treated as such. One thing is certain: the line has done more for Marzemino than any set of production rules.

## The Vallagarina, where Marzemino is at home

Today Marzemino is scattered across northern Italy, often blended or made in sweet and fizzy styles in Lombardy and the Veneto, but its heart is the Vallagarina, the Adige valley around Rovereto, south of Trento. Isera is considered the capital of its production, and the Trentino DOC rules, created in 1971, recognise two dedicated subzones: d'Isera and dei Ziresi, the latter in the commune of Volano. Both require Marzemino gentile with no more than 15% of other black grapes, and for the Superiore lower yields and at least ten months’ ageing. Local guides make much of the soils: basalt in one part of the valley, more clay at Ziresi, with softer, more structured wines in the latter case. It is a distinction worth checking in the glass rather than on paper.

Genetic research carried out at San Michele all'Adige has cleared up part of its family tree: Marzemino has a direct parent-offspring link with Teroldego, whose surprising family we have already written about, as well as with Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso and with a white Veneto grape, Marzemina bianca. Other accounts, which bring it from far away along Venetian trade routes, are evocative but less solid.

In the vineyard it is not an easy grape. It ripens late, suffers from powdery mildew and, if allowed to overcrop, gives thin wines. Well looked after, it has an intense ruby colour, a scent almost everyone describes as violets, cherry and plum fruit, soft tannin and moderate alcohol. It is not a wine for contemplation and does not pretend to be: it is a table wine in the best sense, which does exactly what it should with the valley’s polenta and cured meats.

## Nosiola, the white nobody knows

If Marzemino is little known, Nosiola is almost clandestine. It is a native Trentino white grape which, according to the Slow Food Foundation, accounts for about 1.5% of the province’s production, on a little over a hundred hectares. The name is often linked to *nocciola*, hazelnut, for the colour of the ripe berries or a hint on the finish of the wine, but the etymology is not documented with any certainty.

Made dry, it gives a light white of good freshness, with a faintly bitter streak that makes it more interesting than its shyness suggests. The Trentino rules allow it as a single-variety wine. But Nosiola owes its fame to another transformation, the one that happens in the attic.

## Vino Santo, or patience in a bottle

Trentino’s Vino Santo is born in the Valle dei Laghi, the valley that runs down from Trento towards Lake Garda, and its historic heart is Santa Massenza, a hamlet of Vezzano. In late October the loosest bunches, those with well-spaced berries, are chosen and laid out on *arèle*, racks once made of reed and now often of mesh, in airy lofts. The work is done by the Ora del Garda, the wind that blows up from the lake every afternoon. Over the following months noble rot, Botrytis cinerea, attacks the berries and, together with the air, dries them out: by the end the grapes can lose 80% of their weight or more, and a hundred kilos of Nosiola yield a mere 15–18 litres of must.

Tradition has the pressing take place during Holy Week, hence the name. The rules are more prosaic and set only minimum dates: for Vino Santo, pressing no earlier than 1 February; for the Superiore, a drying period that cannot end before 1 March, with at least 18% potential alcohol once the grapes are dried. The grape must be at least 85% Nosiola. The dense must then goes into small oak barrels and ferments slowly, for years. Local sources speak of six to eight years of fermentation and ageing; the rules require at least 48 months for the Superiore, made in only five communes (Calavino, Cavedine, Lasino, Padergnone and Vezzano). There is, it should be said, a more famous namesake, Tuscan Vin Santo, with which the Trentino wine shares a name and a capacity for patience but little else: we covered it when writing about *caratelli* and the *madre*.

The history of Trentino’s Vino Santo is also a story of near-extinction. It was prized in Habsburg times and sold on Austrian and German markets; with the end of the Empire after the First World War that market collapsed, and production survived thanks to a few growers. Today it is a Slow Food Presidium, which lists six member producers. Collectors’ numbers, not supermarket ones.

## Two grapes, one idea of Trentino

My view is that Trentino was quite right to bet on traditional-method sparkling wine, but that it owes a debt to these two grapes. Marzemino is an honest red with a story any wine region in the world would envy, and it deserves to be drunk more often and told better, without being turned into something it is not. Vino Santo is one of Italy’s great sweet wines, and the fact that only a minority of enthusiasts know it says more about our distraction than about its quality. Don Giovanni, before sinking into the flames, at least had the good taste to ask for a Marzemino. We, with no Commendatore at the door, might allow ourselves a glass of Vino Santo too.
