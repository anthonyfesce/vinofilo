There is a picture that every wine shop hangs on its wall sooner or later: the portly friar raising his glass in front of a barrel. It is an endearing caricature and almost entirely wrong. Medieval monks were not jolly provincial tipplers but meticulous, at times obsessive, managers of land, harvests and archives. And it is that obsession, more than any thirst, that made them central to the history of European wine.

## A quarter of wine and a sigh

It all begins with a rule written in the 6th century by Benedict of Nursia, the text that would order the life of Western monasteries for centuries. Chapter 40 is devoted to the measure of wine, and it is a small masterpiece of realism. Benedict admits that he sets the ration “with some hesitation”, and allows each monk one *hemina* a day (Italian translations often render it as “a quarter”; the exact volume of that old Roman measure is disputed, and it is best not to pretend to precision). Those who can abstain altogether, he adds, will receive a special reward.

Then comes the finest line. We read, Benedict writes, that wine is not for monks at all; but since in our day they cannot be persuaded of this, let us at least agree not to drink to the point of satiety. The superior may allow more for hard labour or the heat of summer, and those who live where no wine can be had should bless God and not complain. It is not a eulogy of wine but a truce with human nature. Yet it is enough to make wine a normal part of the monastic day, and therefore something to be produced.

Another commonplace deserves debunking: the famous motto “ora et labora”, which everyone attributes to the Rule, does not appear in it word for word. Manual work is certainly there, but the formula is a later summary.

## Wine for the altar

The second reason is liturgical. The Mass requires wine from the vine, every day, in every church. In a Europe where trade had become difficult and roads unsafe, a monastery or a bishop’s seat that wished to celebrate regularly had every reason to tend its own vines. Add to that the hospitality towards pilgrims and travellers that the Rule demands, and the need for something to trade or sell.

Here, though, the popular story needs correcting. To say that the monks “saved” viticulture after the fall of Rome is a simplification. Vineyards survived in the hands of bishops, nobles and peasants too, and monastic land itself often came from lay donations. The monasteries’ distinctive contribution was something else: continuity. An abbey does not die, does not split its inheritance among heirs, does not sell up after a bad vintage. It can watch the same vineyard for centuries and write down what it sees.

## Cîteaux and the plot that became a legend

At this the Cistercians excelled. Born as a Benedictine reform seeking a return to the austerity of the origins, they set out from the abbey of Cîteaux, in Burgundy, and spread rapidly across Europe. Their oenological masterpiece is Clos de Vougeot, in the Côte de Nuits. The first gifts of land to the monks date from between 1109 and 1115; purchases continued over the next two centuries until, by 1336, the vineyard was complete and enclosed by a wall. That is where the word “clos” comes from: an enclosed vineyard.

The château that stands at its centre today was added in 1551, rebuilding and enlarging older structures. With the French Revolution, Church property was confiscated and sold to private buyers, and over the centuries the Clos has fragmented: in the early 2000s it had more than eighty owners across roughly 50 hectares, the largest grand cru in the Côte de Nuits. Since 1945 the château has been the headquarters of the Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin.

Clos de Vougeot is also linked to the tradition that the Cistercians observed and distinguished the best parts of the vineyard, laying the foundations of the idea of the “cru” that Burgundy would later codify. We touched on this in our piece on Burgundy’s crus: it is a plausible and widely cited account, but the legends of monks “tasting the soil” should be taken for what they are, anecdotes.

## And in Italy?

Those who live in Italy have an advantage: the monastic history of wine is not only in Burgundy, it is a few hours’ drive away. In some cases it can still be touched, because there are abbeys where the religious make wine today.

In Alto Adige, the Abbazia di Novacella, at Varna in the Valle Isarco, is a community of Augustinian canons (so neither Benedictine nor Cistercian) with a winery producing hundreds of thousands of bottles, chiefly of Sylvaner and the valley’s other whites. In Bolzano, the Benedictines of Muri-Gries are associated with Lagrein and Moscato Rosa. Near Padua, in the Colli Euganei, the Benedictine abbey of Praglia farms around ten hectares and also makes a traditional-method sparkling wine. In Tuscany, above Arezzo, the monastery of Camaldoli has a small production of its own, including a wine aged at length in the manner of Vin Santo.

These are not museums: they are real wineries, with their own markets and modern technical choices. But walking through a cloister and then out among the rows helps you understand, better than any book, why vineyard and prayer kept the same timetable for centuries.

## A lesson that has nothing to do with faith

My view is that wine’s debt to the monasteries is real, but has been told badly. We do not owe the great wines to monks because they were saints, nor because they liked a drink: we owe them to the fact that they had time, archives and no hurry to sell. In an age when companies think in quarters, that is the most modern part of the whole story. Terroir, before it was a marketing word, was the result of a few hundred years of attention to the same piece of land.
