Some grapes ask to be understood; others ask to be tamed. Tannat belongs, beyond argument, to the second family. The name itself sounds like a warning, and for centuries the growers of south-west France treated it the way one treats a temperamental horse: with respect, patience and a touch of fear. Then, in the 19th century, a Basque emigrant carried it across the Atlantic, and there Tannat found a second homeland that now regards it as a national symbol. Few grape varieties have had so clean a second life.

## At the foot of the Pyrenees

Tannat’s historic home is south-west France, in the band running through Gascony, Béarn and the Basque Country, in the shadow of the Pyrenees. Its benchmark appellation is Madiran, an AOC since 1948, with some 1,300 hectares of vines given over solely to red wine. The rules require Tannat to make up at least 60% of the vineyard; the rest may be Cabernet Franc, known here as Bouchy, Cabernet Sauvignon and Fer, locally Pinenc. Tannat also appears in Irouléguy, the small appellation of the French Basque Country, and as a supporting player in Cahors, which we covered in our piece on Malbec.

For Italian readers the obvious comparison is Sagrantino di Montefalco, the Umbrian red with which Tannat has long vied for the reputation of the world’s most tannic grape. Like Sagrantino, it is a thick-skinned variety, extremely rich in polyphenols, that gives dark, almost impenetrable wines and, when young, can be hard to the point of surliness. Traditional Madiran was a wine to forget in the cellar for years before opening.

## An invention to tame it

It was in Madiran, precisely, that one of the most debated cellar techniques of recent decades was born. Patrick Ducournau, a grower at Château d'Aydie, developed micro-oxygenation: the controlled introduction of minute quantities of oxygen into wine during ageing, to soften tannins and stabilise colour without resorting to long years in barrel. The idea grew out of a practical problem, namely how to make drinkable within a reasonable time a wine that by nature demands patience.

The technique then travelled the world, far beyond Tannat, and drew criticism just as fast: some accuse it of standardising red wines, others defend it as a neutral tool whose results depend on how it is used. The debate is still open, but it remains a curious thing that so obstinate a grape forced its growers to invent something now used everywhere.

## Monsieur Harriague and Uruguay

Tannat’s second life began around 1870, when Pascual Harriague, a French Basque emigrant, introduced it to the department of Salto, in north-west Uruguay. For a time the grape even took its importer’s name, and in Uruguay “Harriague” is still a synonym for Tannat. Today the country grows more than 1,570 hectares of it, 26.5% of the national vineyard in the 2024/2025 vintage according to the government agency Uruguay XXI, making it the world’s leading producer of Tannat. Every 14 April brings Tannat Day, held in Harriague’s honour.

Uruguay is a small wine country squeezed between two giants, Argentina and Brazil, and its climate is very different from that of Mendoza at the foot of the Andes: more humid, breezier, with a strong Atlantic influence. In these conditions Tannat tends to give wines less ferocious than the French, with darker, blackberry fruit and rounder tannins, although sources note that the newer clones produce more powerful, more alcoholic reds than the old vines. The grape lends itself to different interpretations, from long-ageing reds to younger styles and blends with other varieties.

Exports of bottled wine hit record levels in 2024, up 40% in volume, and reach 57 markets, led by Brazil, followed by the United States, the United Kingdom and Mexico. Small numbers in absolute terms, but they tell of a country that chose to make its name with a single grape, rather as Argentina did with Malbec or New Zealand with Sauvignon Blanc.

## Procyanidins, with no promises

There is another chapter that made Tannat famous beyond enthusiast circles. In 2006 a London research team led by Roger Corder published a study in Nature on procyanidins, a family of condensed tannins found in grape skins and seeds. Among the wines analysed, the highest levels came from south-west France and from the province of Nuoro, in Sardinia, and the researchers linked them to Tannat and to traditional winemaking methods.

For wine lovers the point is not a medical one: on that we offer no opinion, and those who do so too breezily usually have something to sell. The point is chemical and oenological. Procyanidins are part of what makes Tannat so structured, so dark and so capable of ageing, and they explain why handling it in the cellar is a balancing act: extract enough for character, but not so much that the result is a wine to chew rather than drink.

## A grape that makes no apologies

Tannat will never be everyone’s red. It has neither Merlot’s instant charm nor Pinot Noir’s elegant transparency, and anyone who meets it young without warning may not try again. Yet its story says something important: a grape deemed difficult can become the identity of an entire country, provided someone is willing to understand it rather than correct it.

My view is that Uruguay has done with Tannat what Madiran never quite managed: given it a recognisable face without erasing its character. And if Europe wants to rediscover this grape, it would do well to look towards Montevideo with the same curiosity with which the Basques once looked out to sea.
