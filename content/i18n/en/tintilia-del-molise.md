Molise doesn’t exist, goes the most repeated joke on the Italian internet. It is a region you cross without noticing, squeezed between the fame of Abruzzo and that of Puglia, and until 1963 it did not even have its own administrative standing: it was one and the same with Abruzzo. In wine the story has been similar. For decades Molise produced grapes and wines that ended up elsewhere, often nameless. Then came Tintilia, a grape nobody is quite sure of the origins of, and the invisible region found something to put on a label under its own name.

## One name, two theories

Start with the name, because that is where the debate begins. The most common explanation links it to the Italian *tinta*, dye, for the wine’s deep colour. Another line of thought looks to Spain and *tinto*, red, and ties in with an idea that has been around for a long time: that the vine reached southern Italy in the Bourbon era, brought over from the Iberian peninsula. It is an appealing tale, but it remains a hypothesis without decisive proof.

Its family tree has been argued over too. For years Tintilia was likened to Sardinia’s Bovale, itself often linked, in the telling, to Spain. The most widely cited genetic analyses suggest instead that Tintilia has a distinct profile and does not match other known Italian varieties, Bovale included. The matter is not entirely closed, though: the sources do not always agree, and the question of a Spanish origin remains open. What is certain is more prosaic. In 2002 Tintilia was entered in Italy’s National Register of Vine Varieties, the step that gave it an official existence.

## A grape that almost vanished

Like many varieties of central and southern Italy, Tintilia came close to disappearing. In the second half of the twentieth century the Molise countryside emptied, and those who stayed planted more productive grapes that were easier to sell. A study from the University of Molise published in 2014 describes it in exactly those terms: a native vine that was at risk of disappearing and has been rediscovered.

The rediscovery has a few names attached. According to the available accounts, some growers had begun replanting it as early as the 1970s; at the end of the 1990s the first modern vineyards devoted to it were planted, and one of the long-established estates on the coast, at Campomarino, was among the first to bottle a single-varietal Tintilia commercially. It is the classic script of the forgotten grapes that come back, of which we have told other examples: a few stubborn souls, a few bottles, then the attention of the guides.

## The 2011 DOC

Formal recognition arrived in 2011, with the Tintilia del Molise DOC. It is the region’s fourth and youngest denomination, after Biferno, Pentro di Isernia and Molise, and it stands apart from the others through one clear choice: it is a varietal denomination. The rules require at least 95% Tintilia, with a small margin for other non-aromatic local red grapes.

There are three styles: Rosso, Rosato and Rosso Riserva. The Riserva must age for at least two years and reach a minimum of 13% alcohol, while 11.5% suffices for Rosso and Rosato. The most telling detail, however, lies elsewhere: the vineyards must sit at least 200 metres above sea level, and some climb beyond 600. Tintilia is a grape of hills and low mountains, and the rules put that in writing. The zone covers the provinces of Campobasso and Isernia, and so almost the whole of Molise, but the numbers are tiny. By one recent estimate, about 65 hectares are registered to the DOC, and those planted to Tintilia across all of Italy come to little more than a hundred. These are the figures of a private collection rather than a denomination, yet on that handful of hectares rests the name of an entire region. The protection consortium was founded in 2017, which says something about how young this story is.

## In the glass

The name does not lie: Tintilia gives a deep ruby red, with purple glints when young. The rules speak of a vinous, distinctive aroma and a dry, soft palate; in the Riservas the colour turns garnet and spicy notes appear. Altitude does the rest, or at least one may reasonably expect it to: high vineyards mean cool nights, and cool nights mean wines less heavy than the south might lead you to think. If that promise is kept, it may well be its strongest card.

There is also the Rosato, less well known, which the rules require to be dry or nearly so, and which shows off the grape’s fresh nature nicely. As for food, Molise tradition offers plenty to work with: mutton and lamb, salumi, mountain cheeses, the peasant cooking of the Apennines.

## Why it matters

My view is that Tintilia is worth more than the sum of its bottles. In a region that struggles to be recognised, a grape with a name of its own, an uncertain history and a precise territory is a precious tool of identity, and Molise was right to build a dedicated denomination on it rather than dilute it in a generic label. The risk is the one facing every rare grape that becomes fashionable: chasing concentration and oak in order to look important. Tintilia is interesting precisely because it is a hillside red, and it should stay that way. If it does, the joke about the Molise that doesn’t exist will at last have an answer you can uncork.
