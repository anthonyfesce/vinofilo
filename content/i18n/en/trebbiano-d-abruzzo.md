If there is one name that lowers expectations in Italy, it is Trebbiano. Say it and the image springs to mind of demijohn white, the stuff of village festivals, poured cold and forgotten fast. Then someone opens a bottle from Abruzzo with fifteen or twenty years behind it and the prejudice begins to creak. Trebbiano d'Abruzzo lives precisely in that contradiction: it is the region’s most common white and, in the right hands, one of the longest-lived in Italy.

## A surname that is far too common

The first problem is one of identity. In Italy “Trebbiano” is not a grape but an extended family of varieties that often have nothing to do with one another. Trebbiano Toscano is the same grape the French call Ugni Blanc, the basis of Cognac: productive, generous, usually neutral, planted across half of Italy precisely for its yields. Trebbiano Romagnolo is another, and one of the most widely planted whites in the country. Trebbiano Spoletino is an Umbrian grape, revived in the early 2000s around Montefalco and Spoleto. Trebbiano di Soave, as Vinofilo has explained when writing about Verdicchio and Lugana, is genetically Verdicchio and has no ties to the Trebbianos at all.

Trebbiano Abruzzese is something else again. The trouble is that in the vineyard it was long confused with Bombino Bianco, with Trebbiano Toscano and even with Passerina, so much so that its true extent in the region is not precisely known. Some sources suggest it is the same as Bombino Bianco, but DNA analysis has not confirmed this. In short: even those who grow Trebbiano in Abruzzo do not always know for certain which Trebbiano is in their rows, especially in old plantings.

## What the rules say

The Trebbiano d'Abruzzo DOC was created in 1972 and covers almost the whole region, from the hills looking out over the Adriatic to the foothills of the Apennines, with vineyards on hillsides or upland plateaus up to 500 metres, or 600 for well-exposed sites. The decisive point is the blend: at least 85% must come from Trebbiano Abruzzese, Trebbiano Toscano and/or Bombino Bianco. The rest, up to 15%, may be other non-aromatic local white grapes such as Cococciola, Passerina or Malvasia.

That “and/or” is the key to everything. It means the same label can cover a pure Trebbiano Abruzzese and a wine based on Trebbiano Toscano, two grapes of very different character and potential. The rules do not require producers to tell them apart, so few have had any incentive to do so. It is the main reason why, on the shelf, the name says so little.

There are then three tiers. Basic Trebbiano d'Abruzzo can be sold from 1 January after the harvest, with at least 11.5% alcohol. Superiore requires 12% and can be released from 1 March. Riserva rises to 12.5% and roughly eighteen months in the cellar, with oak optional. Four provincial subzones have recently been introduced too, Chieti, Pescara, Teramo and L'Aquila, available only for Superiore and Riserva. The province of Chieti produces most of the appellation’s wine.

## Two names that changed the story

If Trebbiano d'Abruzzo is taken seriously today beyond the region, the credit goes to a handful of producers who treated it as a great white when nobody else did. The first is Edoardo Valentini, at Loreto Aprutino in the province of Pescara: his family had farmed the land since the mid-seventeenth century, and he released wine from the 1956 vintage until his death in 2006. It is documented that he bottled only a small share of production, around 5%, and sold the rest to the local cooperative: a ferocious selection, built on spontaneous fermentations, cement vats, old Slavonian oak casks, and no fining or filtration.

The second is Emidio Pepe, who died in July 2026 at the age of 94. He founded his estate at Torano Nuovo, in the province of Teramo, in 1964, starting from about one and a half hectares of vines his father had used for bulk wine. The white grapes were trodden by foot, fermentations were spontaneous in cement tanks, the wines unfiltered. But his most radical trait was time: Pepe held back a significant share of each vintage and released it years later, convinced that not only Montepulciano but Trebbiano too could age for a long time in bottle. In a region used to selling wine in bulk, the idea seemed almost a whim. Today the estate is run by his daughters and granddaughters.

Alongside them, in recent decades, other producers have helped to understand the grape better, starting with the work of distinguishing Trebbiano Abruzzese from the rest. But the legacy of Valentini and Pepe remains the yardstick by which the appellation is measured, for better and for worse: two examples so lofty that they risk overshadowing everything else.

## Why it ages

Trebbiano Abruzzese is not an easy grape. Those who have studied it closely point out that harvest timing is critical, because acidity falls quickly if the grapes overripen, and that the must oxidises easily, a weakness modern winemaking has helped to correct. When the vineyard is farmed for modest yields, though, the grape gives whites of structure and texture, not very aromatic in youth, that build complexity over the years. Small wonder it is often cited among the Italian dry whites best suited to ageing.

Young, it must be said, it can seem reticent: little perfume, an impression of substance rather than fruit. It is a wine easily misjudged six months after the harvest, which is exactly when the market expects whites to be at their best. That is also why the wider public has known it almost only in its simplest form.

## The problem is not the grape

My view is that Trebbiano d'Abruzzo is paying above all for a badly written rule. As long as the same label can describe wines from different grapes, the name will keep dragging behind it the image of an anonymous white, and anyone working with Trebbiano Abruzzese will have to explain from scratch, every time, what is in the glass. The new subzones are a step in the right direction, because they tie the wine to a place. The next step would be to state clearly which grape it comes from. A white able to cross the decades deserves at least that much: a name that does not confuse it with its humbler relatives.
