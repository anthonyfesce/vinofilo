Some wines can be explained in a single line; others seem made on purpose to fit no box at all. Freisa belongs to the second family. It can be still or fizzy, dry or off-dry, almost rustic or serious enough for the cellar, and in the same area all these versions often live side by side. For anyone who craves order it is a nightmare. For anyone who wants to drink a piece of Piedmont without going through Barolo, it is one of the most enjoyable discoveries within easy reach.

## Family, but a different temperament

The first thing to know is also the most surprising. DNA analysis, in studies published from 2004 onwards, has revealed a direct parent-offspring relationship between Freisa and Nebbiolo. Which of the two is the parent is unclear: the scholar Ian D'Agata, for one, thinks Freisa the more likely. Either way, apart from Nebbiolo Rosé, no variety is closer to the king of the Langhe.

And you can taste it. Like Nebbiolo, Freisa has high acidity and substantial tannin, a colour that is not especially deep but with more marked purple glints, and aromas of raspberry, strawberry and violet. The name itself, according to the most widely held view, points to the strawberry. Where it parts company with its illustrious relative is in temperament: a wilder streak, a bitter edge on the finish, crunchier fruit. If Nebbiolo is the professor, Freisa is the bright student sitting in the back row.

## A history written in customs duties

The first written trace is, curiously, a fiscal one. In the early 16th century, at Pancalieri, near Turin, the customs tariffs list a wine called *Fresearum*, taxed as a product of quality. Read closely, and it is clear this was no humble wine: it was a bottle worth taxing properly.

For centuries Freisa was everywhere. Until the 19th century it may have covered, according to some accounts, up to half the vineyards of the provinces of Asti and Alessandria, and by the end of that century it was among Piedmont’s main grapes, partly because it stood up well to downy mildew. Then the 20th century cut it down to size. Today there are just over a thousand hectares: still a real presence, but far from its former weight. Ampelographers believe it was born in the hills between Asti and Turin, and it is no accident that this is where it holds out best.

There are also several forms of the vine. Freisa piccola, small-berried, is the most widespread and gives the best wines; Freisa grossa, also known as Freisa di Nizza, has larger berries, is often planted on fertile flatland and makes less interesting wine. At Chieri they grow a type that some consider a sub-variety in its own right, highly perfumed and tannic.

## Still or fizzy, and there is a reason

Traditional Freisa was red, lightly sparkling and a touch sweet. This was no whim: a little residual sugar and the bubbles served to soften tannin and bitterness that, with the techniques of the past, could be harsh. Anyone who grew up in Piedmont remembers it that way, next to the salumi and the country picnics.

With temperature-controlled fermentation and the use of oak came dry, still versions, more structured and built to last. And there is a curiosity worth knowing: Freisa *nebbiolata*, fermented on the skins left over from Nebbiolo, a technique similar to the *ripasso* of Valpolicella. There is that kinship again, this time in the cellar.

The upshot is that today the same grape covers almost the whole spectrum: from off-dry frizzante for a picnic to a dry red that can take a few years in bottle. It is not confusion, it is heritage. The trouble is that the label does not always make clear what you are buying, so it pays to read it carefully.

## Chieri, Asti, Langhe

There are two dedicated denominations, both born in the early 1970s. Freisa d'Asti DOC dates from 1972, Freisa di Chieri DOC from 1973. The latter, on the doorstep of Turin, provides for a range of styles that on its own sums up the nature of the grape: secco, amabile, frizzante, spumante and superiore. For the Superiore wines, in both Chieri and Asti, at least a year of ageing is required.

Chieri also has a small urban gem: the Freisa di Chieri made in the vineyard of the Villa della Regina, on the hill of Turin, whose first bottles came out in 2009. A historic vineyard inside a major city is a rarity in itself, and the fact that it is planted with Freisa says a great deal about the bond between this grape and the Turin countryside.

Further south, Freisa appears within broader denominations: Langhe Freisa, Monferrato Freisa, Pinerolese Freisa. In the Langhe, where everything revolves around Nebbiolo, some producers treat it as a structured dry red, and the comparison with its illustrious relative becomes direct, sometimes awkwardly so for anyone who believes red Piedmont begins and ends with Barolo and Barbaresco. Readers of our pieces on Barbera and Dolcetto will find here the third point of a triangle of everyday reds, and perhaps the least predictable.

## The appeal of an awkward wine

Freisa will never please everyone, and that is precisely its merit. It has always divided critics, between those who find it irresistible and those who cannot bear that nervy tannin and the bitterness on the finish. In my view it is a wine that asks to be taken for what it is, without looking for a miniature Nebbiolo in it. The fizzy version deserves to shed its embarrassment and return to the table with pride; the still one deserves to be given a few years. Piedmont built its fame on solemn reds; Freisa is a reminder that part of its soul has always been irregular, sparkling and a little awkward. And it is a pity that so few people, outside the hills between Asti and Turin, ever notice.
