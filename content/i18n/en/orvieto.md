Some towns have a cellar. Orvieto is a town that sits on top of one. The tuff cliff it stands on has been hollowed out by centuries of caves, wells and tunnels, and some of those cavities were used to make and store wine. That image alone tells you that white wine here is not one farm product among many, but part of the stone itself. And yet, outside Umbria, Orvieto is often the wine people order without much thought. That is a pity, and a bit of an injustice too.

## A town built over caves

Tuff is a soft volcanic rock, easy to dig and able to hold cool, steady temperatures. The Etruscans, who had one of their most important centres here, were the first to exploit it: according to the most widely accepted accounts they brought viticulture to the area and carved cellar-like chambers into the rock, where must could ferment slowly in the cool.

Those caves also explain the wine's historic style. A long, cold fermentation, at a time when nobody knew how to control one, lent itself well, according to the sources, to the sweet wines the ancient world prized. From the Middle Ages until the mid-twentieth century, after all, the true face of Orvieto was that of a golden, sweet white.

## The wine the popes loved

The nickname “the popes' white” is no label-writer's invention. In the thirteenth century Orvieto repeatedly served as residence and refuge for the papal court, and some sources count five popes who stayed there during that century, Urban IV among them. It was in this period that the town's wine gained prestige, poured at the tables of those who mattered.

The bond with power and art carried on afterwards. It is often said that Luca Signorelli, when he undertook in 1499 to fresco the chapel of San Brizio in the cathedral, had his contract guarantee him all the Orvieto wine he wanted. The anecdote is repeated by many sources, though the details vary, and should be taken for what it is: a good story, plausible, that shows how highly the wine was regarded. Later Gabriele d'Annunzio would call it “the sun of Italy in a bottle”, a line local producers are still happy to quote today.

## Procanico, Grechetto and today's rules

Orvieto has been a DOC since 1971. Around 95% of the zone lies in Umbria, in the province of Terni, with a small part in Lazio, in the province of Viterbo; the Classico is the historic heartland, entirely Umbrian. Vineyards cover about 2,100 hectares, average production is close to 78,000 hectolitres and roughly three bottles in four go abroad, a figure that says a great deal about the name's international recognition.

The rules call for at least 60% Grechetto and Trebbiano Toscano, alone or together, with the rest left to other authorised white grapes; among those permitted are Malvasia, Drupeggio and Verdello. Here Trebbiano goes by the name Procanico, and is considered a local biotype with smaller berries: it was the main grape of old Orvieto. Grechetto, on the other hand, is what gives the wine its backbone, with more structure and sharper aromas.

Then there is the Superiore, with at least 12% alcohol and a minimum ageing period that keeps it off the market until 1 March after the harvest. On paper it is the more ambitious version; in practice quality depends far more on vineyard yields and the producer's choices than on the word on the label.

## The sweet tradition and noble rot

Alongside the dry style, now the norm, Orvieto still allows *abboccato*, *amabile* and *dolce* versions, from off-dry to fully sweet. But they have become a niche: according to available estimates, off-dry and sweet wines together account for less than 5% of production. A complete reversal of the past, which took place in the second half of the twentieth century, when the market demanded light, dry whites and Orvieto obliged, sometimes all too readily.

The rules also recognise two special categories: Vendemmia Tardiva, late harvest, with grapes picked no earlier than 1 October, and Muffa Nobile, from botrytis-affected grapes that must reach at least 16% potential alcohol. The autumn mists rising from the rivers below the cliff make this one of the few areas in Italy where noble rot is at home, as we explained in our article on the subject. There is also a curious historical detail, reported in some accounts but hard to document precisely: in the past, botrytis is said to have struck the grapes mainly after picking, while they were stored in the damp caves, rather than on the vine as in Sauternes.

## A name worth reclaiming

My view is that Orvieto is still paying for the years when it was a byword for any old white, sold in vast quantities with few questions asked. The raw material for a comeback is all there: a grape with real character in Grechetto, a history few regions can match, a sweet tradition nobody else in Italy can claim in quite the same way. What is needed is to stop thinking of it as the trattoria's house white and start treating it as a territory, with all the differences a territory brings. The sun in the bottle is still there. You just have to want to look for it.
