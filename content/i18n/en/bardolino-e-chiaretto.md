Anyone who lives in northern Italy knows Bardolino before they have ever tasted it. It is the name of a lakeside town, of a Sunday stroll, of a carafe served with lake whitefish in a trattoria in Lazise. That very familiarity has done it a disservice: for decades it was treated as the holiday red, the one you drink without thinking, squeezed between the prestige of Amarone a few kilometres away and, more recently, the success of its pink sibling. Yet behind that pale glass lies a precise territory, an unusual geology and a denomination that in recent years has decided to take itself seriously.

## A lake carved by ice

To understand Bardolino you have to start with what you cannot see. The hills on the eastern shore of Garda, between the lake and the Adige valley, are moraine hills: heaps of debris pushed forward and then abandoned by the ancient glacier that filled the lake basin during the Ice Ages. When the ice retreated it left behind arcs of stones, gravels, sands and clays jumbled together with no order, often changing within a few metres.

For the vine, this is instructive ground. The stony soils drain well and allow no excess; those with more clay hold water and give slightly fuller wines. The lake, for its part, acts as both radiator and fan: it softens the winters, cools the summers with its breezes and lengthens the season. The result is not a powerful wine, nor should it be. It is a red of transparency, in which soil and exposure read more clearly than they would in a more concentrated wine.

Bardolino Classico, the historic zone, takes in the communes of Bardolino, Affi, Cavaion, Costermano, Garda and Lazise, and it is here that the hills come closest to the water. In all, the denomination counted around 2,500 hectares of vineyard in 2022.

## Corvina, but not the Amarone kind

The lead grape is Corvina, the same as in Valpolicella. But the same grape, a few kilometres away and without *appassimento*, the drying of grapes before fermentation, tells another story. In Valpolicella, as we explained when writing about the valley of four wines and about Amarone, Corvina is often pushed towards concentration. On Garda it stays true to its natural character: pale colour, light tannin, lively acidity, crunchy red fruit and a spicy note reminiscent of pepper.

The production rules allow between 35 and 95% Corvina and/or Corvinone, with Corvinone capped at 20%, from 5 to 40% Rondinella and up to 20% of other authorised red grapes, among them Molinara, which may not exceed 15%. The most telling detail is recent: from the 2021 harvest the maximum share of Corvina rose from 80 to 95%. It is a stylistic choice. That freedom could have been used to beef the wine up with international varieties; instead the region chose to back the grape that best expresses the lightness of the lake.

A basic red Bardolino may not be released before 15 January of the year after the harvest and needs a minimum of 10.5% alcohol. Modest numbers, which already say what kind of wine is intended: not a reserve for the cellar, but a red that lives on its freshness.

## Three subzones that already existed

The real novelty of recent years has an old flavour. With the production rules that came into force in 2021, and could already be applied to the 2020 harvest, Bardolino recognised three subzones: La Rocca, Montebaldo and Sommacampagna. The Consorzio likes to point out that it did not invent them. As early as 1825 wine merchants distinguished between these areas, and in 1900 Giovanni Battista Perez, in his study *La Provincia di Verona ed i suoi vini*, had described them on geomorphological grounds. The 1968 rules had simply ignored them.

The three zones have distinct characters.

- **La Rocca** takes its name from the hill north of Bardolino that overlooks the lake, and covers the communes facing the water, from Torri del Benaco to Peschiera, by way of Garda, Bardolino, Lazise and Castelnuovo.
- **Montebaldo** climbs towards the slopes of Monte Baldo, between Affi, Caprino Veronese, Cavaion, Costermano and Rivoli: higher altitudes, a cooler climate, tauter wines.
- **Sommacampagna**, the ancient *Summa Campànea*, occupies the south-eastern hills towards Verona and the Mincio, between Bussolengo, Pastrengo, Sona, Sommacampagna and Valeggio.

The rules for subzone wines are tighter: maximum yields of 100 quintals per hectare instead of 120, at least 11% alcohol, release no earlier than 1 September of the year after the harvest, and the subzone name on the label printed twice the size of the word Bardolino. The message is clear: the buyer should read the place first, the denomination second. It is Burgundian reasoning applied to a wine that, curiously, once had relatives in Burgundy. The Consorzio recalls that in the late nineteenth century these reds were served in Swiss hotels alongside Burgundy and Beaujolais, a historical detail hard to verify in full but telling about how they were perceived.

Alongside Bardolino DOC there is also Bardolino Superiore DOCG, recognised in 2001, which requires at least a year of ageing and a higher alcohol level. A small niche which, in volume and profile, has never really changed the image of the denomination.

## Pink, novello and the question of identity

Bardolino has always had more than one soul. There was the Novello, born in the 1980s on the model of Beaujolais nouveau, which survives today as a curiosity. And there is Chiaretto, which since 2021 has been called Chiaretto di Bardolino and has built an identity of its own on its bet on pale rosés, which we covered when explaining how Italian rosés are made. Its success has brought money and attention to the lake, but it has also left the red in an awkward position: too light to compete with Valpolicella, less fashionable than the pink.

The subzones are the answer to this stalemate. They do not ask Bardolino to become another wine; they ask it to be more precise. A La Rocca with a year of ageing and a Montebaldo from the higher slopes can show differences that a Bardolino from the flatlands, made with generous yields, never had the chance to express.

## A red to bring back to the table

For those who live in Italy, Bardolino has an advantage many grander reds lack: it is easy to find, fairly priced and demands no ritual. Served slightly cool, at around 14-15 degrees, it is an ideal partner for lake fish, salumi, white meats, pizza, even certain sea-fish dishes where a tannic red would do damage.

My view is that Bardolino has suffered above all from a misunderstanding: it was judged by the yardstick of powerful wines and found wanting. But lightness is not a flaw to be corrected; it is a form of precision. If the subzones manage to persuade growers to work with low yields and to sell the place before the name, the Garda red could become what cru Beaujolais has become for France: proof that a pale wine can also be a serious one.
