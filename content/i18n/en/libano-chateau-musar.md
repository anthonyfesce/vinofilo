Some wines are best told through soil and climate, others through history. Lebanon’s belong firmly to the second kind, for a simple reason: few countries have so long a memory of wine and, at the same time, so fragile a present. This is a land where vines grew before anyone had settled Rome, and where, between 1975 and 1990, the harvest went on while a civil war raged around it. Both stories meet in one name: Serge Hochar, the man behind Château Musar.

## Before the Greeks, before Rome

The roots are Phoenician. The Phoenicians, the merchants and seafarers who traded across the Mediterranean from the cities of the Lebanese coast, Tyre, Sidon and Byblos, were among the great spreaders of wine and vine-growing in the ancient world. Much of what is drunk today from Sicily to Spain and North Africa owes them, at a distance, part of its lineage.

The evidence is not lacking. At Tell el-Burak, near Sidon, excavations have uncovered a wine press dating from the seventh century BC. According to some scholars, the wine of Byblos was already being shipped to Egypt during the Old Kingdom, in the third millennium BC. And in the Bekaa Valley, at Baalbek, the so-called Temple of Bacchus, one of the best-preserved Roman temples anywhere, still has vine tendrils carved into its stone. For an Italian reader the nearest comparison is perhaps Magna Graecia, the Greek-settled south of Italy: a landscape where wine is an archaeological find before it is a product.

## The Bekaa, a high plateau between two ranges

The Bekaa is a long, elevated valley wedged between the Mount Lebanon range, on the seaward side, and the Anti-Lebanon, towards Syria. Most of the country’s vineyards lie here. Altitude and the swing between day and night temperatures preserve freshness in a climate that, at sea level, would simply be too hot.

Modern viticulture begins in 1857, when Jesuits planted Cinsault near Zahlé and founded Château Ksara, still Lebanon’s oldest commercial winery and its largest producer, with cellars dug into tunnels that tradition dates to Roman times. France did the rest. During the French Mandate, between 1920 and 1946, soldiers and diplomats created both a market and a culture of wine, and new houses were born. The grapes still tell of that era: Cinsault and Carignan, varieties of southern France, alongside Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Syrah. Among the whites, though, Lebanon guards two natives, Obaideh and Merwah, which in recent years have been moving from curiosity to something like a small national flag.

## Gaston, Serge and Ghazir

Château Musar was founded in 1930 by Gaston Hochar. The winery is not in the Bekaa but at Ghazir, on the coast north of Beirut; the grapes come from vineyards in the valley, some 70 kilometres away. A logistical detail that, a few decades later, would turn into an epic.

His son Serge, born in 1939, trained as a civil engineer before choosing wine. He studied oenology in Bordeaux under Émile Peynaud, the professor who did more than anyone to shape modern winemaking, and returned to Lebanon to take over from his father in the cellar. His style, however, ended up bearing little resemblance to textbook Bordeaux: reds that age for a long time, evolve unpredictably and divide enthusiasts. Musar’s red, traditionally built on Cabernet Sauvignon, Cinsault and Carignan, has always been a wine to love or to argue with, rarely one to ignore.

## Harvesting under fire

With the civil war the domestic market crumbled, and Hochar began travelling to sell abroad, starting with the United Kingdom. The breakthrough came in 1979 at the Bristol Wine Fair, when Michael Broadbent, one of the most authoritative voices in British wine, named the 1967 Musar the discovery of the fair. From then on, the name of a Lebanese producer began to circulate in English cellars alongside those of Bordeaux.

Back home, meanwhile, making wine meant crossing a front line. The grapes, hand-picked in the Bekaa, travelled by lorry to Ghazir, and those 70 kilometres could take days or weeks. In 1982, during the Israeli invasion, Musar’s vineyards found themselves in the line of fire. The 1990 vintage required a 250-kilometre detour. The 1984 crop came by sea and began fermenting during the voyage: the wine was never released.

It was against this backdrop that, in 1984 itself, the British magazine Decanter created its Man of the Year award and gave it, for the first time, to Serge Hochar, for the stubbornness with which he had gone on making wine in spite of the war. Hochar died in 2014, aged 75, while on holiday with his family in Mexico; the estate is now in the hands of the next generation, starting with his sons Gaston and Marc.

## After Musar

For a long time Lebanese wine was, in the eyes of the world, little more than Musar and Ksara. Until 1995 there were just five producers. Today there are around fifty wineries, and in 1997 the Union Vinicole du Liban, the producers’ association, was set up after the country joined the International Organisation of Vine and Wine. Production estimates do not agree, ranging between 9 and 15 million bottles a year depending on the source: a figure that on its own shows how small Lebanon remains on the world stage, and how much its weight is cultural rather than commercial.

My view is that the lesson of Musar reaches beyond Lebanon. It is a reminder that wine is not only terroir and technique, but also will: the act of someone who keeps on harvesting when it would be more reasonable to stop. Yet it would be unfair to reduce Lebanese wine to a tale of resistance. The test of maturity now is a different one: to be chosen for what is in the glass, not for the story behind it. The native whites and a new generation of wineries seem to be heading that way.
