Few grapes lead a double life quite so successfully. In France it is called Syrah and lives on slopes so steep the vines look as if they have been pegged out to dry; in Australia it is called Shiraz and built the reputation of an entire country. Same grape, same DNA, two opposite ways of being in the world. For decades the name on the label was almost a promise of style: Syrah for the taut, peppery wine, Shiraz for the warm, generous one. A simplification, of course, but not without a grain of truth.

## A Persian legend, a village truth

For a long time the favourite story had Syrah arriving from the Persian city of Shiraz, or from Syracuse, carried into Gaul by some Roman emperor. Both tales are charming and entirely undocumented. The real answer came from the laboratory: in the late 1990s Carole Meredith’s team at the University of California, Davis used DNA profiling to establish that Syrah is the offspring of two obscure varieties, Dureza, a black grape from the Ardèche, and Mondeuse Blanche, a white grape from Savoie. Both parents come from a small corner of south-eastern France, not far from the northern Rhône.

So, no Persia: Syrah is a provincial grape that made good. It also has unexpected relatives in Italy. As we noted when writing about Teroldego, genetic studies have found a second-degree link between that Trentino red and Syrah, something like an uncle and nephew separated by the Alps.

## Hermitage and Côte-Rôtie, the Rhône school

The northern Rhône is the cradle, and here Syrah is the only red grape that matters. By rule, labels name not the variety but only the appellation: anyone buying an Hermitage or a Côte-Rôtie is expected to know that Syrah is inside.

The hill of Hermitage, above Tain, was for centuries the most famous name in the valley. Between the 18th and the mid-19th centuries much of the wine leaving the area went to Bordeaux, where the leading châteaux used it to lend body and colour to their claret in weak vintages. A luxury supporting role that would horrify any modern legal department. The rules also allow a small share of white grapes, Marsanne and Roussanne, but in practice most red Hermitage is pure Syrah: deep, tannic wines that need years.

Further north, Côte-Rôtie, the “roasted slope”, has been an appellation since 1940 and covers little more than two hundred hectares of vertiginous terraces. Here lies a detail that has been copied around the world: the rules permit up to 20% Viognier, an aromatic white grape, provided it is fermented together with the Syrah rather than added afterwards. This is co-fermentation, not blending. Viognier softens, adds floral perfume and helps fix the colour, and anyone who has tried to imitate it elsewhere, Australia included, has learnt that the dose makes all the difference. Within the appellation, tradition distinguishes the Côte Brune, on dark, iron-rich schist, with more tannic, longer-lived wines, from the Côte Blonde, on granite and pale schist, with more elegant wines that are ready sooner.

## Barossa, where Syrah becomes Shiraz

The grape reached Australia in 1832 with cuttings gathered in Europe by James Busby, planted first around Sydney and in the Hunter Valley and then, from 1839, in South Australia. The name Shiraz, already recorded in British sources in the 1830s, took hold there; for a long time “Hermitage” was used too, until it was dropped once the French appellation gained full protection.

The Barossa Valley, north of Adelaide, is where history becomes vineyard. Phylloxera, which devastated vineyards elsewhere, never reached South Australia, so ungrafted vines planted in the 19th century survive: the Shiraz vines at Turkey Flat in Tanunda, planted in 1847, are considered among the oldest in the world still in commercial production. There is a pleasing paradox here: to see what a European vineyard might have looked like before the catastrophe, you have to travel to the other side of the planet.

The classic Barossa style is the one that made Shiraz famous in the 1990s: ripe fruit, generous alcohol, often American oak, plushness. In 1951 Max Schubert created Grange from Shiraz, the wine that gave Australia a red worth collecting. In recent years, however, many producers have sought cooler sites and a lighter touch, and the Syrah-or-Shiraz choice on the label now says more about the winemaker’s intentions than about latitude.

## And in Italy? Cortona

Readers who know Italy through Sangiovese and Nebbiolo may be surprised, but Syrah has found a home here too. It is grown in Sicily, where it often partners Nero d'Avola, in Lazio and in Puglia, and Piedmont even has a DOC that spells it Sirah. The most interesting case, though, is Cortona, the Etruscan hill town in eastern Tuscany, on the border with Umbria.

Syrah’s presence there comes with its own legends, from the Crusades to the Medici, none of them documented. More solid is the account of vines brought from France in the early 20th century by a Tuscan nobleman, rediscovered in a few vineyards in the 1960s and then championed from the 1970s, when agronomists, among them Attilio Scienza of the University of Milan, noticed how well it performed on those sunny hillsides. Today Syrah accounts for a very large share of local vineyards (some sources say four vines in five, a figure best taken as indicative) and the Cortona DOC includes a varietal version. It is one of the few places in Italy where an international grape seems less a guest than a resident.

## Two names, one question

My view is that the Syrah-versus-Shiraz opposition was useful for selling wine but has become rather lazy. The real question is not which name is on the label but whether the wine lets the place speak: the granite of Côte-Rôtie, the old roots of the Barossa, the hills of Cortona. A grape born of two unknown provincial parents conquered three continents precisely because it adapts. The risk is that, by adapting too much, it ends up resembling nothing but itself.
