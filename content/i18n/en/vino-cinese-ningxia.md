Every year, around November, something happens in Ningxia’s vineyards that no European grower would do by choice: the vines are bent to the ground and buried. Covered with sand and soil, they stay underground until spring, when they are dug out one by one and tied back onto the wires. It is an enormous job, done largely by hand, and it says more than any statistic about what making wine in China’s dry heartland means. Yet it is from this corner of inland Asia that, over the past fifteen years, some of the wines have come that forced the world to take the idea of fine Chinese wine seriously.

## Between the Yellow River and the Helan Mountains

Ningxia is a small autonomous region in northern China, far from the coast and from the great markets of Shanghai and Beijing. The wine zone runs along the eastern flank of the Helan Mountains, a bare, rugged range, and across the plain that slopes down towards the Yellow River. The climate is continental and dry, with long, cold, arid winters. Experts consider the climate and geography well suited to wine grapes; the problem is surviving the winter.

Hence the burying. Frost and dry air, from November to March, would kill vines left exposed, and the earth serves as a blanket. For an Italian reader, used to hillsides where the vine stands all year round and winter work means pruning, the image is almost baffling; for anyone growing vines in the most extreme continental climates, much less so. The winemaker Emma Gao, one of the region’s better-known figures, has warned that harsher winters are damaging the vines: here too, climate is a moving target.

## A region built in a hurry

Growth has been dizzying. According to figures cited in the sources consulted, the region’s vineyard area went from about 2,660 hectares in 2004 to about 39,300 in 2014. These numbers should be read with caution, since it is not always clear what they include, but the order of magnitude says it all. By the end of 2016 there were around a hundred working wineries, another eighty under construction and some fifty more in planning.

Behind it lies a deliberate political choice. The Chinese authorities designated the eastern foothills of the Helan Mountains as an area for wine, and the country’s big groups followed: from the historic giant Changyu to the Dynasty group, and on to names with no obvious link to wine, such as the state oil company CNPC and the appliance maker Midea. For a sense of historical depth, Changyu, regarded as China’s first modern winery, was founded in 1892 at Yantai, in Shandong, by the entrepreneur Zhang Bishi. Ningxia, by comparison, is a story from yesterday morning.

The grapes are the ones the Chinese market has learnt to recognise: chiefly Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot among the reds, Chardonnay and Riesling among the whites. The declared model is Bordeaux, with its château-style wineries and its blends. Elsewhere in China, Marselan has also spread, a French crossing of Cabernet Sauvignon and Grenache created in 1961 by the ampelographer Paul Truel, which has become very popular in China.

## The medal that changed the conversation

The date everyone quotes is September 2011. At the Decanter World Wine Awards in London, one of the most closely followed competitions in the world, a 2009 Bordeaux-style red, Jiabeilan from the Helan Qingxue winery, won the international trophy for red Bordeaux varietals over £10. Some experts raised doubts about the wine’s origin, but the news went round the world. A few months later, in December 2011, at a blind tasting in Beijing pitting wines from Ningxia against Bordeaux, four of the top five places went to the Chinese.

One medal does not make a region, and a blind tasting says a great deal about who is tasting and what goes into the glasses. But from that moment Ningxia stopped being a curiosity for exotic travel pieces. We touched on it too when covering the Hong Kong Wine & Dine Festival, where wines from the region now appear alongside European ones.

## The consumption paradox

The most interesting point, however, lies elsewhere. Just as China was learning to make better wine, the Chinese began to drink less of it. In the early 2010s the country was among the world’s great markets: according to Vinexpo and IWSR estimates, about 2.17 billion bottles were drunk in 2013, the equivalent of more than 16 million hectolitres. Today the picture is reversed. According to the OIV, the International Organisation of Vine and Wine, consumption in 2025 stopped at 4.8 million hectolitres, 13% down on the previous year. The two sets of figures come from different sources and are not strictly comparable, but the direction is beyond doubt: in little more than ten years the market has shrunk to less than a third.

There is a further paradox, which we flagged when discussing the OIV’s world figures: China has one of the largest vineyard areas on the planet, about 733,000 hectares, yet produced just 2.2 million hectolitres of wine. Much of that fruit ends up on the table as fresh grapes or becomes raisins. Wine is a minority share of Chinese viticulture, and Ningxia is its most ambitious shop window, not the rule.

## A gamble worth following

My view is that Ningxia is one of the most instructive stories in contemporary wine, even for those who will never drink a bottle of it. It shows that with capital, political will and capable consultants a region able to make heads turn in London can be built in twenty years. But it also shows the limits of that model: a region born for a growing market now has to reckon with a shrinking one, and with an identity still borrowed from Bordeaux. The next medal will matter less than the next decision: working out what Ningxia can say that nobody else can. Vines buried under the sand every winter are a good place to start.
