Some grape varieties are saved by love, others by stubbornness. Arneis belongs to the second family. For centuries it was a supporting player, a white grape planted next to Nebbiolo to keep it company; then, within a few decades, it all but disappeared. Today it is Piedmont’s most recognisable white and the calling card of a territory, the Roero, that until not so long ago was introduced mainly as “the one across from the Langhe”. A double comeback, then, that deserves to be told without rhetoric.

## A grape in the service of Nebbiolo

The name already says a lot about its character. In Piedmontese dialect “arneis” suggests something wayward and unreliable: depending on the source, a little rascal or a thing of little worth, and the etymology remains disputed. What is certain is that it is a difficult grape. Its acidity is naturally low, it tends to overripen if picked after September, it suffers from powdery mildew, yields little and oxidises easily. Not exactly the profile of a variety on which to build a career.

That is why, for a long time, its role was a different one. It was planted in the same vineyards as Nebbiolo and its grapes, by long-standing practice, were used to soften the hardest reds of the area, Barolo included. It was even said that its scent drew the birds, distracting them from the more precious bunches. Not by chance its old names include “Nebbiolo bianco” and “Barolo bianco”, even though it has no genetic kinship with Nebbiolo: it was white, it grew close by, and that was enough.

## The 1970s, two names

The twentieth century was the century of decline. Plantings fell throughout it: once the job it had been planted for disappears, a wayward and ungenerous grape is the first to be grubbed up. By the 1970s, according to the most widely cited accounts, only two producers were still making it as a varietal wine: Vietti and Bruno Giacosa, two names that helped write the history of Barolo and that, in this case, bet on a white nobody was looking for.

The revival began in the 1980s, when a taste for fresh, aromatic whites opened up a market and Roero’s producers realised they had something of their own. In 2000 estimates put plantings at around 745 hectares; today, according to the most recent figures we found, the denomination’s Arneis exceeds 800 hectares, far more than Nebbiolo in the same area. The understudy has become the lead.

## What the DOCG says

Roero became a DOC in 1985 and was promoted to DOCG in the mid-2000s. The denomination has two souls: Roero Arneis, the white, and Roero, a red from Nebbiolo. For the white the rules require at least 95% Arneis, with an optional balance of other non-aromatic white grapes. Maximum yield is 10 tonnes of grapes per hectare and the wine may not be released before 1 March of the year after the harvest. There is also a Riserva, with at least 16 months of ageing, and a sparkling version.

The production zone covers 19 communes on the left bank of the Tanaro, four of them in full: Canale, Corneliano d'Alba, Piobesi d'Alba and Vezza d'Alba. For some years the rules have also allowed the name of the commune or locality of origin to appear on the label, the so-called *menzioni geografiche aggiuntive* (additional geographical mentions), as well as the word “vigna” for individual vineyards entered in a regional register. It is a sign that here, too, people are thinking in terms of precise places, not just of a grape.

## Sands, rocche and another Nebbiolo

Anyone driving from Alba across the Tanaro notices it at once: the landscape changes. The Roero hills are steeper and more irregular, broken by the *rocche*, the sheer walls carved out by erosion, with woods and chestnut groves alternating with vineyards. The soils, compared with the compact marls of the Langhe, are looser and sandier.

That difference shows in the glass. Roero Arneis gives whites of delicate perfume, often with notes of white flowers, pear and a faintly almondy finish, playing on finesse rather than power. Precisely because acidity is not its strong suit, the timing of the harvest and a cellar careful with oxygen do almost all the work: an Arneis picked late turns flabby, a well-made one has a subtle tension of its own.

Then there is the red, which many forget. Roero DOCG must contain at least 95% Nebbiolo and age for at least 20 months, six of them in wood; the Riserva goes up to 32 months. On sand, according to the most common reading, Nebbiolo tends to give wines that are more perfumed and less severe than those of Barolo and Barbaresco, with less rasping tannin, ready sooner. It is not a lesser Barolo: it is Nebbiolo with a different accent, and anyone who loves the grape knows that it changes character every time it moves a few kilometres.

## The lesson of an almost lost white

The Arneis story is instructive for the whole of Italian wine, from the Timorasso of the Tortona hills to the many forgotten varieties now making a return. It shows that a difficult grape is not doomed, provided someone is willing to understand it, and that a territory sitting “across from” a more famous one can build an identity without imitating it.

Our view is that the Roero has won the first match, the one for survival, and is now playing the second, harder one: proving that Arneis is not just a pleasant aperitif white but a wine able to express different communes and vineyards. The geographical mentions point in that direction. If producers can resist the temptation to turn it into a uniform white, easy and ready, the wayward grape that nearly vanished could become the most interesting of Piedmont’s whites. It already has the character.
