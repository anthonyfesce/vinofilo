The problem with capon is not the capon. It is everything we put inside it, underneath it and around it. On its own it is a gentle white meat, almost shy, that would ask for a wine of equally good manners. Then along comes the stuffing with sausage, chestnuts, *mostarda* (candied fruit in mustard syrup) and Parmigiano, or the broth that has spent three hours on the stove with celery and cloves, and the wine we chose for the meat finds itself duelling with an entire larder.

The same goes for turkey, which is topical in Italy this year too, thanks to an American calendar ever more present in Italian homes. Thanksgiving falls on Thursday 26 November 2026, and anyone celebrating it in Milan or Rome faces the same question a Piedmontese faces in front of the Christmas capon. The answer is the same: you pair the dish, not the animal.

## The meat matters less than the sauce

A capon is a castrated cockerel, raised for a long time, with meat fattier and more flavourful than chicken but which remains, on the palate, a white meat. Turkey is even more so, and it also tends to dry out. Neither has the strength of a braise or of game: pour a tannic, concentrated red alongside and the wine covers everything, reducing the meat to a mere support for the glass.

But nobody eats a naked capon. The stuffing adds fat, salt and spice; the roasted skin adds toasty notes; the mostarda adds sweetness and heat at once; the side dishes (mash, cabbage, chestnuts, and in the American case cranberry sauce) add yet more flavours. That is where the bottle is decided. The practical criterion is simple: the lighter the preparation, the finer and fresher the wine should be; the richer it is, the more structure the wine needs, but almost never heavy tannins. Acidity, at these long lunches, is the true ally: it cleanses the mouth of fat and keeps the conversation going all the way to the panettone.

## Boiled capon, the realm of lightness

Boiled capon is the most delicate of the three dishes and the hardest to pair, because it forgives no excess. In the traditions of the North it often arrives after tortellini, *anolini* or *cappelletti*, small filled pastas cooked in its own broth, served with mostarda or *salsa verde*. Here the wine has two jobs: to refresh and not to overpower.

The most immediate choice, at least in Emilia, is a dry Lambrusco, ideally in the paler, more acidic versions such as those from Sorbara: the bubbles and acidity cut the fat, the light fruit does not overwhelm. It is the same reasoning that puts Lambrusco beside *bollito misto*, the mixed boiled meats, and it works with capon too. Those who prefer whites can opt for a dry, savoury white from the North that is not too aromatic: a Soave Classico, a Lugana, a Friulano. With salsa verde, which has herbs, garlic and anchovies, a white with good acidity holds up better than a red.

If the lunch is Piedmontese, a Grignolino or a not-too-structured Dolcetto does the job with elegance. The rule remains the same: no intrusive oak, no excess alcohol.

## Stuffed capon, when the dish raises its voice

Stuffed capon is another story. Filled with minced meat, sausage, eggs, cheese, sometimes chestnuts or dried fruit, and then roasted: it is a rich, fatty, fragrant dish. Here a slender white simply vanishes.

You need whites with body and a certain softness that still have acidity. A Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi with a few years behind it, a Fiano di Avellino, a Timorasso from the Colli Tortonesi: wines with the structure to stand up to the stuffing, but which never become cloying. This is also the territory of light, fragrant reds, the ones the French would call bistro wines and that we call, more simply, table wines in the best sense. An Alto Adige or Oltrepò Pinot Nero, a Schiava, a Valpolicella that is not a *ripasso*, a young Barbera not aged in barrique: all have red fruit, freshness and soft tannins.

If there is mostarda or fruit in the stuffing, beware of wines that are too dry and austere: the sweetness of the dish makes them seem hard and bitter. Better a wine with more evident fruit.

## Roast turkey, the Thanksgiving case

The American Thanksgiving turkey is perhaps the hardest meal of the year to pair, because everything is on the table at once: dry white meat, spiced stuffing, gravy, sweet potatoes, green beans and the cranberry sauce, tart and sugary. No wine can be perfect with all of it. You have to look for the one that gets nothing wrong.

That is why, in the United States, the most recommended choice has long been Pinot Noir, which has the acidity for the cranberry sauce and the lightness for the meat. An alternative is Beaujolais, and some people play on a quirk of the calendar: Beaujolais Nouveau is released on the third Thursday of November, a week before Thanksgiving. Those who want to stay in Italy can apply the same reasoning to a Pinot Nero from Alto Adige, a Schiava, a Sicilian Frappato, a Grignolino: pale reds, almost transparent, to be served slightly cool.

And then there are rosés, which remain underrated in November. A Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo, a deeply coloured, full-bodied rosé, has the structure of a light red and the freshness of a white; a Chiaretto from Lake Garda is more delicate but equally versatile. For a table where different tastes have to coexist, a rosé with some substance is often the smartest compromise.

## Bubbles, the year-end wild card

It is worth remembering in the days when corks are popped out of tradition: a dry *metodo classico*, Italy’s traditional-method sparkling wine, with a few years of ageing on the lees, is a serious companion for capon in any form. The acidity and the carbonation cut through fat, while the notes of bread crust and dried fruit converse with the roasted skin and the stuffing. A Franciacorta, a Trento, an Alta Langa can see you through an entire lunch, from broth to roast, without embarrassment. The richer versions, with longer on the lees, cope better with stuffings.

The one mistake to avoid is sweetness: an off-dry sparkling wine beside capon is a mess for both of them.

## Our view

Faced with white meats and complicated stuffings, the temptation is always to open the most important red in the cellar, because it is a holiday and because the bottle has been waiting. It is almost always a mistake. A great Barolo or a great Amarone deserves a dish that can answer back, and capon, even stuffed, is not that dish.

The right wine for capon and turkey is one with more freshness than power, more fruit than oak, more grace than muscle. A pale red, a structured white, a serious rosé, a dry sparkling wine. Bottles that do not demand attention but repay it, and that turn up empty at the end of lunch without anyone having noticed. It is the finest compliment a wine can receive at Christmas.
