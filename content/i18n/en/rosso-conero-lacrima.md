Anyone who spends the summer on the Riviera del Conero tends to come home with two memories: the white beaches beneath the cliffs and a bottle of Verdicchio bought out of habit. A pity, because a few miles from Ancona two reds are made that have little in common beyond their closeness to the sea: a dark, briny Montepulciano, and a small, forgotten grape that smells like a bunch of roses. We know the white Marche. The red one deserves a second trip.

## A mountain, a park, a grape

The Conero is a limestone promontory that rises almost sheer from the Adriatic, an oddity on a coastline that runs flat for long stretches. Around it lies a regional park of some 6,000 hectares, and on the hills behind it, between Ancona and Osimo, Montepulciano has found one of its best homes outside Abruzzo. We have dealt elsewhere with the confusion between the grape and the Tuscan town; what matters here is something else, namely what this grape becomes when it grows with the sea in front of it and limestone underfoot.

Rosso Conero has been a DOC since 1967 and, by several accounts, was the first in the Marche. The rules require at least 85% Montepulciano, with up to 15% Sangiovese making up the balance; in practice many producers make it as a single variety. The zone is small and precisely drawn: Ancona, Camerano, Offagna, Sirolo, Numana and parts of Castelfidardo and Osimo. The soils are lime-rich clays, which strip Montepulciano of some of the round generosity it shows in Abruzzo and give it a tauter texture in exchange, at times almost rustic in youth.

## From Rosso to Conero DOCG

In 2004 the old Rosso Conero Riserva became a denomination in its own right: Conero DOCG. Its rules are those of a wine made to wait. At least two years of ageing counted from 1 November of the harvest year, a compulsory spell in wood, a minimum of 12.5% alcohol. There is a curious detail too: the word Montepulciano may not appear on a DOCG label. The grape is there, but the declared protagonist has to be the place.

It is a choice that pleases or irritates depending on how you look at wine. Those who shop by grape are left bewildered; those who believe in terroir find it only right. The scale, in any case, is that of a niche denomination: according to the available figures, in 2021 the DOCG had about 173 registered hectares.

Its modern reputation is, after all, recent. Until the 1980s Rosso Conero was chiefly the red of Ancona’s *osterie*, the old-fashioned taverns; then a few cellars began cutting yields and working on ageing, and the guides took notice. According to the record, the first Marche wine to win Gambero Rosso’s Tre Bicchieri was a Rosso Conero, from the 1988 vintage.

## Lacrima, the grape that weeps

Move a few dozen kilometres inland, into the hills behind Senigallia, and the register changes completely. Here, around the village of Morro d'Alba, survives Lacrima, a black grape that came close to vanishing in the twentieth century. It was once common along the central Adriatic; then the vineyards shrank almost to nothing. Its rescue came through the Lacrima di Morro d'Alba DOC, created in 1985.

The name has a more physical than poetic explanation. The skin, firm but fragile, tends to split when the grape is ripe, and from those small cracks seep drops of juice that look like tears. The nose does the rest: rose, violet, wild strawberry, cherry. The credit goes chiefly to geraniol, a terpene present in notable quantities in this grape, which makes it one of Italy’s rare semi-aromatic reds. Those who taste it without knowing what it is usually suspect a mistake, as if someone had poured an aromatic white into a red.

The zone takes in six communes: Morro d'Alba, Monte San Vito, San Marcello, Belvedere Ostrense, Ostra and Senigallia, where valley floors and seaward-facing slopes are excluded. The rules require at least 85% Lacrima. There is a Superiore version, which may not be released before 1 September of the year after the harvest, and a Passito from dried grapes. Then there is the legend, which they tell with relish in Morro d'Alba: in 1167 Frederick Barbarossa is said to have stayed at the castle and admired the local wine. A legend it is, and written records of the grape only appear in the nineteenth century. But the walled village is worth the stop even without emperors.

## Two reds, two tables

The beauty of this pair is that they do not compete. Conero, especially as DOCG, asks for serious food and time: meat, slow-cooked sauces, the inland cooking of the hills around Ancona, ideally after a few years in the cellar. Lacrima should be served younger and a little cooler, and its perfume makes it an odd, likeable red for the everyday table, even with salumi or gently spiced food. The danger with Lacrima is excess: when the aroma turns to boiled sweets, the wine loses its grace. The best versions keep the scent inside a dry frame.

For those living in Italy, the simplest thing is to go and find them on the spot. Ancona can be reached by train from almost anywhere along the Adriatic line, the Riviera del Conero can be covered in a weekend, and Morro d'Alba is not far. In winter, without the crowds of bathers, the promontory goes back to being what it is: a mountain on the sea with vines at its feet.

## A region worth rereading

My view is that the Marche are still paying for Verdicchio’s success: so good that it makes people forget the rest. Conero DOCG is a red of place with serious rules and tiny numbers, which is exactly why it should stake everything on identity rather than concentration. Lacrima, for its part, has an advantage many rare grapes lack: you recognise it at the first sip. In an Italy full of correct, interchangeable reds, a wine that smells of roses and asks no one’s permission is a small stroke of luck. It would be a shame to notice it only in summer, under a beach umbrella.
