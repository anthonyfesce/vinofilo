From the beach at Alassio or Sanremo, Liguria seems made for the sun lounger. Climb a few hairpin bends, though, and the sea becomes a distant glint while the land turns vertical: dry-stone walls, terraces as narrow as steps, vineyards that seem to hang over the void. The Ponente, the western half of the region running from Genoa to the French border, makes little wine and makes it the hard way. That is exactly why it deserves to be known better than as the generic “white for fish” ordered in a trattoria without a glance at the label.

We have already written on Vinofilo about the Cinque Terre and Sciacchetrà, at the other end of the region. Here we stay in the west.

## A small denomination with many voices

Riviera Ligure di Ponente has been a DOC since 1988 and takes in much of the western Riviera, chiefly the provinces of Imperia and Savona. The figure that says it all is its size: around 235 hectares declared in 2021, according to data gathered by Italian Wine Central. For a sense of scale, that is smaller than many single estates in Bordeaux or the Napa Valley. Within those hectares, though, sit five subzones: Albenganese, Finalese and Riviera dei Fiori for Pigato, Vermentino and Rossese; Quiliano for Granaccia, the Ligurian name for Alicante; Taggia for Moscatello, including passito and late-harvest versions.

The apparent muddle is a faithful portrait of a territory made of valleys running at right angles to the coast, each with its own microclimate and habits. Wine is made where it can be, on small plots, often alongside the olive tree, which here has always come first.

## Pigato and Vermentino: siblings or twins?

Pigato is the white the Ponente considers its own. Its name, according to the most often repeated etymology, comes from the dialect word *pigau*, spotted, for the tiny rust-coloured freckles that appear on the ripe berries. Its cradle is the plain of Albenga and the Arroscia valley, where it gives more structured, savoury wines, with a bitter finish reminiscent of scrubland herbs and an almost oily texture.

Vermentino, on the other hand, is everywhere: in Liguria, Sardinia, Tuscany, Corsica and Provence, where it goes by Rolle. In Liguria it tends to be fresher, more floral and more immediate than Pigato. The question that divides technicians and enthusiasts is whether they are really different grapes. Several DNA analyses have found a very close relationship between Pigato, Vermentino and Piedmont’s Favorita, to the point that some ampelographers regard them as biotypes of a single variety; other sources keep them distinct, and as early as 1964 the Italian Ministry of Agriculture registered Vermentino and Favorita separately. The question remains open.

Drinkers tend to settle the argument quickly: in the glass, they can be told apart. Whether that comes down to clone, soil or the winemaker’s hand is another matter, rightly left to the geneticists. What counts is that the Ponente has two whites with different personalities, and would do well not to blur them for commercial convenience.

## Rossese di Dolceacqua, the red that walks on air

In the far west, between Ventimiglia and the valleys climbing towards the Maritime Alps, lies Liguria’s most famous red. Rossese di Dolceacqua was the first Ligurian DOC, established on 28 January 1972, and covers eleven communes, from Dolceacqua to Apricale, from Perinaldo to Soldano. The vineyards sit between 300 and 600 metres, on gradients steeper than 35%: everything is done by hand, and the label *viticoltura eroica*, “heroic viticulture”, so often overused, is here simply descriptive.

The wine is a pale red, scented with strawberry and rose, with a spicy, saline streak and light tannin. It is a red to drink slightly cool, as happy with Ligurian-style rabbit as with oily fish, and at times it recalls a mountain Pinot Noir more than a Mediterranean red.

Two stories circulate around Rossese, and both need handling with care. The first has Napoleon, passing through, enjoying it at a banquet and ordering casks of it: it is a tale told with a “so they say”, with no known documents to support it, and should be taken as such. The second links Rossese to Provence’s Tibouren, which according to local tradition reached Dolceacqua with the Doria family, lords of the village; the grape’s precise origin, however, is still debated. France is only a few kilometres away, after all, and the border here has always been more administrative than cultural.

## Ormeasco, the Dolcetto that changed its name

Following the Arroscia valley up towards Piedmont you reach Pornassio, home of Ormeasco. It is no obscure grape: it is Dolcetto, the same variety as on the Piedmontese hills, called by its local name. The Pornassio or Ormeasco di Pornassio DOC was born in 2003 and requires at least 95% Dolcetto. Alongside the red, also made as a Superiore with at least a year’s ageing, there is a rosé with an irresistible name, Sciac-trà, and a passito.

Ormeasco is a useful reminder of how porous wine’s borders are. In Dogliani or Alba, Dolcetto is the everyday red; here, in the mountains, with wider swings between day and night temperatures, it tends to turn drier and more linear, with less plush fruit. Same grape, different character.

## The price of toil

None of these wines will ever be as cheap as a white from the plains, nor should they be. Yields are low, mechanisation is all but impossible, and the cost of an hour’s work on a sheer terrace cannot be squeezed. The real risk in Liguria is not price but abandonment: every vineyard left untended is a wall that collapses and a piece of landscape that slides down the hill.

My view is that the Ponente has an image problem, not a quality problem. It is drunk on holiday, absent-mindedly, and forgotten by autumn. It deserves instead to stay on the list all year round, alongside the Alto Adige whites and Etna reds we so love to write about. A Pigato from Albenga and a Rossese di Dolceacqua are not souvenirs: they are two of Italy’s most personal wines, born of a territory that has never had anything easy to offer, except the view.
