Harvest photos all end in the same place: brimming crates, purple hands, a tractor climbing the hill at sunset. Then the story breaks off, as if the wine were born that very day. In reality, when the last crate comes into the cellar, the most delicate work has yet to begin. Must becomes wine by degrees, and almost everything that decides what it will be happens in the weeks when nobody is taking pictures any more.

## The day the wine leaves its skins

For red wines the first step is called *svinatura*, the draining of the new wine off its skins. During alcoholic fermentation the must stays in contact with skins and pips, and that is where it takes its colour, tannin and much of its aroma. How long that contact should last is one of the most personal choices a winemaker makes: a few days for a red to drink young, weeks for one meant to age. At some point, though, the wine has to be separated from the solids. The valve at the bottom of the vat is opened and the liquid runs off by gravity or with a pump, leaving behind a dark, damp mass, the pomace.

What flows out of its own accord is the so-called *vino fiore*, the free-run wine: the finest, the cleanest. But a good deal of liquid is still trapped in the pomace, and this is where the press comes in. Pressing squeezes out what gravity left behind and yields a different wine, deeper in colour and tannin, sometimes coarser. Many cellars keep it apart and decide later whether, and how much, to blend back into the free-run: a few points of press wine can lend structure to a slightly slender red, too much makes it hard. It is one of the first real blending decisions, and it is taken when the wine is barely a few weeks old.

For whites the order is reversed: the grapes are pressed straight away, before fermentation, because skin contact is usually what you want to avoid. Those who make orange wine, naturally, play by the opposite rule.

There is also a limit that has nothing to do with taste. European rules forbid pressing grapes too hard and pressing the lees, precisely to keep the bitterest, coarsest substances out of the wine. And the leftover pomace is not just any waste: it is a by-product the producer must dispose of under supervision. In Italy the classic route is the distillery, which gives us grappa, but the 2023 national decree also lists other permitted uses, from spreading on fields to biogas, and exempts the very smallest producers.

## The second fermentation, the one that makes no noise

Once alcoholic fermentation is over, the new wine has a chemical problem: it contains malic acid, the same acid as in an unripe apple, sharp and angular. This is where malolactic fermentation steps in, which despite its name is not a fermentation in the yeast sense. It is carried out by lactic acid bacteria, above all a species called *Oenococcus oeni*, able to survive where almost nothing else does: high alcohol, strong acidity, scant nutrients. They turn malic acid into the softer lactic acid, releasing a little carbon dioxide along the way.

You can taste the result. The wine loses its green-fruit sharpness and gains roundness. But the more important reason is another, less poetic one: stability. Malic acid left in the wine is food for unwanted microbes, and a malolactic that starts on its own in bottle delivers uninvited bubbles and unpleasant smells. That is why nearly all red wines complete it before bottling. In whites it is a matter of style: some seek it out for softness, as often happens with Chardonnay, while others block it to keep freshness, not least because it can bring those buttery notes that divide drinkers, as our glossary reminds us.

The bacteria are demanding. Technical guidance from the Australian Wine Research Institute puts the ideal temperature between 18 and 22 °C, with 15 to 25 acceptable; above 25 the process slows and the risks rise. Sulphur dioxide inhibits them too, which is why it is added sparingly until the malolactic is finished, and more firmly straight afterwards. The end is not guessed by tasting: it is measured in the laboratory, by checking that the malic acid has gone.

## Why November is the quiet month

And here is why cellars seem asleep in November. The tumultuous fermentations are over, the vats no longer bubble, the sharp smell of must has faded. What happens now is slow and invisible: the bacteria work without foam, the suspended particles sink to the bottom, the wine begins to clear by itself.

The old farmhouse cellar knew this rhythm well without knowing anything about bacteria. When the cold got into the walls, the malolactic stopped, and it started again in spring with the first warm days. A wine that “stirred” in April was no mystery; it was biology postponed. Today many cellars prefer not to wait: they heat the rooms or the tanks, or inoculate selected bacteria, sometimes already during alcoholic fermentation. Those who work more traditionally let the winter take its course and accept the long wait.

It is also the time when the old Italian saying about St Martin’s Day, which we discussed in our piece on 11 November, finds a technical truth of its own: by then the must has become wine. But it is still a raw, cloudy wine that needs to be left in peace.

## Racking, or the art of not stirring things up

In peace, but not abandoned. The deposit that forms at the bottom, the lees, is made of dead yeast, fragments of skin and other particles. The gross lees, the first to settle, need to come off early: left too long they can give smells of rotten eggs and stale air. Racking does exactly this, moving the clear wine into another vessel and leaving the sediment where it lies.

It sounds trivial, and is in fact an exercise in balance. Every racking brings in a little oxygen: sometimes just enough to open up a closed wine, sometimes too much for a delicate one. So some rack often and some as little as possible, and some deliberately keep the wine on its fine lees, the lighter ones, stirring them now and then for texture, the famous *bâtonnage* of white Burgundy. In between comes topping up, refilling casks and tanks, because empty space above the wine is an invitation to oxidation.

None of this ends up on the label, and almost none of it is visible on a cellar visit. Yet this is where a good grape grower can turn into a poor winemaker, or the other way round.

## An opinion

Wine storytelling loves the spectacular moments: the harvest, the first taste, the bottle opened after twenty years. The months in between make no headlines, and perhaps rightly so. But anyone who really wants to understand a wine should ask the producer not only when they picked, but when they drained the vats, how much press wine went back in, whether they let the winter do its work or switched on the heating. They are dull questions, and for that very reason revealing. A wine’s character is often decided in a month when, apparently, nothing happens.
