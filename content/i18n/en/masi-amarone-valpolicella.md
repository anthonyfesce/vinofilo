In Valpolicella there is a small valley called the Vaio dei Masi, and the fact that a company selling millions of bottles still bears the name of that fold in the hills says a lot about how the Boscaini family tells its own story. The documented starting point is 1772: Giobatta Boscaini leased vineyards in that *vaio*, which the family would later acquire through marriage. This is the third chapter of the "Italian Excellence" series: after the sparkling wines of Trento and the Antinori's Sangiovese, the wine born of waiting.

## A valley, a family, many centuries

The hills where [Masi](https://www.masi.it/) works were planted with vines long before the Boscaini arrived: documents from 1194 already mention vineyards at Mazzano and Torbe, the slopes that today give two of the house's crus. In 1882 Paolo Boscaini founded the family firm, which his son Guido would expand. In 2022 the house celebrated its two hundred and fiftieth harvest, an anniversary with the merit of being counted from a contract rather than a legend.

The generation that took Masi to the world was that of Sandro Boscaini, at the helm for around half a century and now chairman. Under him Amarone, long a local, niche wine, became one of the most recognisable names in Italian wine abroad.

## Campofiorin and the birth of Ripasso

If Masi has one invention to claim, it is Campofiorin. With the 1964 vintage Guido Boscaini looked for a wine halfway between easy young Valpolicella and Amarone, designed for international markets. He achieved it by refermenting the wine on Amarone pomace: the method that would give rise to a whole category, Ripasso. Masi had registered the name as a trademark, then released it, and since 2000 the word has vanished from the Campofiorin label.

The technique changed in the same year: instead of Amarone pomace, semi-dried grapes are added to the fresh wine, a "double fermentation" the house considers more precise. The blend has stayed the same throughout, roughly 70% Corvina, 25% Rondinella and 5% Molinara. It is a textbook example of how a historic house can change its method without betraying the wine.

## Appassimento as a discipline

Amarone della Valpolicella has been a DOCG since 2010, and its rules are kept by the [Consorzio Tutela Vini Valpolicella](https://www.consorziovalpolicella.it/). But Masi built its reputation above all on one idea: that the drying of grapes in the *fruttai*, the lofts where bunches rest for months before pressing, is not a technical detail but the heart of the wine. Sandro Boscaini has said it many times: without at least a hundred days of drying, to his mind, there is no Amarone. For the fundamentals of the technique, see our [Amarone, the wine born of waiting](/en/articles/2026.10.02/amarone-appassimento-recioto-ripasso/).

The house also revived Oseleta, an almost forgotten local grape that Masi was the first to include in the blend of its top Amarones. And the crus trace a precise geography: Campolongo di Torbe, bottled separately since 1958, Mazzano since 1964, Vaio Armaron since 1979. The last comes from the Serego Alighieri estate at Gargagnago, bought in 1353 by Pietro Alighieri, Dante's son: Masi has managed it since 1973.

## What to taste first

The house's Amarones are deep garnet, with ruby glints in younger wines; Campofiorin is an intense ruby red. A three-glass path:

- **Campofiorin**, to understand the idea that changed Valpolicella. In the UK, according to The Drinks Business, it sells for around 15–20 pounds;
- **Costasera**, Amarone della Valpolicella Classico, the house's most representative wine, from hills facing west towards Lake Garda, as the name suggests;
- one of the **crus**, Mazzano or Campolongo di Torbe, for anyone who wants to see how much the hillside matters even in a wine made from dried grapes.

For the crus and the riservas, prices vary widely by vintage and market, and are those of great age-worthy reds.

## How to visit

Since 30 May 2025 the house has welcomed visitors at [Monteleone21](https://www.masi.it/en/masi-wine-experience/visit-our-sites/monteleone21/), its new headquarters at Sant'Ambrogio di Valpolicella, Via Monteleone 26. The highlight is a twelve-metre-tall drying loft that shows *appassimento* live, next to the cellars and the hospitality spaces of the Serego Alighieri estate. Visits and tastings are booked on the [dedicated page](https://www.masi.it/en/masi-wine-experience/cellar-tours-and-tastings-book-wine-tour/).

Some accuse Amarone of having become too big, too sweet, too much like itself. It is a risk for the whole appellation, and partly for those who made it famous. But Campofiorin is a reminder that innovation in Valpolicella was born right here, in an attempt to make a wine different from what everyone expected. The challenge today is to remember that.
