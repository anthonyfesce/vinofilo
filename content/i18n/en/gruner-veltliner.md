In the summer of 1985 Austrian wine discovered it had a problem no good vintage could fix. In German laboratories, analysts found diethylene glycol, a substance used in antifreeze, in a number of Austrian bottles. Within a few months a country that had been exporting tens of millions of litres found itself with almost no customers. Forty years on, Austrian wine is held up as a model of rigour, and its signature grape, Grüner Veltliner, sits on restaurant lists across half the world. It is a story of a fall and a recovery, and it is best told through the grape.

## The home grape

Grüner Veltliner is Austria’s most planted variety: according to the available figures it accounts for almost a third of the country’s vineyards, around 17,000 hectares in the years around 2010, out of a national vineyard area of a little over 44,000 hectares in 2024. For an Italian reader the proportion is hard to picture: no single grape in Italy carries so much of an entire country’s identity.

DNA has settled the question of its origins. One parent is Traminer (or Savagnin); the other is an almost unknown vine found in 2000 at St. Georgen, in Burgenland, and christened St. Georgener-Rebe. Research published in 2007 confirmed the cross. Outside Austria it is grown mainly in central Europe (Slovakia, the Czech Republic, Hungary) and, in small quantities, in New Zealand and the United States. Italy has a corner of it too: in Alto Adige, in the Valle Isarco (the Eisack valley), where the DOC permits it and where the name Veltliner almost always means this very grape.

In the glass, Grüner is recognisable above all for one thing: white pepper. Alongside come citrus, green apple, herbs, sometimes lentils and fresh pulses in the younger versions, and a saline streak that makes it excellent at the table. It can be light and thirst-quenching, a tavern wine, or dense, broad and capable of ageing for years. That dual nature is the key to everything else.

## Wachau, white wine by weight

The Danube valley west of Krems, the Wachau, is where Grüner learnt to be taken seriously. The vines climb narrow terraces held up by dry-stone walls, a sight that reminds an Italian more of the Valtellina or the Cinque Terre than of any plain.

Here a growers’ association, the Vinea Wachau, devised a classification based not on the sugar in the grapes, as the German one is, but on the weight of the wine. Three categories, all for dry whites with no trace of oak:

- **Steinfeder**: up to 11.5% alcohol. The name comes from a feathery grass that grows on the stony terraces, as light as the wine.
- **Federspiel**: no more than 12.5%. The term comes from falconry: it was the lure used to call the falcon home.
- **Smaragd**: at least 12.5%. It takes its name from the emerald-green lizard that lives among the dry-stone walls.

It is a simple and slightly poetic system that tells the drinker what to expect without forcing them to learn oenological German. Steinfeder is the aperitif wine, Federspiel the elegant lunchtime white, Smaragd the cellar wine, the one that built the Wachau’s reputation. It should be said that these are the terms of a private association, not of the law, and not every producer in the valley uses them.

## Kamptal and Kremstal, the same grape in another light

Just to the east, along the Danube and its tributaries, two areas have built their identity on the same pair of grapes as the Wachau, Grüner Veltliner and Riesling. Kremstal, around the town of Krems, gained DAC status (roughly the Austrian equivalent of an Italian DOC, tied to origin) in 2007; Kamptal, along the river Kamp, in 2008. The very first DAC had been the Weinviertel, the large region north of Vienna, from the 2002 vintage, and it was devoted to Grüner.

The logic of the DAC reverses the traditional Austro-German one: the first thing on the label is not the grape but the place. It is the same path Italy took with its denominations, and in Austria it was in large part a response to the shock of 1985.

## The scandal and the lesson

Back to that summer. The first contaminated wine, according to most accounts, was a 1983 Ruster Auslese bought in a Stuttgart supermarket and analysed at the end of June 1985. On 9 July West Germany’s health ministry warned consumers against Austrian wines.

The motive was commercial, not medical. After a weak 1982 vintage, some producers and merchants could not supply supermarkets with the sweet late-harvest wines promised in their contracts. Sugar alone could not imitate their richness: glycol added body and apparent sweetness. Some of those wines were also illegally blended with German wines, spreading the scandal across the border.

The consequences were brutal. Exports, which before 1985 ran at about 45 million litres a year, collapsed to about 4.4 million in 1986, and it took some fifteen years to return to earlier levels. But the Austrian parliament reacted quickly: on 29 August 1985 it passed a far stricter wine law. The following year, in Italy, the methanol scandal would have far more tragic consequences, and there too the answer was a tightening of controls. Two countries, the same lesson: a reputation is lost in a week and rebuilt over a generation.

## The rebirth, and a temptation to resist

The generation that rebuilt Austrian wine bet on dry wines, on precision and on origin. Grüner Veltliner was the perfect candidate: a native, versatile grape that no other country could claim. A tasting organised in 2002 by Jancis Robinson and Tim Atkin, in which some Grüners finished ahead of great white Burgundies, became the symbol of that redemption, rather as the Judgement of Paris of 1976 had been for California. Robinson described the grape, in a phrase that stuck in the English-speaking world, as “distinctly groovy”.

My view is that Grüner won its battle precisely because it never tried to resemble Chardonnay. The temptation today, with the market rewarding it, is to push for power: more ripeness, more alcohol, more Smaragd. But the lesson of 1985 is that Austrian wine was reborn when it stopped chasing what the market thought it wanted. The best Grüner is still the one that tastes of pepper, stone and the Danube, not the one that shouts loudest.
