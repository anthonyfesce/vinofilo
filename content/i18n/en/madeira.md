Every rule you learn about wine tells you to protect it: from heat, from light, from air. Madeira breaks every one of them and comes out the better for it. It is heated for months, aged under the eaves, oxidised by design, and once opened it can sit on the table for weeks without losing face. There are bottles that have passed two centuries in good condition. This is not a wine that survives mistreatment: it is a wine born of it.

## An island on the world’s shipping lanes

Madeira is a Portuguese island in the Atlantic, reached by the captain João Gonçalves Zarco in 1419 and settled soon afterwards. Within a few decades it was already exporting wine. Its fortune was its position: it was an obligatory port of call for ships bound for the Americas and the Indies. From the 17th century English merchants took control of much of the trade, and by the mid-18th century it had become customary to add grape spirit to the casks so that the wine would survive the voyage.

That was when the strangest effect came to light. Wine that crossed the tropics in the hold, slowly cooking for weeks, came back better than it left. So was born the legend of *vinho da roda*, the “round-trip wine”, and with it the idea of reproducing that heat on land. Italian readers will think at once of Marsala: when the English merchant John Woodhouse arrived in Sicily in the 18th century, he saw in the local wine a potential rival to Port, Sherry and Madeira, which his countrymen drank in vast quantities.

## Estufagem and canteiro, two ways of cooking time

Today the heat comes in two ways. *Estufagem* is the fast route: the wine is warmed in stainless-steel tanks with hot-water jackets, at 45 to 50 °C, for at least three months, then left to rest and cool slowly. The rules forbid bottling or selling it before 31 October of the second year after the harvest. It is the method behind most younger, more affordable Madeira.

*Canteiro* is the slow road. The wine ages in wooden casks, usually on the upper floors of the lodges, where temperatures can climb above 30 °C with high humidity. No artificial heating, just the island’s climate and time: at least two years in cask, and sale is allowed only after three years. Each year a little of the wine evaporates, and what remains grows more concentrated. Anyone who knows Tuscan Vin Santo, aged in small *caratelli* casks beneath the roof tiles, will recognise the idea: attic heat as a tool, not an enemy.

Either way the result is an oxidative, stable wine, with alcohol that as a rule sits between 17.5 and 21 degrees. That is why Madeira fears air so little: it has already come to terms with it.

## Four grapes, four degrees of sweetness

The traditional classification reads like a ladder. At the top is Sercial, grown in the highest, coolest sites on the north side: the driest and the most acidic. Then Verdelho, also mostly from the north, medium-dry. Boal, which the British call Bual, grows in the warmest spots on the south coast and gives medium-sweet wines with notes of raisin and caramel. At the bottom sits Malvasia, the Malmsey of British tradition: the sweetest, yet always held in balance by an acidity that is the island’s true signature. If the grape appears on the label, it must make up at least 85% of the wine.

Alongside these are rarities such as Terrantez and Bastardo, and above all Tinta Negra, a red grape that on its own accounts for some 80-85% of production. It is the great silent protagonist, long regarded as second-rate, and according to several sources promoted to the rank of recommended variety only in 2015.

Age statements complete the picture: 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40 and over 50 years for blended wines; Colheita for single-vintage wines aged at least five years; Frasqueira for those that spend at least twenty years in cask.

## A 19th century of disease, a 20th century in the kitchen

The story has not been a straight line. In the early 1850s oidium devastated the vineyards, and a few decades later phylloxera arrived. Many growers replanted with American varieties and hybrids, later banned from wine production in 1979. The 20th century dealt further blows: the Russian revolution and American Prohibition closed two major markets, and trade routes no longer needed to call at the island. Madeira ended up associated with cooking wine, the same fate that befell Marsala for decades. Today the island’s vineyards cover around 400 hectares: a tiny area for so large a name.

## The toast of 1776, between history and legend

Which leaves the American chapter. In the 18th century the North American colonies took an enormous share of the Madeira exported: estimates vary widely, from three-quarters to almost all of it, depending on the source. Some episodes are documented: in 1768 a dispute over duties on a cargo of Madeira that arrived on the Liberty, John Hancock’s ship, ended up in court, with John Adams as counsel; and a Philadelphia tavern bill from September 1787 lists 54 bottles of Madeira. George Washington, it is said, drank it every day at dinner.

The toast at the signing of the Declaration of Independence is another matter. You read it everywhere, often with precise details, but I have found no documentary source to confirm it: however plausible, it should be treated as tradition rather than established fact.

## A wine for the patient, and for the impatient

My view is that Madeira is the most democratic of the wines that cost time. The patient can seek out a Frasqueira and find a piece of the 19th century in the glass. The impatient can open a bottle, drink a glass and forget it in the cupboard for a few months without doing any harm. Few wines are so generous to those who treat them badly. That it remains a niche product around the world says more about our prejudices against “cooked” wine than about its quality.
