You can tour an entire appellation in an afternoon, at an easy pace, and still have time for a coffee in the square. That is what happens at Scanzorosciate, a few kilometres east of Bergamo, where Moscato di Scanzo is made on the first hills rising towards the Orobie Alps. It surprises first-time drinkers for two reasons: because it is a red Moscato, and because it is sweet without being cloying. It is also, by extension, one of the smallest denominations in the country.

## A DOCG in a single commune

The first fact you meet, wherever you read about this wine, is its size. The production zone coincides with the territory of the single commune of Scanzorosciate, and the vineyard area given by the sources consulted is about 31 hectares. To put it in perspective, that is roughly the space of forty football pitches. It is often presented as Italy’s smallest DOCG; the claim is repeated widely, but declared areas change from year to year, and it is best taken as an indication of scale rather than an official ranking.

Its administrative history is recent. Moscato di Scanzo gained DOC status in 2002 and was promoted to DOCG in 2009. The same area is also home to the Valcalepio DOC, which covers the dry wines. DOCG status did not change the nature of the wine, but it gave it a name of its own and a precise boundary, which for so small a product counts for a great deal.

## The grape and the moonstone

The grape is a black-skinned Moscato, grown here for centuries and regarded as typical of the area. The comparison with the white Moscato of Canelli, the grape of Moscato d'Asti, only goes so far: the aroma belongs to the same family, but the colour, the structure and its fate in the cellar are quite another matter.

The soil is the other half of the story. Beneath the vines lies what the local dialect calls *sass de luna*, moonstone: a grey-blue marl that crumbles in the sun at the surface and, deeper down, forms very hard limestone nodules. The workable topsoil is thin, the vines must struggle for water and nourishment, and yields necessarily stay low. It is one of those cases where geology does half the work of the production rules.

## How a red passito is made

Moscato di Scanzo is a *passito*. After the harvest the grapes are left to dry, losing water and concentrating sugars and aromas. The rules require at least 21 days of drying, until the sugar in the must reaches at least 280 grams per litre. That is the sugar in the grapes before fermentation, not what remains in the bottle: part of it turns into alcohol, the rest provides the final sweetness.

The result, in the glass, is a deep ruby red that moves towards garnet with time, with aromas in which people usually find rose, red fruit in spirit, sweet spices and a note many describe as sage or aromatic herbs. The sweetness is held up by good freshness and light tannin, and it is this balance that sets it apart from many heavier sweet wines. Anyone wanting to place it in the national picture can start with our survey of Italian sweet wines, where Scanzo sits alongside passiti and botrytised wines of far greater fame.

At table the territory suggests the pairings: the cheeses of the Bergamo valleys, blue cheeses above all, dry biscuits and pastries, dark chocolate. It also works on its own after a meal, as a *vino da meditazione* in the most literal sense: a small glass and plenty of time.

## An old reputation, between documents and legend

Bergamo Moscato appears in records as early as the 14th century. In 1347 the jurist Alberico da Rosciate left a quantity of Moscato to a beneficiary in his will; and the 17th-century chronicles of the historian Donato Calvi relate that in 1398, during the feuding between factions, the Guelphs seized 42 cartloads of red Moscato from Scanzo. If the detail is true, it proves the wine was already worth stealing.

Better known, and harder to verify, is the 18th-century chapter. The story goes that the Bergamo-born architect Giacomo Quarenghi, who worked for many years at the Russian court and owned a house in Rosciate, took bottles of Scanzo as gifts to Catherine II and to English dignitaries, some of whom supposedly mistook it for a new kind of sherry. There is also the claim that in the 18th century it was the most expensive wine in the world, quoted in London. We found no solid documentary evidence for this last part, which should be taken as local legend: evocative, but entirely unproven.

## Small does not mean minor

Today wine is also a reason to stop in Scanzorosciate: the commune has promoted the Strada del Moscato di Scanzo, a wine route linking vineyards, cellars and local cooking, and some of the historic producers work in tiny numbers, in the order of a few thousand bottles a year.

My view is that Moscato di Scanzo is one of the most interesting, and least understood, cases in Italian wine. It is not an everyday wine and has no intention of becoming one. Precisely for that reason it risks being remembered only as a quiz-night curiosity: the smallest DOCG, the one with the red Moscato. That would be a shame. It is, rather, proof that a denomination can be minute and still have a precise identity, made of a grape, a stone and a hill. In an Italian wine world where DOCs often expand to take in as many hectares as possible, one that fits entirely within a single commune deserves to be defended exactly as it is.
