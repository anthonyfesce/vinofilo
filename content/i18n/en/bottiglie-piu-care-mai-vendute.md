On 13 October 2018, in a matter of minutes, a bottle of wine stopped being a drink. It was a 1945 Romanée-Conti, the auction house had estimated it at $32,000, and the hammer came down at $558,000, more than seventeen times that. Shortly afterwards a second, identical bottle went for $496,000. Nobody in that room was buying something to drink. They were buying a story, a rarity and, as we shall see, a certainty as well: that the bottle was genuine. Which is the most expensive commodity of all.

## The Romanée-Conti record

For anyone who does not move in Burgundian circles: Romanée-Conti is a single Grand Cru vineyard in Vosne-Romanée, planted to Pinot Noir and owned outright by the Domaine de la Romanée-Conti. A tiny plot that has sat for centuries at the very top of the Burgundy hierarchy, the vinous equivalent of a painting that will never come back on the market.

The two bottles sold by Sotheby’s in 2018 came from the private cellar of Robert Drouhin, patriarch of Maison Joseph Drouhin, so their provenance was about as hard to challenge as it gets. According to reports of the sale, some 600 bottles had been made in 1945. The previous record belonged to a three-litre bottle of Château Mouton Rothschild 1945, also sold by Sotheby’s, in 2007, for $310,000. This was no small step, then: in a single afternoon the record nearly doubled, and the figure still stands in Guinness World Records as the highest price ever paid at auction for a single bottle of wine.

What is interesting is not the number itself but the combination behind it. A mythical vintage, an unrivalled vineyard, a laughably small quantity and a clean chain of custody: take away any one of those and the bottle would have fetched a fraction.

## Screaming Eagle and the charity factor

The second name on the list comes from California and follows a different logic. Screaming Eagle is a small estate in Oakville, in the Napa Valley, founded in 1986 by Jean Phillips; its first commercial vintage was the 1992, released in 1995. In 2000, at the Napa Valley charity auction, a six-litre bottle of Screaming Eagle was knocked down for $500,000 to Chase and Susan Bailey. Most accounts give the vintage as 1992, although not every contemporary source specifies it.

Here the comparison with Romanée-Conti needs handling with care. A charity auction is not the secondary market: whoever raises the paddle is also paying for the cause, the visibility and the pleasure of winning in front of everyone. It is a genuine record, but it measures the generosity of an evening rather than the value of the wine. The same applies, on a different scale, to the Hospices de Beaune sale, where the prices of the most celebrated lots have always followed the rules of charity rather than those of the price list.

## Jefferson’s Lafite

Then there is the most famous bottle of all, which may never have been what it claimed to be. In 1985 Christie’s offered a bottle of Château Lafite 1787 engraved with the initials “Th.J.”, attributed to the cellar of Thomas Jefferson, the future American president and an ardent lover of French wine during the years he represented the United States in Paris. It was bought for $156,000 by Christopher Forbes, on behalf of his father Malcolm, the publisher. For years it was cited as the most expensive bottle in the world.

It had been put into circulation by Hardy Rodenstock, a German collector, who spoke of a hidden cache of bottles found in a house in Paris. The tale began to creak when the American collector Bill Koch, who had bought other bottles from the same series, had their provenance checked before exhibiting them in Boston in 2005. The Thomas Jefferson Foundation, which runs Monticello, Jefferson’s home, concluded that the link to Jefferson could not be substantiated. The affair became a book by Benjamin Wallace, *The Billionaire’s Vinegar*, and one of the most retold stories in collecting.

The most ironic detail is that nobody bought that bottle to drink it. Its value was always that of an object, and an object whose story does not hold up is worth only as much as someone is still willing to believe in it.

## The forger who filled the cellars

If Jefferson’s Lafite is a mystery, the Rudy Kurniawan case is a verdict. A young and lavishly generous collector, a fixture at the big American auctions of the early 2000s, Kurniawan was making fakes of great Bordeaux and Burgundy at home, blending cheaper wines and pasting on printed labels. Arrested in 2012, he was sentenced by a federal court in New York to ten years in prison, with $28.4 million in restitution to his victims and a $20 million forfeiture. Among the buyers he had duped was, once again, Bill Koch, who by then had become something of a full-time fake hunter.

The lesson of the Kurniawan case is brutal: the rarer a wine, the easier it is to forge, because almost nobody has tasted the original well enough to say that something is off. That is why genuine records, like the one in 2018, nearly always come from producers’ own cellars or from collections with a documented history.

## What about Italy?

Readers may wonder where Barolo, Brunello or the Super Tuscans sit in this ranking. The honest answer is that they are not at the very top of the auction tables: Italy’s great reds enjoy a solid and growing collectors’ market, but six-figure prices per bottle remain, for now, a matter for Burgundy, Bordeaux and a handful of Californian names. That is not necessarily bad news.

## The price of certainty

My view is that these figures say little about wine and a great deal about us. A 1945 Romanée-Conti might be extraordinary or tired, and neither buyer paid to find out. You pay for scarcity, you pay for the name, but above all you pay for the guarantee that what is inside really is what the label says, and that is precisely where the forgers made their millions. The most sensible record, on reflection, was set by whoever bought a bottle from a cellar nobody could doubt. Everything else is faith, and faith, at auction, comes at a very high price.
