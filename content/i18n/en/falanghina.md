In restaurants across Campania, and well beyond it these days, Falanghina has become a password. People order it the way they order sparkling water: without thinking, knowing that a fresh, citrusy white will arrive, easy with fried food and with pizza. That popularity is a blessing, because it saved a grape that was disappearing in the 1970s. But it is also a misunderstanding, because behind that name lie two landscapes, two histories and, according to ampelographic research, even two different Falanghinas.

## A soldier’s name

The most frequently cited etymology goes back to antiquity. The name supposedly derives from *falanga*, the wooden stake to which the Romans tied their vines in orderly rows, which reminded some of the spears of a phalanx on the march. It is a plausible origin but not a provable one, and best taken for what it is: a good story the grape has carried with it for centuries.

What we know about the 20th century is more solid. In the 1970s Falanghina was a marginal grape, grown here and there and often vinified alongside others, and it was in danger of vanishing from the vineyards. The turning point has a precise name: Leonardo Mustilli, an engineer who had founded his winery in 1960 at Sant'Agata de’ Goti, in the province of Benevento. In 1979 he bottled a varietal Falanghina, regarded as the first on record, made from a massal selection of the old local vines. From there the grape began its climb, until it became one of Italy’s most recognisable whites.

## Flegrea and Beneventana

What almost nobody mentions at the table is that there is more than one Falanghina. Studies have identified two main biotypes: Falanghina Flegrea, tied to the volcanic area west of Naples, and Falanghina Beneventana, widespread inland in Sannio. They look alike and share a name, but they are not identical, and growers in the Campi Flegrei tend to regard theirs as the original. A small parochial rivalry that, for once, has its roots in the vineyard.

What they have in common are a few traits that explain the success: thick skins, good disease resistance and, above all, the ability to hold on to acidity even in the warm climate of the South. That is what makes Falanghina so drinkable young, and what allows the best versions to age well: with a few years the colour deepens, the wine gains weight and saltiness, and the aromas move from fresh citrus to candied peel and dried herbs.

## The Campi Flegrei, vines among the craters

The Campi Flegrei, the “Phlegraean Fields”, are one of the strangest vineyard areas in Europe. A still-active caldera dotted with craters, where Monte Nuovo was born in a matter of days in the eruption of 1538, and where vines squeeze in between apartment blocks, warehouses and car dealerships on the outskirts of Naples. According to some observers, few big cities in the world have so many vines within their limits; the comparison most often made is with Vienna, though the claim is best treated with caution.

The Campi Flegrei DOC dates from 1994 and covers a small planted area, around a hundred hectares, shared among producers who are almost always family-sized. The soils are young and volcanic: ash, sand, crushed pumice and tuff, poor in organic matter but rich in minerals and able to hold moisture deep down through dry summers.

And this is where you meet its most precious peculiarity: *piede franco*, ungrafted vines. Phylloxera, the insect that destroyed Europe’s vineyards at the end of the 19th century and forced growers to graft their vines onto American roots, finds sandy volcanic soils a hostile place. Many Phlegraean growers can therefore plant vines on their own roots, without rootstock, as was done before the catastrophe. Whether ungrafted vines in themselves make better wine is an open question; what they certainly offer is a rare continuity, and wines that producers regard as a more direct expression of place. Flegrea Falanghina tends to be tauter and more saline, with that almost smoky note that volcanic soils seem to leave in so many southern whites.

## Sannio, where most of the bottles come from

If the Campi Flegrei are the romantic cradle, Sannio is the engine. The province of Benevento accounts, according to industry estimates, for about 80 per cent of Falanghina’s hectares, and much of its output passes through a few large co-operative wineries. The landscape here is one of inland hills, far from the sea, dominated by the Taburno massif, which rises above 1,300 metres and shapes the climate of many vineyards.

Since 2011 there has been a Falanghina del Sannio DOC, which requires at least 85 per cent Falanghina and provides for four subzones: Guardia Sanframondi (or Guardiolo), Sant'Agata dei Goti, Solopaca and Taburno. Besides the still white, the rules allow sparkling wine, including traditional-method, as well as late-harvest and passito. According to the most recent figures available, the denomination covers about 1,360 hectares, with average production of around 46,000 hectolitres. Numbers that explain why the Falanghina we come across most often in restaurants and on shelves is, nearly always, from Sannio.

Beneventana Falanghina is generally softer and fruitier, with notes of pear, yellow apple and flowers, and a freshness that remains its backbone. Wines from the higher, breezier sites of Taburno can show surprising energy, closer to the Phlegraean style than their reputation might suggest.

## More than a pizza white

It is easy to dismiss Falanghina as the friendly white to put on the table with a margherita or a plate of fried small fish, and in that role it works beautifully. But that would be like judging Fiano or Greco di Tufo, the great whites of Irpinia, only by their simplest versions. My view is that Falanghina pays precisely the price of its popularity: by dint of being the wine that never disappoints, it is rarely asked to astonish. It is worth asking. A Flegrea Falanghina from ungrafted vines, or a Taburno left a few years in the cellar, tells of a different Campania from the postcard one, more volcanic, saltier, more serious. And it shows that a grape saved by a whisker can now afford to be ambitious.
