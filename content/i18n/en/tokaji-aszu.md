Some wines are famous for their taste and some for the people who drank them. Tokaji Aszú has managed to be both. For three centuries it travelled to the tables of Europe’s courts, Versailles included, earned a place in the Hungarian national anthem and a nickname any press office would kill for. Yet its real legacy, for anyone who studies wine, is less romantic and more important: in this north-eastern corner of Hungary people decided, earlier than almost anywhere else, that some vineyards were worth more than others, and that this should be written down.

## A map before the others

The story is often told like this: Tokaj has the world’s first vineyard classification. It is half true, and the missing half is the interesting part. From the 1730s the vineyards of the area were divided into quality classes, three according to the most cited accounts, work later completed by the censuses of 1765 and 1772 and revised at the end of the century. Then, on 1 October 1737, a decree by the Habsburg emperor Charles VI delimited the region: only twenty-two localities could use the name Tokaji, new plantings needed a licence and vineyard and cellar had to follow precise rules.

For an Italian reader the comparison comes naturally. In 1716 a proclamation by the Grand Duke of Tuscany had already drawn the boundaries of Chianti, which is why Tokaj’s claim to be “the world’s first appellation” is contested. The difference is that Florence drew a perimeter, while in Tokaj they were also beginning to rank the individual vineyards, the *dűlő*, by quality. If you are looking for the ancestor of Burgundy’s crus and of Barolo’s named vineyard sites, this is where to look. Today the region counts 28 localities on the Hungarian side, plus a small extension in Slovakia, a legacy of the borders drawn in 1920, and since 2002 its cultural landscape has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## King of wines, with a pinch of salt

The nickname is well known: *vinum regum, rex vinorum*, the wine of kings and the king of wines. It is usually attributed to Louis XIV, and it is true that in 1703 Prince Francis II Rákóczi, lord of vast estates in the area, sent some bottles of his wine to Versailles. But whether the phrase really passed the Sun King’s lips is a matter of tradition, not documented fact. Best treat it as one treats a good dinner-party anecdote: with pleasure, and without swearing to it.

The chronology of the wine itself is on firmer ground. Legend has it that the first Aszú was made in 1630 by Szepsi Laczkó Máté, which is a handy date to remember. But the documents are older than the legend: a wine from *aszú* grapes appears in a dictionary completed in 1576, and a recently discovered inventory pushes it back five years, to 1571.

## Furmint, mould and baskets

The leading grape is Furmint, which covers about 60% of the vineyards, followed by Hárslevelű at around 30%; the rules also allow Yellow Muscat and a few minor varieties. Furmint has thin skins and very high acidity, two qualities that make it ideal for *Botrytis cinerea*, the noble rot that, in the humid autumns where the Bodrog meets the Tisza, dehydrates the berries and concentrates their sugars and acids.

The method, as we explained in our piece on noble rot, is unique. The botrytised berries are picked one by one, crushed into a paste and macerated in a must or a base wine. Traditionally sweetness was measured in *puttonyos*, the baskets holding about 25 kilos of aszú paste added to a 136-litre Gönc cask. Today the grade is based on analysis of the finished wine and the rules are stricter than they used to be: since 2013 an Aszú must correspond to at least the old five puttonyos. At the top of the scale sits Eszencia, the juice that runs from the berries under their own weight, usually with 500 to 700 grams of sugar per litre and alcohol so low, no more than 5-6%, that it is technically not even considered wine.

Between Aszú and dry wine there is also Szamorodni, a name meaning roughly “self-grown”: whole bunches picked with their varying share of botrytised berries. The result is sweet or dry depending on the vintage, one of the most honest ways of telling the story of an autumn.

## The short century and dry Tokaji

The 20th century was not kind. Under communism the quality and reputation of Tokaji declined, and the wine of kings became largely a mass product. After 1990 came substantial investment, Hungarian and foreign, and what locals call the Tokaj renaissance: today there are some six hundred wineries, around fifty of which make the full range.

The most interesting development, however, is not sweet. Dry Furmint is a recent phenomenon: interest took off after a Mád producer, István Szepsy, presented a dry single-vineyard Furmint from the 2000 vintage, followed from 2003 by other growers in the village. It is a taut white, often with considerable structure, that lets you read the dűlő without the filter of sweetness. In a sense it is the most direct tribute to that old classification.

A final note for Italian readers: the name Tokaji lies behind the long dispute that forced Friuli to give up “Tocai” for its white wine, now called Friulano. It is a story we have told elsewhere, and it says a great deal about the value of a name.

## More than a dessert wine

My view is that Tokaji still pays for its reputation as an end-of-meal wine, for small glasses and rare occasions. That is a pity, because the region holds something rarer than sweetness: three centuries of written memory about its vineyards. Dry Tokaji is not a betrayal of Aszú; it is the acid test. If the vineyards classified in the 18th century really were better, it should show even without the sugar.
