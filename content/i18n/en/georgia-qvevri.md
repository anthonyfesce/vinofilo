Every history of wine needs a “before”. For the Romans it was Greece, for the Greeks it was the East, and for us Italians the Etruscans usually do the job. But if you go looking for the real first chapter, the one written in clay rather than in books, the road leads to a small country wedged between the Caucasus and the Black Sea. Georgia is not merely a place where wine has been made for a very long time. It is the place where, as far as we know, wine begins.

## Eight thousand years in a jar

In November 2017 the journal *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* published a study led by Patrick McGovern, the archaeologist at the University of Pennsylvania’s Penn Museum who has spent decades chasing the chemical traces of ancient drinks. His team had analysed pottery fragments from two Neolithic villages, Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora, in the Kvemo Kartli region, some 50 kilometres south of Tbilisi.

Inside those sherds was tartaric acid, the chemical signature of grapes and wine, along with malic, succinic and citric acids. The dating places them between 6000 and 5000 BC. Until then the record had belonged to an Iranian site, Hajji Firuz Tepe, a few centuries younger. Nor were the jars small: some could hold more than 300 litres. People making wine in those quantities, the authors argued, are not gathering wild grapes by chance; they are probably already growing a domesticated vine.

A word of caution is in order. “Eight thousand years” is a neat, convenient figure, made for a label, and archaeology never truly closes the books: tomorrow another dig could move the starting line elsewhere. But today, the oldest chemically documented evidence of winemaking is this.

## The qvevri, a cellar underground

The vessel that sets Georgia apart from everyone else is the qvevri: a large, egg-shaped earthenware pot, with no feet or handles, that lives not in the cellar but beneath its floor. It is buried up to its neck in the *marani*, the traditional wine cellar, and the surrounding earth acts as insulation, keeping the temperature steady without any equipment. Sizes range from a few hundred litres to several thousand.

The method, in its classic form, is almost brutally simple. After pressing, the juice goes into the qvevri together with the skins, stalks and pips. The vessel is sealed and the wine stays there, fermenting and maturing for five or six months before it is drunk. No barrels, no constant racking, no technology that did not already exist thousands of years ago.

In 2013 UNESCO inscribed the “ancient traditional Georgian winemaking method using the qvevri” on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The point of the listing is not only the technique: it is that the knowledge is handed down within families, among neighbours and relatives who harvest together, and that children learn by watching their elders collect the clay, shape the vessels and fire them. In the Georgian home, the nomination file notes, the wine cellar is considered the holiest place.

For an Italian reader the closest comparison is perhaps the terracotta *anfora*, which has made a comeback in many cellars across the peninsula in recent years. But the modern amphora is nearly always a stylistic choice, one vessel among others alongside steel and wood. In Georgia, the qvevri is the norm that the rest of the world has rediscovered.

## Saperavi and Rkatsiteli, the two names to learn

Georgia lays claim to some 500 indigenous varieties, a heritage as dense as Italy’s, but two are enough to get your bearings.

Saperavi is the benchmark red, and it has a rare quirk: it is a teinturier, a grape with coloured flesh, not just coloured skin. The name is usually translated as “dye” or “paint”, and one sip, or one stain on the tablecloth, explains why. It makes dark, dense wines with marked acidity, which also suits them to ageing.

Rkatsiteli is the great white of the tradition and one of the stars of what Georgians call “amber” wines: white grapes fermented at length on their skins, yielding a colour that runs from deep gold to orange and a structure that is almost tannic. This is the family the West knows as orange wine, and we have told the story of its Friulian branch, between Oslavia and the Carso, in our piece on orange wines.

Both are tied above all to Kakheti, the great eastern region that accounts for roughly three quarters of Georgia’s vineyards. But Georgia is no monolith: to the west, in Imereti, tradition calls for shorter skin contact, and the amber wines come out lighter and more delicate than those of Kakheti.

## Why it matters to Barolo drinkers too

It would be easy to file all this away as a curiosity for archaeology buffs. That would be a mistake. Georgia reminds the wine world of something marketing tends to forget: many of the “innovations” of the past twenty years, from skin-contact whites to fermentation in clay to the rejection of new oak, are in fact homecomings.

There is a subtler lesson, too. In Georgia wine has never been separated from the table, the family, songs and celebrations. That is exactly the kind of wine culture Italy claims as its own, with its *osterie* and its long Sunday lunches. Acknowledging that someone practised it before us, and with remarkable continuity, takes nothing away from Piedmont or Tuscany.

## The future of the oldest wine

The risk for Georgia is turning into a museum: a country the world visits for its history, applauds for its UNESCO listing and then forgets on the shelf. I think its real interest lies elsewhere. A well-made Saperavi or an amber Rkatsiteli is not an artefact; these are wines that talk easily with today’s cooking and with the curiosity of anyone tired of the same three international grapes. Eight thousand years make an excellent calling card. But a calling card, on its own, does not fill a glass: Georgia deserves to be drunk, not just admired.
