Baccalà is the most Italian of fish that never swims in Italian waters. It comes from the North Atlantic, travels salted or dried, and once in the kitchen becomes twenty different dishes, one per city. That is precisely why the question “what wine goes with it?” has no single answer. It depends less on the fish than on what is done around it: the milk, the tomato, the frying oil.

## First, a matter of names

*Baccalà* and *stoccafisso* come from the same animal, Atlantic cod. The difference lies in the preserving: baccalà is salt cod, stoccafisso is stockfish, dried in the open air, traditionally on the wooden racks of Norway's Lofoten islands. The first is soaked to draw out the salt, the second to give it back its water, which takes several more days.

To complicate matters there is the Veneto, where “bacalà”, with a single c, is the name for what the rest of Italy calls stockfish. Tradition traces it to the Venetian merchant Pietro Querini, who left Crete in 1431 with a cargo of Malvasia and was shipwrecked the following year on Røst, the outermost of the Lofoten islands, where he discovered fish dried by the wind. Whether he really brought it back to Venice is a story to take with a smile, but the bond between the Veneto and Norway is real to this day.

For wine the distinction matters: stockfish has a more fibrous texture and a more intense flavour, almost nutty, while salt cod is softer and more savoury. The former tolerates wines with a little more structure; the latter mostly asks for freshness.

## Whipped: bubbles and a small glass

Venetian *baccalà mantecato*, despite its name, is made with stockfish: poached, flaked and beaten at length with oil until it becomes a white, airy cream, to be spread on grilled polenta or bread. It is the king of the *cicchetti* in the *bàcari*, the Venetian bars where you eat standing up with an *ombra*, a small glass of wine.

What is needed here is a wine that cleans the oil from the palate without masking the delicate flavour of the fish. The most natural choice is a dry local sparkler: a Prosecco Brut, or better still a Prosecco col fondo, refermented in bottle, more rustic and savoury. A young, unoaked Veneto white works very well too, such as a Soave or a Lugana from the latest vintage. Avoid aromatic whites, which swallow the fish.

## Fried: acidity above all

Salt cod fried in batter is one of Italy's most widespread dishes, from the fillets of Roman fry shops to Neapolitan Christmas Eve tables. Here the problem is not the fish but the oil, and the rule that applies to all fried food holds: acidity and bubbles.

A dry traditional-method sparkling wine, Franciacorta, Trento DOC or Alta Langa, is the safest answer, because the fizz and acidity scrub the palate with every sip. Those who prefer a still wine can stay local: in Rome a young Frascati Superiore, in Naples a Falanghina or an Asprinio di Aversa, a Campanian white famous for its acidity and often lightly sparkling too. A pale lager would not be heresy, but this is a wine magazine.

## Livorno-style and with tomato: red is allowed

When the cod ends up stewed in tomato, as in the Livorno version with garlic and parsley, the game changes. The sauce brings acidity and sweetness, and a light white risks vanishing. This is the territory of rosés and young, low-tannin reds, served cool.

In Tuscany a coastal rosé comes naturally, or a light red from Sangiovese or Ciliegiolo, drunk young and at 14-15 °C. The tannin must stay low: in contact with fish and salt, a structured red tends to leave a metallic, bitter sensation. The same reasoning applies to stockfish Messina-style, *pesce stocco* stewed with tomato, olives, capers and potatoes: with this dish an Etna Rosato or a Frappato di Vittoria, a pale, fragrant Sicilian red, does a better job than many whites.

For the tomato stews of central and southern Italy, and for the eel that keeps them company on Christmas Eve, we refer you to our dedicated guide to the seafood feast: here it is enough to remember that tomato opens the door to red, provided it is light.

## Vicenza-style: structure without tannin

*Bacalà alla vicentina*, which is stockfish slow-cooked with onion, anchovies, milk, oil and grated cheese, is a dish of long cooking and great persistence. The Confraternita del Bacalà alla Vicentina, founded in Sandrigo in 1987, guards its codified recipe, and at the village festival in September the drinks are Vespaiolo from Breganze and Durello.

We have already covered it in our pieces on polenta and on Christmas Eve, so we will not repeat ourselves. The principle, in short: full-bodied whites with good acidity, no big reds. Amarone, for all that it lives next door, is the temptation to resist.

## The Christmas Eve table

On 24 December, in many Italian homes, baccalà appears two or three times in the same evening: whipped as a starter, fried, stewed. There is no need for a bottle per dish. A dry sparkler for the start and the fried course, a structured white for the milk-based preparations, a rosé or light red for the stews: between them they cover almost every combination. Temperatures do the rest: sparklers well chilled, whites at 10-12 °C, light reds just cooled.

My view is that baccalà is one of the best teachers of food and wine pairing we have. It shows that wine is chosen not on the main ingredient but on what surrounds it, and that the rule “fish means white” is a starting point, not a law. A humble dish, born to last months in a ship's hold, that at the table makes you think harder than many rich ones: that is no small thing.
