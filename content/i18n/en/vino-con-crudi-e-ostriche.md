The oyster is the simplest dish in the world and the most ruthless with wine. There is no sauce to hide a mistake, no cooking to soften a clash: just a raw mollusc, a little seawater and a glass. In the festive season, when platters of *crudo* reach the table alongside the bubbles, it pays to know why some wines work and others suddenly taste metallic, flat or bitter. The rules are few, and almost all of them have to do with salt.

## The rules of salt and iodine

At the table, salt is more of an ally to wine than people think: it softens the perception of bitterness and makes wines seem rounder and smoother. With an oyster, though, there is a great deal of salt, and it comes with that marine flavour we conveniently call iodine. A wine low in acidity tastes watered down beside it. Hence the first rule, a simple one: **high acidity**, which gets the saliva flowing again and cleans the mouth like a squeeze of lemon.

The second rule concerns what must not be there. New oak and tannin are the age-old enemies of raw seafood. This is not merely a matter of taste: as we explained when writing about fish, a Japanese study of 2009 pointed to the iron found in certain wines as the main culprit behind that stale, fishy aftertaste that appears in ill-judged pairings. With something as delicate as an oyster or a raw prawn, the risk is not worth taking.

The third rule is restraint. High alcohol and very exuberant aromas, those of aromatic grapes or very ripe whites, smother the discreet sweetness of the flesh. And noticeable residual sugar, next to salt, turns cloying. The ideal wine for raw seafood is dry, taut, savoury and fairly reserved on the nose. One last practical note: mignonette, the vinegar-and-shallot dressing of French tradition, is yet more acidity added to that of the wine. It is perfectly fine, but with an already razor-sharp wine a drop of lemon, or nothing at all, is better.

## Traditional-method pas dosé

If you have to open a single bottle for a whole platter of raw seafood, the safest answer is a traditional-method sparkling wine with no dosage. *Pas dosé*, *dosaggio zero* or brut nature, depending on the label: it means that no sugar is added after disgorgement, and that the residual sugar stays between 0 and 3 grams per litre. An extra brut, up to 6 grams, does a similar job with a touch more softness.

Italy offers a wide and serious range here: Franciacorta, Trento DOC, Alta Langa, Oltrepò Pavese metodo classico. They work for three reasons: the acidity, which stands up to the salt; the bubbles, which clear the palate of the fat in richer flesh such as raw red prawns or tuna tartare; and those notes of bread crust and yeast that come from time on the lees and sit well with the sweetness of shellfish. With fatter, meatier oysters, a pas dosé with a few more years of ageing also has the structure not to disappear.

## Verdicchio and Vermentino, the Adriatic and the Tyrrhenian

Among Italy’s still whites, two grapes seem made for the job. Verdicchio, from the Marche, has lively acidity, marked savouriness and that faintly almondy, slightly bitter finish which offsets the sweetness of raw seafood. In the Castelli di Jesi it leans towards a certain fullness; in Matelica, an inland valley with a more continental climate, it is drier and more vertical. For oysters and the most delicate raw fish, choose a young Verdicchio from the latest vintage; for langoustines and prawns, which have more flesh and more sweetness, a bottle with a year or two behind it holds up well too.

Vermentino is the white of the Tyrrhenian coast: Liguria, coastal Tuscany, Sardinia. In Gallura, home to the island’s only DOCG, it grows on granite soils and gives savoury wines with notes of Mediterranean herbs and citrus. It is softer and more perfumed than Verdicchio, and so it suits raw fish, amberjack, sea bream, dentex, better than the most iodine-rich oysters. A useful rule: the more the *crudo* is dressed, with oil, citrus, herbs, the more at home Vermentino feels.

## Chablis and Muscadet, the French classics

Then there are the two wines the French regard as the obvious answer, and which are worth knowing on this side of the Alps too. Chablis is 100 per cent Chardonnay from the far north of Burgundy, and it is the Chardonnay furthest from the idea of a buttery, vanilla-scented white: basic Chablis and Petit Chablis rarely see oak. The poetic coincidence lies in the soil. The Premiers and Grands Crus grow on Kimmeridgian marls, from a geological age some 150 million years ago, and halfway up the slope there is a layer rich in *Exogyra virgula*, tiny fossil oysters. That the taste of those shells reaches the glass is a romantic notion, not a fact. But the pairing works all the same, thanks to acidity and leanness.

Muscadet is more radical still. It comes from the Atlantic end of the Loire, around Nantes, and is made almost entirely from Melon de Bourgogne, a neutral, acidic grape picked early precisely to keep its freshness. The rules even impose a ceiling on alcohol, 12%, a unique case among unfortified French wines. The *sur lie* versions, allowed only in the sub-appellations such as Sèvre et Maine, rest on their fine lees at least through the whole winter and may not be bottled before the third week of March following the harvest: this way they gain a little texture. Lightness, salt, acidity: it is hard to imagine a wine closer to the sea.

## What to leave in the cellar

The list of mistakes is fairly predictable. Whites aged in new oak, which bring out bitter, almost metallic notes alongside an oyster. Tannic reds, although with very fatty raw tuna a dry rosé or a light, low-tannin red, served cool, can work. Sweet wines and exuberant aromatics, which drown everything. And, to stay with the festive theme, Prosecco Extra Dry, sweeter than a Brut despite its name, which loses all its grace next to salt.

## Our view

With oysters, the wine is not there to show off: it has to vanish at the right moment, leaving the mouth clean for the next one. That is why the best wines for them are often the ones that, drunk on their own, seem almost too lean. Chablis and Muscadet are deserved classics, but anyone living in Italy need not cross the Alps: a well-made pas dosé or a young Verdicchio does the same job with a local accent. The real luxury with raw seafood is not the rare bottle. It is acidity.
