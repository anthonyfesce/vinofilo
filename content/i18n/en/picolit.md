Some grape varieties produce too much and have to be reined in. Picolit is the opposite case: the vine does the sorting itself, before the grower even gets a look in. Look at one of its bunches in September and it seems unfinished, as if someone had gone along plucking off two berries in every three. From this natural parsimony came one of Italy's most celebrated sweet wines, and one of its most misunderstood.

Picolit has already made a fleeting appearance on these pages, in our tour of Italy's sweet wines. Here it is worth pausing, to understand why so rare a wine enjoyed, for two centuries, a reputation far larger than its output.

## The flaw that became a style

The name almost certainly comes from *piccolo*, small, and it is no affectation. Picolit suffers from what viticulturists call floral abortion: at flowering a good share of the flowers fail to set, partly because of a degree of pollen sterility, and simply drop. What remains on the bunch is a few berries, often a few dozen, widely spaced. In Friuli the phenomenon is called *acinellatura*, and for any other grape it would be a misfortune.

For Picolit it is the signature. The surviving berries, with more room and less competition, ripen into concentrated sugar and aroma, and the loose bunch breathes and falls ill less often. The price is quantity: a Picolit vineyard yields little by nature, and the production rules, as we shall see, leave no room for anyone tempted to push it.

Therein lies the paradox. A variety that all but refuses to bear fruit built its fame precisely on that refusal. Nobody ever had to explain to customers why Picolit was expensive: showing them the bunch was enough.

## The count and the courts

The modern legend of Picolit has a name. Count Fabio Asquini, born in 1726 and died in 1818, was a Friulian agronomist with an Enlightenment turn of mind: he busied himself with mulberries and silkworms, with rural economics, with making the land pay. And he made Picolit, which he sold across Italy and Europe in small bottles of green blown glass.

The sources say the wine reached the pope's table in Rome and that the Emperor of Austria judged it finer than any other. Generous figures also circulate for the number of bottles shipped each year to the courts of Europe, but they are hard to verify and best treated with caution. What can be said with confidence is that in the 18th century Picolit was a wine for diplomatic gifts, Friuli's answer to what Tokaji was for Hungary: sweet, rare, exportable, and good for getting noticed.

Then came the 19th century, with vine diseases and phylloxera, and so unproductive a variety was the first candidate for abandonment. Picolit came close to vanishing. According to the most widely repeated accounts, the revival began in the early 20th century at the Rocca Bernarda estate, on the initiative of the Perusini family, and the wine only came back into fashion in the second half of the century.

## One DOCG, not two

A clarification is needed, because the confusion is common. Friuli has two sweet DOCG wines and they remain separate. Ramandolo, a DOCG since 2001, is made from Verduzzo Friulano in the hills of Nimis and Tarcento and is another story, more rustic and tannic. Picolit has its own denomination, Colli Orientali del Friuli Picolit: a DOC since 1970, a DOCG since 2006.

The rules say a great deal about the wine. Picolit must make up at least 85%, with the balance from other local white grapes, aromatic varieties such as Traminer excluded. The maximum yield is 4 tonnes of grapes per hectare and the wine yield may not exceed 55%, that is, 22 hectolitres per hectare. The grapes may be dried on the vine, on racks, in crates or in rooms with controlled temperature and humidity. The finished wine must reach at least 15% total alcohol and may not be sold before 1 September of the year after the harvest.

The zone covers 19 communes in the centre-east of the province of Udine, from Cividale to Buttrio, from Corno di Rosazzo to Prepotto. And it is in Prepotto, on the border with Cividale, that the strictest subzone lies, Cialla: 100% Picolit, at least 16% total alcohol, no release before the second year after the harvest. Only Cialla may use the term Riserva, after at least four years of ageing.

## How to drink it, and with what

Picolit is not a syrupy wine. At its best it has a measured sweetness, scents of yellow flowers, dried apricot and acacia honey, sometimes a note of candied fruit when the drying has been long. Its historic weakness is acidity: it does not have a great deal, and a very concentrated Picolit risks turning heavy by the second sip. That is why the most careful producers work on balance rather than power.

For anyone opening a bottle at home, at the end of a meal, the rule for sweet wines applies: the dessert must be less sweet than the wine. Picolit suffers next to custards and chocolate puddings, but is happy with dry biscuits and pastries, with blue or aged cheeses and with *gubana*, the leavened pastry filled with walnuts, raisins and pine nuts from the Natisone valleys, born a few kilometres from its vineyards. Served cool, around 10-12 °C, it keeps the tension it loses when warm. And an opened bottle, well stoppered, keeps for a few days in the fridge better than a dry white, thanks to its sugar and alcohol.

A practical tip for those who live in Italy: the best way to discover it is still a weekend in Friuli, between Cividale and the Colli Orientali, drinking it where it is born, alongside the dry whites of the same area. It is the simplest way to grasp that Picolit is not an isolated oddity but the sweet side of a territory that makes great white wines.

## More cited than drunk

Estimates of the planted area speak of a few dozen hectares, a figure not confirmed by official data and best taken as a rough order of magnitude. It is enough, in any case, to explain why Picolit is so often named and so rarely drunk.

My view is that its problem today is not rarity but reverence. It is treated as a relic to be opened once a year, and so it remains a name in books rather than a wine in the glass. Picolit deserves the opposite: to be drunk without solemnity, with a piece of cheese or a slice of gubana, by people who have never heard of Count Asquini. The courts of the 18th century have celebrated it enough. Now it is our turn.
