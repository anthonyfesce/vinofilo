There comes a moment on the evening of 31 December when even those who have spent the whole dinner saying “just a little for me” find themselves facing a plate of cotechino and lentils. Not out of hunger: out of superstition. It is the only Italian dish eaten chiefly as a contract with good luck, and precisely for that reason nobody stops to ask what wine should go with it. People open whatever is to hand. A pity, because cotechino is an easy match to get right and just as easy to ruin.

## A sausage, a trotter and a legend

Cotechino and zampone are siblings: the same mixture of pork, fat and rind, in a different casing. Cotechino is stuffed into a gut casing, zampone into a hollowed-out pig’s front trotter, skin and even toenails included. Both are protected as Protected Geographical Indications, as Cotechino Modena and Zampone Modena, and the difference on the plate is not merely cosmetic: zampone, with all that outer rind, is more gelatinous and sticks to the palate, while cotechino is firmer and more spiced.

On their origins, the Modena consortia are happy to tell a story: the siege of Mirandola in 1510–1511, the troops of Pope Julius II at the gates, the besieged townsfolk slaughtering their pigs so as not to leave them to the enemy and preserving the meat inside the trotters. It is a fine story and, precisely, a story: there are no documents from the period to support it. The first written traces come later, a price list of 1745, the verses the Modenese poet Tigrinto Bistonio devoted to cotechino in 1761, and the description of how to make it in Vincenzo Agnoletti’s cookery manual of 1841. A sausage does not need a pope to be ennobled: three centuries of Emilian tables will do.

Outside Emilia the ritual has adapted. In the Veneto and Friuli Venezia Giulia the *musetto* rules, made with meat from the snout as well, and it often arrives with polenta rather than lentils. The principle does not change: plenty of fat, plenty of rind, plenty of substance.

## Why lentils, of all things

Lentils bring money, as every Italian knows. Less clear is since when. The most repeated explanation points to ancient Rome, where at the end of the year people supposedly exchanged little bags of lentils as a wish for prosperity, in the hope they would turn into coins. Many tell the tale, but nobody cites a Latin author who actually says so: let us take it as folk tradition, not historical fact. What is certain is the shape, flat and round like a tiny coin, and the fact that a dried pulse that swells as it cooks makes a fairly intuitive symbol of abundance.

For the wine, though, the lentils matter more than their meaning. They are earthy, sweetish, often cooked with a *soffritto* and sometimes a little tomato, and above all they soak up the fat of the cotechino like a sponge. The dish as a whole is less heavy than it looks, but it is still dominated by three things: fat, salt and spice. They are what decide what to pour.

## Lambrusco solved the problem long ago

We mentioned it in passing when writing about bollito misto, but here it deserves saying with more conviction: with cotechino, dry Lambrusco is the answer the Emilians found centuries ago, and not out of local pride. The bubbles and the acidity scrub the palate, the dark fruit gets on well with the spices, and the tannin is low or at least manageable. The fat is swept away with every sip and the next mouthful tastes like the first.

It is worth choosing the right Lambrusco for the right dish, however. Sorbara, pale, almost rosé, with the highest acidity in the family, is perfect with thinly sliced cotechino and lighter lentils. Grasparossa di Castelvetro, dark and with tannin you can feel, stands up better to zampone, gelatinous and richer. Salamino di Santa Croce sits in the middle and keeps everyone happy. The one caveat: it must be dry. An *amabile*, off-dry Lambrusco turns the salt of the cotechino cloying.

## Barbera, Bonarda and those who don’t want the froth

Not everyone likes fizzy red, and there is no need to convert anybody. Those who want a still red have an excellent alternative in young Barbera, the unoaked kind or one with a measured spell in wood: gentle tannin, high acidity, cherry fruit. It does the same job on the fat as Lambrusco, just without the bubbles. A Barbera d'Asti or del Monferrato from the latest vintage, served lightly cool at around 14–16 degrees, is probably the still red best suited to this table.

Along the same lines comes Bonarda dell'Oltrepò Pavese in its *vivace*, gently sparkling version, halfway between the two worlds: a little froth, generous fruit, a cleansing acidity. Best avoided, on the other hand, are the big reds, very tannic and very alcoholic. A Barolo or an Amarone with cotechino does not disappoint for lack of quality, but because it clashes with the fat, makes the tannin rough and weighs down a dinner that by then is already long.

## The midnight fizz

Then there is the most common case of all: cotechino arriving after midnight, when the glasses are already full of sparkling wine. Good news: a dry traditional-method sparkler, a Franciacorta, a Trento DOC or an Alta Langa in brut or extra brut, works beautifully with cotechino, for the same reasons as Lambrusco. Acidity and carbon dioxide do their job. For an even more precise match, a traditional-method rosé or a blanc de noirs, made from black grapes, has a little more body and fruit and copes better with the spices.

Watch the labels, though. A Prosecco Extra Dry is, against all linguistic logic, sweeter than a Brut, and that residual sugar is no friend to the salt in cotechino. And watch the order of the courses: the brut that gets on with cotechino quarrels with panettone, as we have written before. Savoury first with the dry sparkling wine, then change bottles for the sweet.

## Our view

Cotechino is a humble, fatty, cheerful dish, and it asks for a wine with the same qualities. Dry Lambrusco remains the most fitting choice, because it comes from the same plain and the same idea of the table, but a young Barbera or a dry sparkling wine do an equally honest job. The only real mistake is to treat it as a course for a grand red. On New Year’s Eve luck is not bought with the most expensive bottle in the cellar: it is bought, if it can be bought at all, with a plate of lentils and a wine that wants to be drunk.
