Some French regions seem designed to discourage the outsider: Burgundy, with its climats and its endlessly repeated surnames, or Bordeaux, where the grape barely makes it onto the label. Then there is Alsace, which for decades has done the simplest and, by French standards, most heretical thing of all: it writes the name of the grape on the bottle. Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris, Muscat. It looks like a marketing nicety, but it is the product of a long and tangled history, one that only makes sense with a map in one hand and a history book in the other.

## A ribbon of hills between two countries

Wine Alsace is a narrow strip along the lower eastern slopes of the Vosges, with vineyards climbing roughly from 175 to 420 metres and looking out over the Rhine plain. Across the river lies Germany. The mountains behind shield the vines from much of the rain blowing in from the west, and the result is a climate surprisingly dry and sunny for this latitude: Colmar gets around 600 millimetres of rain a year. That pairing of northern latitude and sunshine explains white wines that are ripe and perfumed yet keep a clean line of acidity. Around 90% of production is white; the rest comes mostly from Pinot Noir and from Crémant d'Alsace, the region’s bottle-fermented sparkling wine.

For an Italian reader the obvious comparison is Alto Adige, the German-speaking corner of the Italian Alps also known as Südtirol: two languages, the grape on the label, Gewürztraminer at home. The resemblance is no accident. The name Traminer is traditionally traced to Termeno, the Alto Adige village whose German name is Tramin, while the compound “Gewürz” form took hold later, in Alsace itself.

## Four noble grapes, and how to read them

According to 2008 figures, the most widely planted variety was Riesling (about 22% of the vineyard), followed by Gewurztraminer (about 19%) and Pinot Gris (about 15%). Muscat, the fourth of the grapes deemed noble, is far rarer.

Alsace Riesling is the drier, broader-shouldered sibling of the Mosel’s: less weightlessness, more frame, and a mineral edge that on the best soils turns almost stern. Gewurztraminer is its opposite, a burst of rose, lychee and spice that splits drinkers into zealots and sceptics, often with a touch of residual sugar. Pinot Gris is the same grape as Italy’s Pinot Grigio, but picked riper and made in a richer, golden, sometimes softer style: anyone who knows only the aperitivo Pinot Grigio of a thousand bar counters will struggle to recognise it. Muscat, finally, is almost always dry, and has the rare gift of tasting genuinely of fresh grapes without being sweet.

Sugar is precisely the sticking point. For years the same label could hide a bone-dry wine or a decidedly off-dry one, and the drinker found out only after pulling the cork. Since the 2021 vintage that has changed: still wines must state their sweetness, either with the terms sec, demi-sec, moelleux and doux or with a graphic scale on the label. It was a small revolution in transparency, and anyone buying Alsace from far away is its first beneficiary.

## A disputed history, and a bottle that never changes

Alsace has changed hands more than once. Annexed by the German Empire in 1871, it returned to France after the First World War and fell under German control again during the Second. For centuries its wine had travelled down the Rhine alongside German wine and was traded as such. Even after 1919, part of the German legislation stayed in force as local law, which, by several accounts, slowed the recognition of its appellations.

The turning point came after 1945. An ordinance that year laid the foundations of the regional rules, and the Alsace AOC was formalised by a decree in 1962. Ten years later, in 1972, a law decreed that appellation wine could circulate and be sold only in bottle, and that bottling had to take place within the two Alsace départements, Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin. The parliamentary papers of the day were blunt about the reason: to stop wine leaving in bulk to be sugared, watered down and blended across the border.

Then there is the bottle. The flûte d'Alsace, tall and tapering, is written into the appellation rules: spot one on a shelf and you know where you are before reading a word. Some find it old-fashioned, but it has a rare virtue: in a world of identical bottles, this one is a signature.

## The 51 grands crus and the late harvests

The Alsace Grand Cru appellation was created by a decree in 1975. In 1983 a further 25 lieux-dits, historically named vineyard sites, were added, more followed in later years, and the last, the Kaefferkopf in Ammerschwihr, was recognised in 2007, bringing the total to 51. In principle the grands crus are reserved for the four noble grapes, with exceptions: Zotzenberg has allowed Sylvaner since 2006, while some sites, such as Altenberg de Bergheim and the Kaefferkopf itself, permit blends in proportions set by the rules. Italian readers will recognise the mechanism from Barolo’s *menzioni geografiche aggiuntive*, the officially mapped vineyard names: a site name that becomes a guarantee, at least on paper.

The grand cru debate, it must be said, has never gone away. Fifty-one is a lot, and not every name carries the same weight among growers and critics. It is the tension built into any classification born of a decree: a vineyard boundary is drawn in a day, a reputation takes generations.

Finally, the sweet wines. The designations Vendanges Tardives and Sélection de Grains Nobles, introduced in 1984, denote grapes picked very ripe, in the latter case often touched by noble rot. The minimum must weights are high and vary by grape: lower for Riesling and Muscat, higher for Gewurztraminer and Pinot Gris. These are rare wines, granted by the vintage only when autumn plays along.

## Why Alsace is more modern than it looks

My view is that Alsace is still underrated for the wrong reason: it looks easy. The grape on the label, the instantly recognisable bottle, the picture-postcard villages. But behind that simplicity is a region that did, before most others, what everyone now preaches: say plainly what is in the bottle, tie the wine to a precise place, and fight off the anonymity of bulk wine. The next step, should it want to take it, is to convince the world that an Alsace grand cru deserves the same patience as a Burgundy. The wines, quite often, are already there. The audience, less so.
