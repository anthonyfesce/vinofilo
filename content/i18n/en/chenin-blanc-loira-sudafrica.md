If there were a prize for the most gifted and least celebrated grape in the world, Chenin Blanc would start as favourite. It can make dry whites as sharp as a blade, botrytised sweet wines that last a century, traditional-method sparkling wines and even bulk wine to drink without a second thought. Yet outside the circle of enthusiasts its name means little, squeezed between the fame of Chardonnay and that of Sauvignon. It is an injustice with precise historical causes, and one that in recent years has, at last, begun to ease.

## A Loire grape with a Friulian relative

Chenin is a Loire grape, and its roots there run very deep. Ampelographers place its birth in the valley, perhaps as early as the 9th century; Pierre Galet, one of the great scholars of the 20th century, pointed to Anjou as its cradle. The name, according to the most widely accepted account, comes from Mont-Chenin in Touraine, where between 1520 and 1535 the so-called Plant d’Anjou, in all likelihood the same variety, was planted. Rabelais, an admirer of Anjou’s white wines, mentions it in the 1530s, although it is not certain he meant the same grape.

The most intriguing part, for an Italian reader, is the genetics. DNA analysis has established that one of Chenin’s parents is Savagnin, the ancient grape of the Jura. In 2019 the French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot proposed Sauvignonasse as the second parent, a variety Italians know well under another name: Friulano, the former Tocai of the Collio and Colli Orientali. It is a hypothesis, based on the shape of leaves and bunches and cross-checked against molecular analysis, not yet a certainty. But the idea that the great white of the Loire might have a parent who speaks Friulian is enough to make one look at it differently.

## Vouvray, Savennières, Saumur: three faces of one grape

In France, Chenin lost ground in the second half of the 20th century: Loire plantings fell from about 16,600 hectares in 1958 to under 10,000 in 2008. But it remained master of the house in a handful of places that, on their own, justify its reputation.

Vouvray, just east of Tours, is the best-known appellation, an AOC since 1936, covering a little over 2,000 hectares across eight communes. Here Chenin grows over a subsoil of *tuffeau*, the soft, pale limestone into which cellars have been dug for centuries. Vouvray is the proof of the grape’s versatility: the same name on the label can mean a sec, demi-sec or moelleux wine, that is dry, off-dry or sweet, or a sparkling one. Indeed the bubbles, mousseux and pétillant together, outstrip the still wines in volume. It is both the great strength and the great weakness of the appellation: anyone who buys a Vouvray without reading the label carefully cannot be sure what will end up in the glass.

Savennières is the opposite: a tiny enclave on the right bank of the Loire, some fifteen kilometres south-west of Angers, with about 140 hectares in production on schist soils shot through with volcanic veins. Here Chenin is nearly always made dry, austere, often closed in youth, and it shows its best after a few years in bottle. Within it sit two appellations of their own, Roche aux Moines and Coulée de Serrant, the latter of about seven hectares, a monopole owned by a single family and one of the emblems of French biodynamic viticulture.

In Saumur, Chenin turns sparkling. White Saumur mousseux, made by the traditional method with at least nine months in bottle before disgorgement, must contain at least 60% Chenin, and here too the tuffeau provides underground cellars at a naturally steady temperature. All the grape’s acidity, which elsewhere serves to balance sugar, becomes backbone for the bubbles.

And then there are the sweet wines of Anjou, Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux, Quarts de Chaume, born of the noble rot Vinofilo has already written about. The finest, from kind vintages, can last a hundred years.

## South Africa, where it was called Steen

The paradox is that Chenin’s homeland by numbers is not France. It is South Africa, which in 2008 had almost 19,000 hectares of it, roughly twice the French total, and where it is still the most planted variety, with about 18% of the national vineyard according to figures cited in 2024. In the past the share reached 30%.

There, for some two centuries, Chenin went by another name, Steen, of obscure origin, and only in 1965 was it identified beyond doubt as the Loire grape. It may have arrived with the first Dutch settlers in the 17th century, or with French Huguenots after 1685: the sources do not agree. For most of its South African history it was a workhorse, planted on fertile soils that pushed yields to levels unthinkable in the Loire, and destined for simple wines in large volumes.

The turning point came with a generation of producers who rediscovered the old vineyards and began bottling them separately. In areas such as the Swartland, on the west coast, those vines have produced deep dry whites that have persuaded international critics that a surprising share of the world’s finest Chenin is now made at the Cape.

## Why it deserves more room

My view is that Chenin pays for its own talent. A grape that can do everything is hard to sum up in a line, and the market loves short lines. Yet that high acidity, which lets it move from dry to sweet to sparkling without losing its identity, makes it one of the most rewarding whites to follow over time. The Loire gave it nobility, South Africa gave it scale. To anyone who loves Italian whites that age, from Verdicchio to Fiano, Chenin should feel like a distant but familiar cousin: and perhaps, if Boursiquot is right, something closer still.
