Every Roman has an opinion about Frascati, and it is nearly always a memory. The day trip out of town, the long table under the pergola, the carafe that arrives without anyone having quite ordered it. It is an enormous store of affection and, at the same time, this wine’s main problem: for half a century Frascati was so familiar that nobody stopped to ask whether it was any good. Today some people are asking again, and the answer is more interesting than memory would suggest.

## A wine with 2,500 years of neighbourliness

Frascati lies some twenty kilometres from Rome, in the Alban Hills, north of Lake Albano. The soil is volcanic and free-draining, the offspring of the same ancient volcano that gave Lazio its round crater lakes. The vineyards climb from about 60 to 300 metres, and in the oldest cellars it is not unusual to find chambers dug out in Roman times.

Proximity to the capital made the wine’s fortune and also shaped its character. Excavations at ancient Tusculum, above present-day Frascati, show vines being grown for wine as early as the 5th century BC. Then came the villas of the Roman aristocracy, the Renaissance popes who spent their summers here, the travellers of the Grand Tour and finally the Rome of La Dolce Vita, which drank Frascati as one drinks the water at home. A city wine, in short, even though it was born in the hills: made for a thirsty metropolis, within reach of a cart.

## The fraschette, or the trattoria without a kitchen

The symbol of this relationship is the *fraschetta*. The name comes from the *frasca*, the green branch that growers hung outside the door to signal that new wine was for sale. Inside, traditionally, only wine was served: customers brought their own food, or bought it from the pork butcher next door. Today the fraschette of the Castelli, especially in and around Ariccia, offer porchetta, cured meats, cheeses and the odd hot dish, but the spirit is unchanged: long tables, greaseproof paper, wine on tap.

There is something precious in this popular liturgy, as anyone who lives in Rome knows. But this is exactly where the misunderstanding begins. If Frascati is the fraschetta wine, then it is the wine nobody judges, the one whose only job is to be cheap and to be there. And a wine whose only job is to be there sooner or later stops having an identity.

## How it got lost, and who tried to find it again

Frascati was among the very first Italian DOCs, by decree of 3 March 1966. As early as 1949 eighteen producers had founded a consortium to defend the name from counterfeiters, a sign that success brought its parasites with it. But the real threat did not come from outside. In the second half of the 20th century the denomination lived on volume: high yields, mass vinification, crushed prices, a neutral white good for everything and memorable for nothing. Accounts agree that it was those decades of quantity, more than any scandal, that ruined its reputation.

Then there was the pressure of Rome itself. The city that had made Frascati’s fortune kept growing towards the hills, and concrete did the rest. Anyone driving from Rome to Frascati today crosses an almost unbroken suburban sprawl, where vineyards appear in patches, like survivors.

The revival was led by a group of estates that decided to bet on quality, and it found institutional form on 20 September 2011, when two styles became DOCG: Frascati Superiore and Cannellino di Frascati. Frascati DOC remains as the entry-level wine.

## Superiore and Cannellino, the dry and the sweet

The rules for Superiore call for at least 70% Malvasia Bianca di Candia and/or Malvasia del Lazio, the variety known as Puntinata, which is the most interesting grape in the area: more aromatic, with an almost almond-like note and a certain richness. The rest may come from Bellone, Bombino Bianco, Greco and Trebbiano, with a little room for other Lazio white grapes. The most significant rules are in the vineyard: maximum yields of 11 tonnes per hectare against 14 for the DOC, at least 3,000 vines per hectare and, above all, a ban on *tendone* and pergola training, the overhead trellis systems that had made the big volumes possible. Minimum alcohol is 12%, rising to 13% for the Riserva, which must age for at least twelve months, three of them in bottle. The zone takes in the communes of Frascati, Grottaferrata and Monte Porzio Catone, plus parts of Rome and Montecompatri.

Cannellino is the other face, and a small rarity. It is a sweet white from late-harvested grapes, again mostly Malvasia, with a minimum of 35 grams per litre of residual sugar. It is the denomination’s sweet version, the one that has always sat alongside the dry white in the cellars of the Castelli. The area under vine is tiny: some sources speak of around ten declared hectares, a figure best taken as indicative. It is worth seeking out, because it tells of a Frascati that has nothing to do with the carafe.

## What to expect, and what to demand

A good Frascati Superiore today is a medium-bodied white, with aromas of white flowers and yellow fruit, a savoury streak that many link to the volcanic soils and a faintly bitter finish. It is not a white for long ageing in the classic sense, but the more carefully made versions hold up well for a few years, and the Riserva is designed for exactly that. Anyone wanting to know how it behaves at the table will find some pointers in our piece on carbonara, amatriciana and cacio e pepe; for the other half of Lazio, our article on Cesanese is the place to go.

What to demand, rather, is a label that says DOCG and a producer willing to put their name to the wine. The fraschetta’s wine on tap has its place, and nobody wants to take it away. But it should be seen for what it is: a convivial tradition, not the calling card of a denomination.

## The house white deserves better

My view is that Frascati has an advantage almost no other Italian wine enjoys: a market of four million people half an hour down the road, who know it by name. It is also its handicap, because those four million think they already know everything. The revival will not be won in London or New York but in the wine shops and restaurants of Rome, on the day a Roman orders a Frascati Superiore with the same curiosity with which they order an Etna Bianco. The wine, in a number of cases, is already there. It is up to the Romans to notice.
