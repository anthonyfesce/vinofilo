For most of the twentieth century Champagne had only one face: that of the great houses. Names written in gilded script, cellars carved into the chalk beneath Reims and Épernay, a style recognisable at the first sip and always true to itself. Those who tended the vines almost always stayed backstage, sold their grapes and signed nothing. Then a few growers began to do something simple and almost subversive: bottle their own wine and put their own name on it. That is where what English-speaking markets call grower Champagne was born, known in France, by preference, as Champagne de vignerons.

## Who grows and who sells

We explained it in our Champagne Day piece: the region lives by an unusual division of labour, in which the houses own a minority of the vineyards yet ship the great majority of bottles, while thousands of growers farm most of the land. The code next to the registration number on the label tells you who is behind the bottle: NM for a house that buys grapes and makes the wine, CM for a cooperative, RM for the *récoltant manipulant*, the grower who makes wine from his or her own grapes. Grower Champagne, strictly speaking, is the last of these.

The numbers help to measure the revolution, and to put it in proportion. According to the Comité Champagne, the region’s interprofessional body, 174.2 million bottles were shipped in the first nine months of 2026. The houses shipped 133 million of them, 76.3% of the total, up 5.1%. Growers stopped at 27.4 million, roughly one bottle in six, down 4%, while cooperatives accounted for 13.8 million. The houses are growing on the back of exports; growers are growing abroad but struggling in France, where their shipments fell by 7%. The revolution, in short, is cultural rather than commercial: it has changed the way people talk about Champagne far more than it has changed market shares.

## The grower of Avize

If one name sums up this story, it is Anselme Selosse. His family made Champagne in Avize, in the Côte des Blancs, the band of hills south of Épernay where Chardonnay reigns. He had studied in Beaune, in Burgundy, and that is where the key to his work lies. Joining the estate in the mid-1970s, he took the reins in 1980 and began applying to Champagne ideas borrowed from the great white Burgundies: fermentation in wooden barrels rather than steel, grapes picked at full ripeness rather than chasing acidity at all costs, and minimal or zero dosage, decided case by case.

The best-known symbol of his obstinacy is a perpetual reserve, modelled on the Sherry solera, set up in 1986 after some six years of lobbying the appellation’s authorities for permission. Each year he draws off part of it and tops it up with new wine from the same parcels. The first cuvée born of that system was released in 1996 under the name Origine, later renamed Substance. In 1994 the Gault Millau guide had named him France’s best winemaker, not merely Champagne’s: an accolade that made many realise a small producer could stand on equal terms with the great houses.

Selosse has never been a comfortable figure. His wines divide opinion, and some criticise their oxidative notes and richness. But the point is not whether everyone likes them: it is that they proved Champagne could be an *auteur* wine, tied to a village and to a person.

## Place versus blending

The heart of the debate is philosophical. The classic house model rests on blending: grapes from dozens of different villages, different varieties, reserve wines from different years, combined by the *chef de cave* to achieve the same style every year. It is a genuine art, and it makes sense in a region where a northern climate can make one vintage very different from the next. The result is a recognisable brand, not a place.

The grower reasons the other way round. He or she has their own vines, often in just a few communes, and the wine can only speak of those. Hence the focus on the single village, the single parcel, sometimes the single variety, and an idea of Champagne as a wine of terroir before it is a party drink.

Anyone who knows Italian wine will recognise the pattern. It is the same one that ran through the Langhe in Piedmont: for generations Barolo was mostly a blend of fruit from different hillsides, then some producers began bottling single crus, and today the *menzioni geografiche aggiuntive*, the officially mapped vineyard names, are the very grammar of the denomination. Grower Champagne is following a similar path, a few decades behind and with a great many more bubbles.

## The limits of the fairy tale

That said, it is wise not to turn the story into a fairy tale of heroes and villains. RM on the label is no guarantee of quality, just as NM is no badge of soulless industry: there are mediocre growers and great houses with extraordinary wines. A small producer has fewer reserve wines, fewer means to make up for a difficult vintage and more exposure to the market, as the 2026 figures show. And the trade as a whole is going through a delicate period, with yields held low so as not to fill the cellars.

What the growers have brought, however, endures. They have pushed even the great houses to talk about vineyards, villages, lower dosages and transparency on the label. They have made Champagne more interesting to understand, not merely to open.

Our view is that the small producers’ real conquest is not a market share but an idea: that Champagne is a wine like any other, the child of a place and of the people who farm it. For drinkers, the practical consequence is simple. That tiny code on the label is worth reading. Not to pick a side, but to know which story is about to be poured into the glass.
