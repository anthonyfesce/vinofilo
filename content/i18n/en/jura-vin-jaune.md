Some wines ask for patience; vin jaune demands it by law. Six years and three months between harvest and bottling, in casks nobody tops up, beneath a thin layer of yeast that acts as both blanket and filter. The result is one of the strangest and longest-lived white wines in the world, a wine that divides opinion like few others and that, once understood, is never forgotten. It comes from a region that even many wine lovers struggle to place on a map: the Jura.

## A border region, with character

The Jura is a strip of hills and mountains in eastern France, between Burgundy and Switzerland. It is small and secluded, with harsh winters and a farming tradition that, at the table, speaks mostly of cheese. For an Italian reader the closest comparison, in spirit more than in style, would be Italy’s own mountain wine areas, where wine long remained a local affair before becoming a cult for the most curious drinkers.

Jura wines have been protected by appellations since 1936. Among them are Arbois, Côtes du Jura, L'Étoile and the smallest and most legendary, Château-Chalon: about 50 hectares spread across four communes, Château-Chalon, Domblans, Menétru-le-Vignoble and Nevy-sur-Seille. At Château-Chalon, Savagnin is made only as vin jaune, and the appellation is granted only in vintages a panel judges worthy. In other years the wine goes out under another label. It is a kind of severity few appellations anywhere allow themselves.

## Savagnin, an ancestor with many relatives

The grape of vin jaune is Savagnin, a late-ripening white of the Traminer family. The name will prick up the ears of anyone who knows Alto Adige, Italy’s German-speaking north, where Traminer is at home and takes its name from the village of Termeno, Tramin in German. Savagnin, however, is far less aromatic than Gewürztraminer: no rose or lychee, rather a firm acidity and a structure built for ageing. It is usually picked at the end of October, aiming for a potential alcohol of between 13 and 15%.

Savagnin is also a distinguished ancestor. As we explained when writing about Chenin Blanc, DNA analysis identifies it as one of the parents of the Loire’s great white. A grape from a tiny region, then, with offspring that have travelled the world.

## Six years and three months under the veil

The heart of vin jaune is its ageing. The fermented wine goes into 228-litre casks that are not topped up: as it evaporates, a pocket of air forms above the liquid, and on the surface a film of yeast develops, which in the Jura they call the *voile*. It takes two or three years to form fully. The veil shields the wine from full oxidation, but not completely, and it is in this imperfect balance that the style is born.

The obvious comparison is with Sherry and its *flor*, which we wrote about for International Sherry Week. The differences matter, though. Fino Sherry is fortified with spirit; vin jaune is not, and its veil grows at lower alcohol levels and is thinner than the Andalusian one. In Italy something similar can be found in Sardinia with Vernaccia di Oristano, also aged under a veil of yeast, another example of the same intuition arising in places far apart.

The chemistry of partial oxidation produces compounds such as acetaldehyde and sotolon, the molecule associated with notes of walnut, curry and fenugreek that make vin jaune recognisable at first sniff. On the palate it remains a dry, taut wine, with an acidity that surprises anyone expecting a tired white. The recommended serving temperature is between 13 and 15 °C, warmer than for an ordinary white.

## The 62-centilitre clavelin

After so long a wait, the wine does not go into just any bottle. Vin jaune is bottled in the *clavelin*, a squat 62 cl bottle. The traditional explanation is arithmetic: after six years and three months of evaporation, about 62 centilitres remain from a litre of wine. The format thus tells, in glass, what time has taken away. It is one of those rare cases where the shape of a bottle has a technical meaning as well as an aesthetic one, a subject we tackled when writing about bottle formats, from the magnum upwards.

As for the table, local tradition is clear: vin jaune goes with aged Comté, walnuts and regional dishes such as chicken cooked in vin jaune itself, often with morels. A pairing rooted in place in the most literal sense.

## La Percée du Vin Jaune

Every year, usually on the first weekend of February, the Jura celebrates the release of the new vintage with the Percée du Vin Jaune: the first cask is “pierced” and the wine that has reached the end of its six years and three months is tasted. The festival was born in 1997 in Poligny, the idea of the grower Bernard Badoz, and moves to a different village each edition, with cellars open and visitors wandering from *caveau* to *caveau*. Around 15,000 visitors came to the first edition; the average, according to reported figures, is close to 45,000, with a record of more than 60,000 in 2011. In 2021 the event gave way to open days at some thirty estates; the 2026 edition was held in Lons-le-Saunier. At the time of writing I have found no official confirmation of the village that will host the 2027 edition.

## A wine that doesn’t need to please everyone

My view is that vin jaune is one of the few great wines that remain truly stubborn. It does not chase international taste, it is not tamed with new oak, it cannot be understood in a single sip. It asks time of those who make it and a little attention of those who drink it, and in return offers something no other white can: the taste of waiting. In a world that wants everything ready at once, a wine that makes you wait six years and three months is almost a manifesto. And the fact that it endures, in so small a region, is good news for anyone who loves wine with something to say.
