The most famous fraud in the history of wine was not uncovered by a laboratory, an algorithm or a police investigation. It was uncovered by a producer who knew his own records. In the spring of 2008, at an auction in New York, a run of Clos Saint-Denis from Domaine Ponsot, a Burgundy Grand Cru, appeared in the catalogue with vintages from 1945 to 1971. There was just one problem: Domaine Ponsot had never made that wine before 1982. The bottles, in other words, came from vintages that did not exist. And the seller was the man who, for a few years, had been the most envied collector in America.

## The young man who bought everything

Rudy Kurniawan, born Zhen Wang Huang, came into the world in Jakarta in 1976. Arriving in the United States on a student visa in the late 1990s, he settled in Los Angeles and within a few years had become a legend in the small world of fine-wine auctions. He was nicknamed “Dr. Conti” for his devotion to Domaine de la Romanée-Conti, the most mythical name in Burgundy. He hosted dinners at which bottles were opened that most enthusiasts will never see in their lifetime. He seemed to have an infallible palate and a bottomless wallet.

2006 was his year. By most accounts he was spending up to a million dollars a month at auction. That same year, through the New York house Acker Merrall & Condit, he put part of his cellar under the hammer in two sales that raised $10.6 million and $24.7 million: the second was, at the time, the record for a single wine sale. Hardly anyone asked where all that abundance was coming from.

## The vintage that wasn’t

The first warning signs came early. In 2007 Christie’s in Los Angeles withdrew several magnums of Château Le Pin 1982 after the estate declared them fake. But it was the Ponsot affair in 2008 that changed everything. Laurent Ponsot, then running the family domaine in Morey-Saint-Denis, noticed that the catalogue listed Clos Saint-Denis from vintages his family had never bottled, along with a Clos de la Roche 1929 from an era before the domaine bottled its own wine. He stepped in, and the lots were withdrawn from the sale.

To take an Italian example, the nearest equivalent would be someone auctioning a vertical of a celebrated Supertuscan, one of the prestige Tuscan reds, including vintages from before the wine was born: a mistake that only a producer, or a very well-informed collector, could spot at a glance. And that is precisely the point. The forger was betting on wines so rare that almost nobody had anything to compare them with.

In the years that followed, suspicions mounted. The American collector Bill Koch, who had bought bottles from Kurniawan, sued him in 2009. Other experts, among them the lawyer and Burgundy enthusiast Don Cornwell, began to flag suspect lots in public. In early 2012 several bottles thought to have come from him were pulled from an auction in London.

## Arrest, trial, sentence

On 8 March 2012 the FBI entered Kurniawan’s home in Arcadia, California. What agents found has been described as a genuine counterfeiting factory: inexpensive Napa Valley wines destined to pass as old Bordeaux, corks, stamps, labels and other materials for dressing up fakes. The following day he was indicted in New York for mail and wire fraud.

The trial ended on 18 December 2013 with a guilty verdict. The prosecution’s evidence included testimony from some of Burgundy’s most respected producers, among them Aubert de Villaine of the Romanée-Conti, Christophe Roumier and Ponsot himself, as well as emblematic cases such as a Bonnes-Mares 1923 attributed to Domaine Georges Roumier, which according to the prosecution made no wine before 1924. On 7 August 2014 came the sentence: ten years in federal prison. In July of that year Koch had settled his lawsuit for $3 million.

Kurniawan left prison in November 2020 and was deported from the United States to Indonesia in April 2021. How many of his bottles are still in circulation nobody knows for certain. *Sour Grapes*, the 2016 documentary about the affair, suggests there could be as many as ten thousand, but that is an estimate, not an established fact.

## How to spot a fake

The most useful lesson of the Kurniawan case is that the most dangerous fakes are not the clumsy ones but the plausible ones. That said, experts and authenticators have been repeating a handful of warning signs for years, and they are worth knowing even if your cellar holds no post-war Grand Cru.

- **The bottle that cannot exist.** Vintages never produced, formats not in use at the time, quantities larger than those on record. One often-cited case is that of eight magnums of Lafleur 1947 offered when, according to a former head of wine at Sotheby’s, only five had been made.
- **The anachronistic label.** An appellation or an owner’s name that appears on the label before it existed. The paper matters too: very white stock, made with optical brighteners, glows under ultraviolet light, and has no business on a label from the 1940s.
- **Printing and glue.** Old labels were printed with specific letterpress techniques; the dot pattern of a modern printer shows up under a loupe. The same goes for modern glues and for stains around the edges.
- **Capsule and cork.** Multiple creases in the capsule suggest it has been removed and refitted. The cork of a great Bordeaux is usually long and branded with a hot iron, not printed in ink.
- **Provenance.** The simplest defence and the most neglected: original invoices, documented changes of ownership, known storage conditions. A deal that looks too good to be true usually is.

## The real culprit is appetite

My view is that Kurniawan was both an exceptionally skilful fraudster and the perfect product of his time. The fine-wine market of the 2000s wanted legendary bottles, wanted them now and in quantity, and stopped asking questions just when it should have been asking more. The forger sold collectors exactly what they longed for. That is why his story matters even to those who will never buy a 1945: it is a reminder that in wine, as elsewhere, trust is a precious commodity, and that the first line of defence against fraud is still the memory of the people who make the wine.
