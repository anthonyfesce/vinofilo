Anyone discovering Italian wine from abroad usually starts with three names: Chianti, Prosecco, Barolo. Then they open a wine list in Rome or Milan and face dozens of acronyms, hundreds of grapes with unpronounceable names and a geography that seems to shift on every page. It is not just an impression: wine is made in all twenty of Italy’s regions, from the Alps to waters within sight of Tunisia. The good news is that the map, once you grasp its logic, can be read.

## Three acronyms to find your bearings

The Italian pyramid has three tiers, matching the European categories. At the top is DOCG, *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*: a defined area, strict production rules and tighter checks. Below it sits DOC, built on the same principle with somewhat looser rules. DOCG and DOC are, in essence, Italy’s equivalent of the French AOC, and both fall under the EU’s PDO. Then comes IGT, *Indicazione Geografica Tipica*, which corresponds to PGI: a broader origin and more freedom over grape varieties.

Beware of reading the pyramid as a quality ranking. Some of Italy’s most celebrated wines, the so-called Super Tuscans born in and around Bolgheri, were released for years as humble table wines or as IGTs precisely because their producers wanted to use grapes and methods the rules did not allow. The label tells you where a wine comes from and which rules it follows, not how good it is.

According to the count by Federdoc, the confederation of Italy’s wine consortia, updated to March 2024, Italy has 77 DOCGs, 330 DOCs and 118 IGTs. Some figures may have changed since, and other sources give slightly different totals. One technical caveat: some denominations are interregional, such as Prosecco DOC, shared by Veneto and Friuli Venezia Giulia, so the regional totals add up to more than the national one.

## The North, between mountains and bubbles

Piedmont is the region with the most DOCGs, and not by chance. It works a little like Burgundy: one noble grape, Nebbiolo, that changes face from hill to hill, and a culture of single vineyards that has grown ever more precise in recent decades. But everyday Piedmont drinks Barbera and Dolcetto, and that is worth remembering.

To the east, Veneto is the powerhouse of volume. Prosecco has made Italian wine a familiar name from London pubs to New York bars, but the same region produces Amarone, a red made from grapes left to dry for months, and Soave, one of the country’s most underrated whites. Trentino-Alto Adige is a case apart: in Alto Adige, or Südtirol, most people speak German, labels are often bilingual and the style of the wines, crisp and alpine, looks more to Austria than to Tuscany. Friuli Venezia Giulia, on the Slovenian border, is the home of Italy’s great whites and one of the places where the tradition of skin-macerated whites, orange wines, was revived. In Lombardy, finally, there is Franciacorta, Italy’s answer to Champagne, made by the same method of second fermentation in bottle.

## The Centre and the South, where Sangiovese makes room

Tuscany is Sangiovese in all its guises: Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino, Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. Anyone who knows Pinot Noir will find something familiar in the translucency and acidity of the best Sangiovese, though the tannin is another story. Next door, Umbria has Sagrantino, one of the most tannic reds in the world, and the Marche have Verdicchio, a white capable of long ageing.

Further south, the map grows more surprising. Campania has a trio that stands comparison with any northern region: Taurasi from Aglianico grapes, Fiano di Avellino and Greco di Tufo. Puglia built its fame on Primitivo, genetically the same grape as California’s Zinfandel, and on Negroamaro. Basilicata has Aglianico del Vulture, grown on the slopes of an extinct volcano, and Calabria has Cirò, one of the wines that lays claim to the most ancient origins.

The islands are a chapter of their own. Sicily went in a few decades from a reputation as a reservoir of bulk wine to that of one of Europe’s most dynamic regions, thanks above all to Etna, where vines grow on the slopes of an active volcano. Sardinia has Cannonau, that is, Grenache, and Vermentino di Gallura, the island’s only DOCG.

## A map you never finish

My advice to anyone arriving from abroad is not to look for the “best” region, because in Italy the question makes no sense. It makes more sense to pick a thread and follow it: mountain reds, volcanic whites, wines from dried grapes. Italian wine is not a country with a few great regions and provinces around them, as happens elsewhere; it is a mosaic of twenty small wine nations, each with its own grapes, its own rules and its own pride. It is demanding, at times frustrating. But it is also the reason why, after years, you keep discovering something new.
