Anyone arriving in Piacenza with the Emilia of Lambrusco and Romagna Sangiovese in mind finds something else entirely. Here the hills look towards the Oltrepò Pavese rather than Modena, the grapes are those of the western Po Valley, and the house red, poured alongside thickly sliced salumi and *tortelli con la coda*, the local plaited pasta, bears a name that seems lifted from an archaeology textbook: Gutturnio. It is not a cover-star wine. It is a table wine in the best sense, with a second life as a serious red that few outside the province know about.

## A Roman name for a modern red

The name comes from the *gutturnium*, a Roman vessel for pouring wine. The production rules themselves, in their historical section, recall the discovery of a silver example in the sands of the Po at Croce Santo Spirito in 1878, now held in a museum in Rome. An older find, though not directly linked to the name, is a metal vessel decorated with vine tendrils and bunches of grapes dug up at Veleia in 1760. It is a noble lineage, assembled after the fact as so often with Italian wines: antiquity serves to give a passport to a red that, in substance, is the child of peasant farming.

The substance is a blend of two grapes. The rules call for 55 to 70% Barbera and 30 to 45% Croatina, known here as Bonarda. The division of labour is clear: Barbera brings acidity and fruit, Croatina colour, flesh and a slightly rustic tannin. Anyone who has read our piece on the Oltrepò Pavese will recognise the pairing, and with good reason: the border is a few kilometres away, and the rules even allow certain cellars in Rovescala, in the province of Pavia, to make Gutturnio. Wine geography here pays little heed to regional borders.

For a long time Gutturnio was one of the styles within the Colli Piacentini DOC, created in 1967. Since 2010 it has been a DOC in its own right, with around 1,000 hectares of vines in 2021 and average production of about 75,000 hectolitres. These are the numbers of a regional wine, not a market phenomenon.

## Fizzy or still, the real choice

The question that matters in a Piacenza restaurant is not which producer but which Gutturnio. The basic version, by law, is the frizzante: a red with a light, “evanescent” sparkle, as the official text puts it, at least 12% alcohol and residual sugar of up to 17 grams per litre, which means anything from dry to off-dry. It is the wine for coppa, salame and pancetta piacentina, the province's three PDO cured meats: the bubbles and acidity cut through the fat, while the Croatina gives enough body not to vanish.

Then there is still Gutturnio, which the rules divide into Superiore, with at least 12.5%, and Riserva, with at least 13% and a minimum of 24 months' ageing, six of them in wood. There is also the Classico designation, reserved for Superiore and Riserva made in a historic zone that takes in the whole commune of Ziano Piacentino and parts of others, from the Val Tidone to the Val d'Arda. Here the wine changes character: darker, more structured, better suited to roasts and bollito than to antipasti.

My suspicion is that the still version is the real frontier. The frizzante is irresistible on home ground but travels badly, in the sense that outside western Emilia it struggles to find the right table. The Superiore and Riserva, by contrast, can speak to anyone who drinks Piedmontese Barbera or the reds of the Oltrepò, and they tell the story of the place better.

## Ortrugo, the other grape that was never meant to be

Among the whites, the best story belongs to Ortrugo. The name is said to come from Piacenza dialect for “the other grape”: the one used only in blends, alongside Malvasia, with no ambitions of its own. It is one of the few local varieties to have survived the great frosts and phylloxera, yet by the 1970s it had nearly vanished from the vineyards. In the second half of that decade the producer Luigi Mossi was about to grub up some old vines; instead he vinified them on their own and then propagated cuttings. Everything started again from there: Ortrugo entered a DOC in 1984 and since 2010 has had its own, now Ortrugo dei Colli Piacentini, requiring at least 90% of the variety and covering around 440 hectares in 2021.

In the glass it is a dry white of moderate structure, delicate aromas and a light saline streak. It is mostly found frizzante, but there are also still and sparkling versions, both Charmat and traditional method. It is, at heart, the white twin of frizzante Gutturnio: a wine born for the aperitivo and the everyday table.

## Malvasia di Candia aromatica, the scent of the hills

The other emblematic white is Malvasia di Candia aromatica, not to be confused with plain Malvasia di Candia grown elsewhere in central Italy: this one really is aromatic, with notes recalling Moscato. The Colli Piacentini DOC allows it still, frizzante, sparkling and passito. The passito has precise rules: grapes dried on the vine and on racks, pressing no earlier than 1 December and at least 14% total alcohol.

Malvasia also goes into the blends of the historic subzones, such as Monterosso Val d'Arda and Trebbianino Val Trebbia, where it partners Ortrugo and Trebbiano Romagnolo. And then there is the true rarity, Vin Santo di Vigoleno, made in a small area of the commune of Vernasca from local grapes such as Santa Maria and Melara, with at least five years' ageing, four of them in small wooden casks. One of those things that justify the journey on their own.

## A province that should believe in itself more

The Colli Piacentini have everything a wine region needs: a tradition documented since Roman times, grapes with an identity, a cuisine that matches them perfectly and four valleys, the Tidone, Trebbia, Nure and Arda, that give different wines. What is missing, it seems to me, is a little ambition in telling the story. Too often Gutturnio remains “the trattoria fizz”, and Ortrugo a white to drink without a second thought. That is fine, as long as you stay in the province. But the day the people of Piacenza decide to put their still reds and aromatic whites centre stage, we will discover that between Milan and Parma lay a wine region we had only ever glimpsed from the train window.
