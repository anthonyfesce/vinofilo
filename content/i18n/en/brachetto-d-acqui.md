The wine world has an unwritten hierarchy that puts anything red, sweet and fizzy at the very bottom. It sounds like a village-fête combination, the bottle that arrives in the corporate Christmas hamper and is then forgotten at the back of the larder. Brachetto d'Acqui has been paying for this prejudice for decades. And yet, at the end of an Italian Christmas lunch, when the chocolate yule log and the red-berry tart reach the table, it is one of the very few Italian wines that does not embarrass itself. It is worth asking why.

## An aromatic grape, and it shows

Brachetto is a red grape from Piedmont, and its defining trait is that it belongs to the family of aromatic varieties, like Moscato. In other words, the perfume is already in the berry before the cellar gets anywhere near it: rose, strawberry, raspberry, sometimes a hint of spice. People tasting it for the first time often think of a Moscato dressed in red, and they are not far off.

Its precise origins are unknown. It is believed to be Piedmontese, at home in the hills of the Monferrato and around Asti, although some 19th-century ampelographers suggested it came from France. Its ancient history is decorated with stories that are prettier than they are provable: there is talk of a *vinum acquense* prized by Roman patricians, and even a legend that Caesar and Mark Antony gave some to Cleopatra. Let us take it for what it is, a legend, mostly useful to whoever prints the brochures.

## What the rules say

The recent history is on firmer ground. Brachetto d'Acqui has been a DOC since 1969 and a DOCG since 1996. The current production rules require at least 97% Brachetto, with a small allowance for other Piedmontese grapes, and set a maximum yield of 8,000 kilos of grapes per hectare.

The zone is small and precisely drawn: 26 communes straddling two provinces. Eight are in the province of Alessandria, led by Acqui Terme and Strevi; eighteen are in the province of Asti, between the Bormida and Belbo valleys, as far as the part of Nizza Monferrato on the right bank of the stream. This is a Piedmont of gentle hills and tiny villages, far from the fame of the Langhe and all the less trodden for it.

There are three styles (and the denomination, incidentally, may also appear on the label simply as Acqui). There is the basic Brachetto d'Acqui, still or lightly frizzante, with pressure that may not exceed 2.5 bar. There is the spumante, the best-known version, with a more assertive fizz. And there is the passito, very hard to find, made from dried grapes, with at least 50 grams of sugar per litre and not released before 1 October of the year after the harvest.

The most interesting detail concerns alcohol. For the basic version the rules require a minimum actual alcohol of just 5%, for the spumante 6%, while the rest of the potential alcohol stays in the glass as unfermented sugar. It is the same principle as Moscato d'Asti: fermentation stops early, and what does not become alcohol becomes sweetness. The rules now also allow less sweet versions, all the way to dry, but the soul of the denomination remains sugary.

## Why it works with chocolate

Anyone who has read our piece on wine and chocolate already knows that cocoa is the natural enemy of almost every bottle. The reason is simple: a dessert makes any wine that is less sweet than itself taste sharper and more bitter. A serious dry red next to a slice of chocolate cake turns rough and woody. A brut sparkler tastes metallic.

Brachetto gets round the problem with four weapons at once. The first is sugar, which lets it stand level with the dessert instead of succumbing to it. The second is acidity, which stops the sweetness from becoming cloying. The third is the bubbles, which scrub the palate clean of butter and cocoa fat. The fourth, and the least obvious, is the red fruit: strawberry and raspberry are classic partners for chocolate in patisserie too, and here they arrive already in the glass.

Then there is a practical advantage that matters at the end of a meal. With so little alcohol, Brachetto does not weigh down a lunch that may have begun at noon and is ending at sunset. It is a wine you can still drink when anything else would be one too many.

A fair warning, though. Brachetto is at its best with milk chocolate, with cocoa cakes that are not too bitter, with mousses and desserts where chocolate meets fruit. Faced with an 85% dark bar it loses, like nearly everything else: that calls for more powerful, concentrated wines, the fortified sweet reds or Barolo Chinato.

## Christmas, beyond the panettone

With classic panettone the most natural choice is still a Moscato, as we discussed in our piece on panettone and pandoro: candied peel, raisins and citrus ask for white, floral aromas. Brachetto comes into its own with everything at Christmas that is red or dark. The chocolate yule log, cocoa biscuits, the forest-fruit tart, the chocolate-filled panettone that has taken over the shelves in recent years. It even works with strawberries, if anyone has the nerve to serve them in December.

Serve it cool, like a sparkling wine, but not ice-cold: too much chill snuffs out precisely the aromas that are its reason for being. And drink it young. The rules make the vintage compulsory on the label for every style except the spumante, and it is worth checking: Brachetto is not a wine that improves in the cellar, it is a wine at its best while its fruit is still fresh and crunchy.

## A wine to take seriously, precisely because it isn’t serious

My view is that Brachetto d'Acqui is one of Italy’s most misunderstood wines, and that its reputation as an easy wine is partly to blame. Nobody asks a sfogliatella to be haute cuisine, yet Brachetto is reproached for not being a Barolo. It is a wine that does one thing and does it very well: it keeps the dessert company without being flattened by it. In a country that loves to end its festive lunches with a dry red poured out of habit next to the yule log, simply remembering that it exists would be a small step forward.
