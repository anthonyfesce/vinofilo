“How strong is wine?” is one of those questions to which the most honest answer is another question: which wine? The range runs from just over 5% to above 20%, and most table wines sit in a fairly narrow band, roughly between 11% and 14.5%. To find your way you need three things: to know what the law says, to understand where the alcohol comes from, and to read the label properly.

## What the law says

For the European Union, wine has a floor and a ceiling. [Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/IT/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013R1308) requires at least 8.5% actual alcohol in the coolest wine-growing zones and 9% in the others, Italy included, and sets a maximum total alcoholic strength of 15%. But the ceiling has exceptions: it can be exceeded, for example, by wines with a protected designation of origin made without enrichment, that is, without adding sugar or concentrated must. That is why an Amarone can easily go above 15%.

The other categories have rules of their own. Semi-sparkling wine must have at least 7% actual alcohol. Liqueur wines, those to which alcohol is added, range between 15% and 22%. And then there are the exceptions written into the production rules, such as that of Moscato d’Asti, which we will come to shortly.

## Where the alcohol comes from

Wine’s alcohol is born from the grape’s sugar, which yeasts transform during fermentation. The more sugar in the must, the more alcohol there can be in the wine. That is why wines from hot regions, or hot vintages, tend to be more alcoholic: the grapes ripen further and build up more sugar. And that is why global warming is pushing alcohol levels upwards in many areas, an issue producers and consortia have been debating for years.

There is, however, a detail that explains almost all the exceptions: fermentation can be stopped before all the sugar has become alcohol. In that case the wine remains sweet and less alcoholic. This is where two label terms come in, “actual” and “total” alcohol. Actual strength is the alcohol really in the glass; total strength adds to it the alcohol that would be obtained if the remaining sugar fermented too. For a dry wine the two numbers almost coincide; for a sweet wine they do not.

## A map, from lightest to strongest

With the caveat that every bottle is a story of its own, here are the strengths you meet most often. Where we do not cite a production rule, the ranges are indicative: they describe the most common labels, not legal limits.

- **Moscato d’Asti, about 5–5.5%.** It is the extreme case. The production rules require a minimum actual strength of just 5%; many producers stop at 5.5%, and the rest of the potential alcohol stays in the glass as sweetness.
- **Sweet German Rieslings, around 8%.** Fruity Kabinett and Spätlese wines from the Mosel often sit at these levels, for the same reason: residual sugar, low alcohol.
- **Sparkling and semi-sparkling wines, usually 10.5–12.5%.** The Prosecco DOC rules, for example, set a minimum of 11% for the sparkling version. Dry Lambrusco generally sits in the same zone.
- **Dry whites, usually 11.5–13.5%.** Lower in cool and mountain areas, higher in the South or with very ripe grapes.
- **Rosés, usually 12–13.5%.**
- **Dry reds, usually 12.5–14.5%.** Barolo and Brunello are often around 14%; light reds to be drunk cool, such as Schiava or Grignolino, can stay below 13%.
- **Amarone and great dry wines from dried grapes, 14% and up.** The Amarone rules set a minimum of 14%, and many go well above 15%: drying concentrates the sugar, and the yeasts transform almost all of it.
- **Vin Santo, high strength by nature.** For Vin Santo del Chianti the minimum is around 13%, but many bottles are higher, with residual sugar that varies greatly from one cellar to another.
- **Marsala, from 17% upwards.** The rules require a minimum of around 17% for Fine and 18% for Superiore: it is a liqueur wine, to which alcohol is added.
- **Port, usually 19–22%.** A neutral grape spirit is added during fermentation, the yeasts stop, and the wine stays both sweet and strong.

## And mulled wine?

Vin brulé, or Glühwein, starts from an ordinary wine, so roughly 12–13%, to which sugar, spices and often citrus peel or juice are added. Under the European law on aromatised wines, Glühwein must be made only from red or white wine, with no added water, and have at least 7% actual alcohol. How much alcohol it loses when heated depends on temperature and time: the idea that “boiling makes it all evaporate” is wrong, because ethanol leaves only a little at a time. Mulled wine heated without being boiled for long remains an alcoholic drink, and should be treated as such, especially if you are driving.

## How to read it on the label

On the bottle the strength is mandatory and is always the actual alcoholic strength, written in whole or half units followed by the symbol “% vol”, as required by [Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/IT/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0033). So 13% vol or 13.5% vol, never 13.2. The number indicates the millilitres of pure alcohol in 100 millilitres of wine: a 150 ml glass at 13.5% contains about 20 ml of alcohol, or some 16 grams.

Our view is that alcohol content is the most underrated figure on the label. It says nothing about quality, but a great deal about style: a red at 12.5% promises freshness, one at 15% warmth and weight, and neither is better than the other in absolute terms. Checking it before you buy helps you choose the right wine for the right evening, and reminds you that half a degree more, by the end of dinner, makes itself felt.
