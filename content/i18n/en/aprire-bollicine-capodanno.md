Every year, a few seconds before midnight, the same scene plays out in millions of homes: someone grabs a bottle, points it at the ceiling, or worse at a guest, and pulls. The bang, the applause, half the wine on the floor. It is the most festive gesture in the calendar and also, when you think about it, the worst possible way to open a bottle holding more pressure than a car tyre. It is worth learning to do it properly, whether you are toasting in Milan, London or New York.

## A small bomb in glass

Start with the number that explains everything. A bottle of Champagne holds between 5 and 6 atmospheres of pressure, more than twice that of a tyre. The same goes for Italy’s great traditional-method wines, Franciacorta and Trento DOC, which, like Champagne, undergo their second fermentation in the bottle. The exception is Franciacorta Satèn, a creamier style that the rules keep below 5 atmospheres.

The Italian vocabulary is quickly mastered. *Spumante* is the generic term for fully sparkling wine: European law sets a minimum excess pressure of 3 atmospheres for the category. *Frizzante* refers to lightly sparkling wines, between 1 and 2.5 atmospheres, such as many Lambruschi or certain Proseccos closed with string-tied corks. Prosecco spumante, made in large pressurised tanks by the Charmat method, belongs firmly in the first camp. And even the less highly charged bottles fire a cork with enough force to do damage.

That is why sparkling-wine bottles are so thick and heavy: the early, thinner glass exploded with embarrassing frequency in the cellars.

## The cork and the cage

Before it goes into the neck, a sparkling-wine cork is almost 50% wider than the opening. It is squeezed in hard, which is why, once out, it takes on that mushroom shape that will never fit back into the bottle. Over it sits the wire cage, the *muselet* to the French: Adolphe Jacquesson invented it in 1844 precisely to stop corks from blowing out on their own. Traditionally it comes undone with six half-turns of the little loop.

The correct method is simple and rather undramatic. Remove the foil, put your thumb on the cork and keep it there, because from that moment the cork can go. Loosen the cage without taking it off, so it gives you grip. Tilt the bottle to about 45 degrees, pointing it at an empty corner of the room. Then, and this is the secret, do not twist the cork: twist the bottle, holding the cork still in your hand. The cork eases out slowly and the pressure escapes with a sigh rather than a shot. The angle also helps prevent a geyser of foam, because it lets air into the neck more gently.

The bang is fun, but it costs wine and occasionally a light fitting.

## Sabrage, for those who insist

Then there is the move everyone wants to see at least once: sabrage, opening the bottle with a sabre. Legend credits Napoleon’s hussars, who are said to have opened bottles on horseback with their swords; another version links it to the widow Clicquot and the soldiers keen to impress her. Legends, but they make the point.

The principle, by contrast, is pure physics. The neck of the bottle has a thin vertical seam in the glass and a thicker ring of glass, the lip, just below the cork. Where the seam meets the lip there is a weak point. With the cage removed and the bottle well chilled, slide the blade, using the back rather than the edge, along the seam until it strikes the lip. The glass breaks cleanly and the pressure sends cork and collar flying, sometimes 5–10 metres away. The blade need not be sharp: Champagne sabres are often blunt, and some people use the back of a sturdy knife or even a spoon.

Two rules are non-negotiable: nobody in front of the bottle, and let a little wine flow out to wash away any shards before pouring. Check the first glass all the same. Sabrage is a spectacle; a cut hand at five past midnight is not.

## The right chill, the right glass

Sparkling wine should be served cold but not frozen: for Champagne the recommended temperature is 7 to 9 °C, and the same applies to Italian traditional-method wines. The quickest way there is an ice bucket filled with ice and water together, not ice alone: in about half an hour the bottle is ready. Too cold and it loses its aromas; too warm and it gushes over the moment it is opened.

On glassware, opinion has become more relaxed. The flute, tall and narrow, reduces the wine’s surface area and keeps the mousse going for longer, and it remains the classic choice for celebrations. The wide coupe, which legend claims was modelled on the breast of Madame de Pompadour or Marie Antoinette, actually dates back to an English design of 1663, and it lets the bubbles escape quickly: beautiful, but impractical. Halfway between them is the tulip, wider in the middle and narrowing at the top, which lets the aromas breathe without sacrificing the fizz; many enthusiasts simply reach for a white-wine glass for the more complex traditional-method wines. Whatever the shape, hold it by the stem, so your hand does not warm the wine.

To pour, tilt the glass and let the wine slide down the side, in two stages: first a finger’s depth, wait for the foam to settle, then top up.

## How many glasses, and how to say cheers

A standard bottle holds 75 centilitres. With flutes filled sensibly, at around 100–125 ml, it yields six or seven glasses. For the midnight toast alone a bottle will stretch to eight people; for a party where sparkling wine flows all evening, allow half a bottle per person. A magnum, a litre and a half, is worth two bottles and always looks the part.

Which leaves the toast itself. In Italy people say *cin cin*, a word that imitates the clink of glasses and has cousins everywhere: the English “chin-chin” and the French *tchin-tchin* come, according to the most common explanation, from the Chinese *qing qing*. Germans say *Prost*, from the Latin *prosit*, used by university students since the early 18th century. The French, Spanish, Poles and Irish all wish the same thing, good health: *Santé*, *¡Salud!*, *Na zdrowie*, *Sláinte*. Scandinavians raise a *Skål*, meaning “bowl”, in memory of old drinking vessels. The Japanese and Chinese, with *Kanpai* and *Gānbēi*, are the most demanding: it means “empty the glass”. As for the idea that we clink glasses so that a little wine splashes from one into the other, as insurance against poisoning, it is a charming story with no evidence whatsoever.

## Our view

The midnight bang need not be banned: it needs toning down. A bottle opened calmly, twisted rather than pulled, with the cork leaving on a sigh, takes nothing away from the party and leaves more wine in the glasses. Those who want a show should learn sabrage, and do it outdoors. But true elegance on New Year’s Eve lies in serving sparkling wine at the right temperature, in a glass where the bubbles can last until the second toast. The rest is noise, and for that we already have the fireworks.
