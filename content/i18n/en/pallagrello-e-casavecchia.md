Some wines have a history, others only a legend. Pallagrello and Casavecchia have the misfortune, and the good fortune, of having both: a past on the royal table, a century of near-total silence and a revival so recent that many wine lovers, even in Campania, have never tasted them. Yet half an hour from the Reggia di Caserta, the vast Bourbon palace, one of the most interesting games in Italian wine is being played.

## The fan-shaped vineyard

To understand Pallagrello you have to start in San Leucio, the village behind the palace where the Bourbons founded their silk colony. Here, under Ferdinand IV, King of Naples, the Vigna del Ventaglio was laid out: a semicircular vineyard divided into ten segments, like the ribs of an open fan, each given over to a different variety among those considered the finest in the Kingdom. The sources agree on the design and on who commissioned it; on the exact date and the designer, however, the accounts differ, and it is wiser to leave them open.

One of those segments held Pallagrello. At the time it was also known as Piedimonte, red and white, after Piedimonte Matese, where the earliest traces of it are found. Several accounts describe it as the king’s wine, served on important occasions at court. It is not the only link with the palace: the Bourbons also had some of the Kingdom’s best grapes planted in the vineyard of the Bosco di San Silvestro, inside the royal park.

The name, on the other hand, is a minor muddle. The most repeated explanation ties it to the round shape of the berry, a little ball, or *palla*; another derives it from the *pagliarello*, the straw mat on which grapes were laid out to dry. Neither is proven, and perhaps it does not matter: in Campania grape names are often peasant nicknames that became official through sheer persistence.

## A century of silence

Then the story breaks off. Phylloxera, which arrived in the second half of the 19th century, and the two world wars did the rest. In the 20th century farmers preferred more productive grapes, and Pallagrello, which by nature gives very little, all but vanished. For decades it was considered extinct, surviving in a few isolated rows and in the memory of old growers, who sometimes confused it with Coda di Volpe.

Rediscovery came in the 1990s, at the hands of a small group of stubborn growers and enthusiasts who found the old vines in abandoned vineyards around Caserta and propagated them again, together with the white version. Some of the estates that signed the first Pallagrello Nero labels, such as Terre del Principe and Nanni Copè, no longer exist: a detail that shows how fragile, and how personal, this revival has been.

## Two Pallagrellos, no obvious kinship

One of the surprises to emerge from research is that Pallagrello Bianco and Pallagrello Nero are not the same grape in two colours. Ampelographers rule out the black being a mutation of the white, and DNA analysis has not yet clarified their relationship. It has, however, ruled out any link with Coda di Volpe, despite one of the old synonyms.

The black is a mid-to-late ripening grape, naturally very low-yielding and rich in anthocyanins, and therefore in colour. In the glass it tends to give dark wines, with cherry fruit, a spicy note recalling pepper and a firm tannin, capable of ageing. The white has body and an unusual perfume, but not the cutting acidity of Fiano and Greco, the two great whites of Irpinia: taste it alongside them and you see at once that it plays a different game, softer and broader.

The area lies north-east of Caserta, along the Volturno river, between the Colline Caiatine and the edge of the Matese park: Alife, Alvignano, Caiazzo, Castel Campagnano, Castel di Sasso, as far as the slopes of the extinct Roccamonfina volcano. On labels it almost always appears under the Terre del Volturno geographical indication.

## Casavecchia, the vine found among the ruins

If Pallagrello has a royal past, Casavecchia has a founding myth. The most often told version holds that the variety was propagated from a single old vine that survived among Roman-era remains, and the name, according to local tradition, recalls that very ruined old house, the *casa vecchia*. It is often linked to *Trebulanum*, a wine praised by Pliny the Elder, but the association is evocative rather than proven, and should be taken as such.

The grape’s character is more concrete. Compared with Pallagrello Nero, Casavecchia is even less productive, more acidic and finer in tannin. Some genetic clues suggest a possible relationship between the two, but its nature remains to be clarified. It has its own denomination, the Casavecchia di Pontelatone DOC, named after one of the local communes, whose rules require time in wood: a debated choice, because oak does not always do justice to a grape that is at its best when it keeps its freshness.

Meanwhile the link with the palace has been retied. Since 2018 a hectare in the Bosco di San Silvestro, within the royal park, has been entrusted to a winery that has replanted Pallagrello Bianco and Nero there: the king’s vineyard is back, at least in miniature.

## Our view

The temptation, with grapes like these, is to sell them as relics: the Bourbons’ wine, Pliny’s wine, the wine brought back from the dead. It is a story that works, but it risks smothering the real question, which is whether Pallagrello and Casavecchia are great grapes even without the historical frame. Our answer is a cautious yes: Casavecchia in particular, with its acidity and its fine tannin, has the makings of a red worth waiting for. Time will decide the rest, and perhaps also the ability of a little-told territory to pull together. The king, after all, chose them for their taste, not for their legend.
