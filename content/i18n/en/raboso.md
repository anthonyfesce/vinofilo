Some wines win you over at the first sip; others insist on being understood. Raboso belongs, beyond argument, to the second group. Young, it is a red that scratches: towering acidity, hard tannin, dark fruit struggling to make itself heard. In the Veneto of Prosecco, of easy bubbles and the soft reds of Valpolicella, it is the exception nobody has managed to domesticate entirely. Which is exactly why it is worth the telling.

## Rage in the name

The explanation you hear most often in the cellars of the Piave is that “Raboso” comes from *rabbioso*, “furious”: an angry wine that rebels in the mouth and leaves the palate in disarray. It is a perfect legend, because it describes the wine’s character better than any tasting sheet. But a legend it remains: I have found no documents to confirm it, and there are also theories tying the name to a local place or watercourse, just as hard to prove. Better to take it for what it is, a portrait rather than an etymology.

The documented history is firmer ground. The grape has been recorded in the Piave since at least the 17th century: in 1678 Giacomo Agostinetti, author of a treatise on agriculture, was already writing about it. Some go further back, to a “picina omnium nigerrima” described by Pliny the Elder, but linking an ancient grape to a modern variety on the strength of a few Latin words is an exercise in imagination rather than history.

## The Serenissima’s travelling wine

The most fascinating chapter concerns Venice. In the days of the Republic, Raboso was known as *vin da viajo*, the travelling wine, because its acidity and tannin let it survive long voyages without spoiling. For a wine bound for ships, that resilience was a huge commercial asset. The noble families who owned land between Treviso and the sea turned it into a business, and the wine spread along the whole course of the river.

It is a story that turns the modern view on its head. What looks like a flaw to us today, the sourness, was then the very reason for its success. Raboso did not need to be pleasant straight away: it needed to arrive in one piece.

## A grape in no hurry

The wine’s character is born in the vineyard. Raboso Piave buds early but ripens late, with a harvest that usually falls between mid-October and early November: one of the longest growing cycles among Italian grapes. The skins are thick, the acidity stays high even at full ripeness, and tannin is abundant. There is also a close relative, Raboso Veronese, grown in the same area and often used alongside it.

In the 20th century, like so many awkward native grapes, Raboso lost ground to international varieties that were easier to sell. The revival began in the 1990s, when a group of producers decided that its roughness could become an identity rather than a problem. We touched on it in our piece on forgotten grapes making a comeback: here it deserves the full story.

## Malanotte, the rulebook that tames it

The tool chosen was a strict *disciplinare*, the appellation rulebook. The Piave Malanotte DOCG, also called Malanotte del Piave, was recognised by decree on 22 December 2010 and published in the official gazette in early 2011. The name comes from Borgo Malanotte, a small medieval hamlet at Tezze di Piave, in the commune of Vazzola, regarded as the historic heart of production.

The rules spell out the strategy. At least 70% Raboso Piave, the rest Raboso Veronese (up to 30%), with a small possible share of other local red grapes. Between 15 and 30% of the grapes must be dried, in the *appassimento* tradition, and crushing may not take place before 15 November. Then comes time: at least 36 months of ageing from 1 November of the vintage year, including at least 12 in wood and 4 in bottle. Minimum alcohol 12.5% vol.

The idea is clear. Partial drying adds sugar, concentration and softness; wood and long ageing round off the tannin. It is a technique that recalls, on a smaller scale, the logic of Amarone: you do not fight the grape’s nature, you wrap it up. Alongside the DOCG there is still the Raboso of the Piave DOC, younger and more immediate, which better preserves the original character.

## The risk of softening too much

This is my one reservation. Every time a cantankerous wine wins a DOCG, there is a temptation to make it gentler so that everyone will like it. Drying and oak are valuable tools, but used with a heavy hand they risk turning Raboso into just another soft red, one among many. That would be a pity: Italy does not need another velvety, slightly sweet wine.

The best Raboso, to my mind, is the one that remains recognisable even after three years in the cellar: still taut, still a little sour, but with fruit that is finally ripe. A wine for rich food, for meat dishes and long winters. Its rage, legendary or not, is its capital. Those who make it would do well not to spend it all.
