Some grapes belong to a place by birthright, the way Nebbiolo belongs to the Langhe or Sangiovese to Tuscany. Others found their homeland by emigrating. Malbec is one of the latter: born in France and long cast as a supporting player, it had to cross an ocean and a mountain range to take the lead. Today, say Malbec and almost everyone thinks of Argentina. But the story begins in a bend of the river Lot.

## The black wine of Cahors

Cahors, in south-west France, has grown vines since Roman times. In the Middle Ages its wines travelled down to Bordeaux and from there put to sea for England, as early as the thirteenth century. Their fame rested above all on colour: a red so dark it was called black wine, *vin noir*, capable of lending body and hue to the paler wines it was blended with.

The grape, known in Cahors as Côt, is a natural cross between Prunelard and Magdeleine Noire des Charentes. The latter is also the mother of Merlot, which makes Malbec Merlot's half-brother: a kinship that explains something of its softness, though not its darker, more tannic character.

For a long time Malbec also had a role in Bordeaux, where it went into the blends of areas such as Bourg and Blaye. Then came the decline. Phylloxera, the insect that ravaged Europe's vineyards in the second half of the nineteenth century and whose story we have told on Vinofilo, struck Cahors between 1883 and 1885. The coup de grâce, or nearly, came in February 1956, when an exceptional frost destroyed most of the area's vineyards. Replanting was massive and, paradoxically, strengthened Malbec's hold. In 1971 Cahors gained its AOC, France's appellation of origin and the equivalent of Italy's DOC: the rules require at least 70% Malbec, with Merlot and Tannat making up the rest. Today the area covers around 4,200 hectares.

## Across the Andes by mule

Malbec's South American journey has a director and a date. The director is Domingo Faustino Sarmiento, an Argentine intellectual who, while in exile in Chile, had encountered the model of the Quintas Normales, experimental agricultural schools, and who would later become president of his country. In Santiago de Chile, in the mid-1840s, cuttings of French varieties, Malbec among them, were arriving under the care of the French agronomist Michel Aimé Pouget.

On 17 April 1853 the Quinta Normal de Agricultura was founded in Mendoza, and Pouget was summoned to run it. The French vines crossed the Andes on muleback. From Mendoza, Malbec spread north to San Juan, La Rioja, Catamarca and Salta, and only later south, along the Río Negro. For a grape about to live through its worst years in Europe, it was a kind of insurance policy: by the time phylloxera reached Cahors, Malbec had already put down roots on the far side of the world, in arid, luminous land watered by snowmelt from the mountains.

## Boom, bust and back again

Malbec's Argentine story is not a straight line. In the 1960s, according to the historian Pablo Lacoste, the variety reached some 58,600 hectares. Then came a slump in domestic consumption, and with it the grubbing-up: by the 1990s only about 10,500 hectares remained. A grape that seemed destined to become a curiosity for the second time in a century.

The turning point came with the opening of export markets, starting with the United States and Canada, in the 1990s. Argentine Malbec introduced itself to the world with a clear style: deep colour, ripe and generous fruit, rounded tannins, a red easy to understand and to love even for people who know little about wine. It was a resounding commercial success, and tens of thousands of hectares went back in the ground.

Today's figures show the scale of it. According to data from the Instituto Nacional de Vitivinicultura reported in the Mendoza press, Malbec covers almost 47,000 hectares, around a quarter of Argentina's vineyard area for wine and over 40% of its red-grape plantings. In 2023 it accounted for almost 70% of the volume of varietal wines exported, worth more than 410 million dollars. Few countries have a single grape that weighs so heavily on their image abroad; Italy, with its hundreds of native varieties, is the exact opposite.

## 17 April, a celebration built well

In 2011 Wines of Argentina, the body that promotes Argentine wine abroad, chose 17 April, the anniversary of the founding of the Quinta Normal, to celebrate Malbec World Day. Since then the date has been marked every year with tastings and events in many countries, often with the backing of Argentine embassies.

On reflection, it is a textbook case: a real historical date, tied to a real story, turned into a promotional tool. Anyone in Italy struggling to tell the world about their own grapes would do well to study it. There is no need to invent anything; you simply pick the right moment in your own history and repeat it, consistently.

## Two Malbecs, one open question

The upshot is that there are now at least two Malbecs. The Cahors version, more austere and tannic, grown along the meanders of the Lot, long dismissed as rustic and now being reassessed by those looking for reds with backbone. And the Argentine one, raised under the Andean sun, which has built a national identity. In recent years Argentina, too, has been talking more and more about high-altitude zones and different soils, a sign that the country is trying to move beyond the single style that made it famous.

My view is that Malbec has a lesson for everyone, Italy included. A grape is not condemned by the place it was born, nor by the reputation it carries. Cahors almost lost it, Argentina almost abandoned it, and both times it came back. The challenge now is not to become a prisoner of its own success: the risk, for Argentina, is that the world keeps asking for the same glass, when the grape is capable of a great deal more.
