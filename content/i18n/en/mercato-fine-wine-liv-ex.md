For a few years collectible wine looked like a stock that never went down. Then, in the autumn of 2022, the curve stalled and began to bend downwards, slowly but without hesitation, for almost three years. Those who follow this world watched it happen on one particular chart, that of the Liv-ex indices, the London platform on which merchants trade the most sought-after bottles on the planet. It is worth understanding what they measure, because they tell us far more than the price of a few famous labels.

## What a wine index measures

Liv-ex, as Vinofilo noted when covering the Decanter Fine Wine Encounter in London, was founded in 2000 by two former stockbrokers. It works as a wholesale market: it does not sell to consumers like us, but connects merchants, importers and brokers, and records the prices at which cases change hands. The indices are built from those trades.

The most quoted is the Liv-ex Fine Wine 100, which tracks the hundred most traded wines on the secondary market, that is, bottles resold after their first release. Alongside it sit the broader Liv-ex 1000 and a series of regional sub-indices: Bordeaux 500, Burgundy 150, Champagne 50, Italy 100. They are the tool the trade uses to look at itself, rather like a stock index for a single sector.

For an Italian reader the most interesting is, naturally, the Italy 100. It contains names familiar to anyone who has leafed through a serious wine list: the Barolos of Bartolo Mascarello, Giacomo Conterno’s Monfortino, Bruno Giacosa’s Barolo Falletto, Gaja’s Barbaresco and, on the Tuscan side, Sassicaia, Ornellaia, Masseto, Solaia, Tignanello and Flaccianello. In other words, Barolo on one hand and the so-called Super Tuscans on the other, the Tuscan reds born outside the rules of the historic appellations that later became icons.

## The slide from the peak

The Liv-ex Fine Wine 100 peaked in September 2022. From then on the sign was almost always negative. By the end of the first half of 2024 the index had lost 17.9% from its peak. 2024 closed with a fall of 9.1% for the Fine Wine 100 and 11.7% for the Liv-ex 1000; adding 2023, the two-year loss was 21.9% for the main index and 23.7% for the broader one.

The causes are not written into the indices, and caution is advisable. Still, it is hard not to notice that the slide began in the very year the major central banks raised interest rates after a decade of almost free money, making it less attractive to keep capital idle in a cellar. Added to this was a certain buyer fatigue with release prices seen as too high: in 2024, for instance, en primeur prices for Bordeaux 2023 were still 21% above 2021 levels.

## Burgundy and Champagne, the hardest hit

Not every region fell in the same way. In 2024 the worst sub-index was the Burgundy 150, down 15.2%, followed by the Champagne 50 and the Bordeaux 500, at minus 11.7% and minus 11.3% respectively. These were the regions that had climbed most in the preceding years, and the downturn had the feel of a correction after the euphoria. In Champagne’s case, moreover, a number of merchants had judged the prices of new releases too high.

And yet Burgundy, even as it lost value, gained weight in the trade. In the first half of 2024 it accounted for 22.7% of the value traded on Liv-ex, against 37.9% for Bordeaux, which remains the market’s centre of gravity even as it loses ground. Champagne was worth 10%, Tuscany almost as much, sharply up from 6.6% a year earlier. Piedmont stood at around 4%, the United States at 6.3%. The figures say something simple: in uncertain times, buyers move towards what they regard as safe havens.

## The Italian case

Italy was the region that held up best. In 2024 the Italy 100 lost 6.8%, the “least bad” result of all the sub-indices. In 2026, according to Liv-ex data for the first seven months of the year, the picture had grown calmer: the main index was slightly up, plus 0.4% year to date and plus 3.3% over twelve months, while the Italy 100 was up 1.5%, the Champagne 50 up 1.6%, the Burgundy 150 up 0.8% and the Bordeaux 500 still slightly in the red, at minus 0.3%.

Within this resilience, Tuscany does most of the work. Liv-ex observes that the Tuscan market has become concentrated: in the first months of 2026, 157 wines from the region were traded, and just nine of them accounted for over 80% of the value. It is a narrow recovery, made of very few names, and the available data do not show how far it extends to the rest of the region’s production. As for Barolo, its share of the trade remains small relative to its fame: a niche of great historic producers, much sought after but made in limited quantities.

An honest caveat: we have not been able to verify the full 2025 figures in detail, and so we do not state how far the market currently sits below its 2022 peak. The indices say the fall has stopped; they do not yet say that the climb has truly begun.

## What a chart can teach us

My view is that the downturn of recent years has done wine more good than it has done harm to those who bought it as an investment. It has been a reminder that a bottle is not a share: it costs money to store, it is hard work to sell, and its value depends on the taste of a public that changes. Italy comes out of it with a sturdier image, that of a country whose great reds weather the storms better than others. But if the market narrows to nine Tuscan labels and a handful of Barolos, the risk is mistaking the health of a few names for that of an entire wine culture. Italian quality wine is far broader than any index, and long may it stay that way.
