There is a good chance that the last glass of wine you drank at a dinner with friends, in a pizzeria or over Sunday lunch came from a cooperative. The label may not have said so, and nobody at the table gave it a thought. Yet the *cantina sociale*, which in the popular imagination still conjures up lorries laden with grapes queuing in September and demijohns being filled from a pump, is the real engine of Italian wine. It deserves a close look, without nostalgia and without prejudice.

## How much they really weigh

The figures are the kind that make you sit up. According to Ismea research presented at Vinitaly in 2023, Italy has 379 cooperative wineries with more than 110,000 members, and they turn out 58% of the wine produced in the country. Their combined turnover was estimated at around €4.8 billion, roughly 40% of national wine revenue, of which about €2 billion came from abroad. The same study found that cooperative exports had grown by 130% between 2010 and 2022, against 101% for Italian wine as a whole.

The gap between those two percentages, 58% of the volume and 40% of the value, already tells half the story. Cooperatives make a great deal of wine, and on average they sell it for less than private companies manage to. That is not a failing: it reflects a model built to give a secure outlet to the grapes of thousands of smallholders, not to chase collectors.

## The giants, in figures

Open Mediobanca’s latest survey of the accounts of Italy’s leading wine companies, covering 2025, and you notice that cooperatives are everywhere at the top of the table. In first place is Cantine Riunite & Civ with Gruppo Italiano Vini, at €635.1 million in turnover: a system born of Emilian cooperation, the very same that gave the world the sweetish Lambrusco that conquered the United States in the 1970s (we covered it in our piece on Lambrusco). In fourth place is Caviro, a cooperative from Romagna, with €351.3 million. Then come Cavit, a Trentino cooperative, with €242.8 million; Terre Cevico, another Romagna cooperative, with €213.2 million; and Mezzacorona, a Trentino cooperative group, with €213 million.

A few details help convey the scale. Cavit, in its 2024/2025 accounts, brings together eleven Trentino cooperative wineries and more than 5,250 growers, and earns 75% of its turnover abroad. Trentino, in other words, is one of the regions of Italy where cooperation is not a slice of the sector: it is the sector. The same holds for Alto Adige, where cooperatives process around 70% of the grapes and where, as we explained when writing about mountain wine, the cooperative is often the most ambitious producer in the valley too.

It should be said that 2025 was not an easy year: nearly all the big names in the ranking saw revenue fall, Caviro by 8.8%, Cavit by 4.1%. The trend affects the whole industry, not just cooperatives, but it weighs more heavily on those who live by volume.

## From Barbaresco to the post-war years

The story begins at the end of the nineteenth century, when low prices and ungenerous middlemen pushed small growers to join forces. The most famous case is Barbaresco, where in 1894 Domizio Cavazza, director of the Alba wine school, founded a cooperative winery so that farmers could make their own wine rather than sell off their grapes cheaply; it was later refounded in 1958 as Produttori del Barbaresco. In the same years the first cooperative wineries appeared in Alto Adige.

The real explosion, though, came after the Second World War, with the decline of *mezzadria*, Italy’s old sharecropping system, and the fragmentation of landholdings. For a farmer with two or three hectares, building a winery was unthinkable; delivering grapes to the village cooperative was the only way to turn a harvest into an income. The result was an Italy dotted with vast sheds full of concrete vats, in Emilia, Puglia, Sicily and the Veneto, where for decades there was only one goal: quantity.

That is where the poor reputation comes from. Members were paid by weight and sugar level, so they had no interest in cutting yields; the winery sold in bulk or in big flagons, often to bottlers in the north or abroad. An efficient system, but one that rewarded whoever produced more, not whoever produced better.

## Quality, when it comes

Change began when some cooperatives realised they could pay for grapes according to the vineyard and not just the weight. That means selecting deliveries, keeping the best parcels separate, setting pruning and yield rules stricter than the denomination’s own, and sometimes paying more to members who agree to produce less. It sounds simple; in an assembly of hundreds of members, each with a vote, it is a small political revolution.

The results show above all where the cooperative is rooted in a small, recognisable territory: Alto Adige, Trentino, Barbaresco, parts of Friuli and Sicily. There the cooperatives collect the guidebook awards and run single-vineyard ranges that have nothing to envy private estates. Elsewhere, especially in the large operations of the south and the plains, average quality has risen a great deal compared with thirty years ago, but the heart of the model remains wine that is correct, clean and fairly priced: the kind that ends up on supermarket shelves and in bag-in-box.

For anyone buying, the practical advice is simple: read the label. The words “cantina sociale”, “cantina cooperativa” or “società cooperativa agricola”, or the abbreviation s.c.a., tell you who made the wine. They do not tell you whether it is good, but they remove the excuse for prejudice.

## The paradox of democracy in the cellar

My view is that the cooperative is the most underrated institution in Italian wine. It has kept viticulture alive on hillsides that would otherwise have been abandoned, has provided an income for more than a hundred thousand member families and, at its best, has shown that quality is not the privilege of castles. Its limit is the same as its strength: many people decide, and it is hard for many people to say no to quantity. The cooperatives that found the courage to do so are now among the most respected names in Italy. The others need not be ashamed of everyday wine, which is a serious trade, but they should stop hiding behind invented brand names: saying “three hundred of us made this” is an argument, not a flaw.
