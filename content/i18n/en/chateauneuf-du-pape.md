Some wines are famous for a flavour, some for a price, others for a story. Châteauneuf-du-Pape is famous above all for an idea: that a wine should have a precise place, written rules and someone prepared to enforce them. Today we take this for granted, to the point that every bottle of Barolo or Rioja carries the fruit of that insight on its label. A century ago, though, in a small village in the Rhône valley between Avignon and Orange, it was an almost revolutionary notion.

## When the pope lived in Provence

The name, for once, is not marketing. In 1309 Pope Clement V, a Frenchman, took the papal court to Avignon, where the pontiffs would remain for some seventy years. His successor, John XXII, had a castle built on the hill north of the city as a summer residence: the pope’s “new castle”, hence the name. Little but ruins remains of it today, yet its silhouette has become the emblem of the appellation.

For an Italian reader the parallel is obvious: imagine if the wine of the Castelli Romani, the hills south of Rome, were named after the papal summer palace at Castel Gandolfo and bore its coat of arms. At Châteauneuf the coat of arms really is there: many bottles still come in heavy dark glass embossed with the papal tiara and the keys of St Peter.

## The baron who put it all in writing

The turning point has a name and a date. On 4 October 1923 the village’s vineyard owners formed a growers’ union and handed its presidency to Baron Pierre Le Roy de Boiseaumarié, owner of Château Fortia. The problem was fraud: the name Châteauneuf was turning up far too often on wines that had nothing to do with the place.

Le Roy answered with a set of rules that sounds obvious today and was anything but then: boundaries drawn according to soil type, permitted grapes, minimum alcohol, yield limits. It took years of litigation. In November 1933 France’s highest court, the Cour de Cassation, upheld the delimitation and the production conditions, and on 15 May 1936 the official decree recognised the Appellation d’Origine Contrôlée. The local growers’ association calls it France’s first wine AOC; to be precise, six AOCs were created that day, so it is fairer to say Châteauneuf was among the very first, and the model on which the others were built.

Le Roy did not stop there: he was one of the founders of INAO, the body that runs France’s appellations, and chaired it from 1947 to 1967. Italy’s DOC system, born in 1963, looked squarely to the French model: behind every Italian *disciplinare*, the production rules of each denomination, lies, at a distance, the regulation of a Provençal baron.

## Thirteen grapes, one in charge

Châteauneuf’s most quoted rule is also its most misunderstood. Traditionally thirteen varieties are permitted: Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre, Cinsault, Counoise, Vaccarèse, Terret Noir and Muscardin among the reds, and Clairette, Bourboulenc, Roussanne, Picpoul and Picardan among the whites. Since 2009 the rules, by distinguishing the white, pink and black forms of the same variety, list eighteen. There is no obligation to use them all, however: the great majority of producers work with a handful, and some use just one.

The one in charge is Grenache, which according to 2004 figures covered 72% of the vineyard area. Anyone who knows Italy will recognise it under another name: in Sardinia it is Cannonau, in Spain Garnacha. It is a grape that loves heat, piles up sugar with ease and gives generous, supple wines scented with ripe red fruit, spice and garrigue, the wild Mediterranean scrub of Provence. Syrah and Mourvèdre add colour, structure and a darker note. The rules set a minimum of 12.5% alcohol and forbid chaptalisation, but in practice the reds often reach 14 or 15 degrees and beyond.

White Châteauneuf exists and is worth seeking out, but it is a rarity: around 5% of production. Almost all Châteauneuf is red.

## The pebbles that warm the night

The image every wine lover associates with Châteauneuf is a carpet of round stones, the size of a fist, from which squat Grenache bush vines emerge. These are the *galets roulés*, pebbles mostly of quartzite carried down by Alpine glaciers and polished over millennia by the Rhône. The classic explanation is that they soak up the sun’s heat by day and release it to the grapes at night, hastening ripening; they also shield the soil from evaporation in dry summers.

But the appellation is not all stones. The galets dominate above all on the plateaux to the north and north-east, over layers of clay; to the east sand prevails, which many believe gives finer, more perfumed reds; to the south the soils are gravelly and looser. That is one reason why talking about “a” Châteauneuf makes little sense. The area, shared between five communes (Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Bédarrides, Courthézon, Orange and Sorgues), covers about 3,200 hectares and counts some 300 growers declaring a harvest.

A curiosity the locals love to tell: in 1954, after a series of alleged sightings of “flying cigars”, the village council passed an ordinance banning flying saucers from flying over or landing in the vineyards. It is said never to have been repealed.

## A classic that needs to find its measure again

My view is that Châteauneuf-du-Pape matters more as an idea than as a style. It taught the world that a wine is first of all a place with rules, and that is a debt that does not expire. The style, on the other hand, has been under scrutiny for some years: rising heat pushes an already generous Grenache towards alcohol levels that threaten to flatten its freshness, and the lure of power long rewarded muscular wines over elegant ones. The answer, in my view, lies in that very list of thirteen grapes that hardly anyone uses in full: more room for Mourvèdre and Cinsault, less tardy harvests, less new oak. If the appellation that invented the rules can also rewrite them for a different climate, it will have taught the world its second lesson.
