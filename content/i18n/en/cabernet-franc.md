Every grand family has a relative who did everything and whom nobody talks about. In wine, that relative is Cabernet Franc. It fathered Cabernet Sauvignon, the most planted and most celebrated red grape on the planet, and Merlot too. Yet in restaurants almost nobody orders a Cabernet Franc by name. People drink it, often without knowing, in a Saint-Émilion, a Loire red, a Tuscan blend. It is time it was properly introduced.

## A paternity revealed in the lab

The revelation came in 1997, when Carole Meredith and John Bowers at the University of California, Davis, showed through DNA analysis that Cabernet Sauvignon was born of a spontaneous cross between Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc. We covered that in our piece on Sauvignon Blanc; here the other half of the story is what counts. Later research identified Cabernet Franc as one of the two parents of Merlot and Carmenère as well. Three of Bordeaux’s emblematic grapes, in short, are its offspring.

Its more distant origins are less clear. Among its synonyms is Achéria, tied to the Basque Country and in particular to Irouléguy, which hints at Pyrenean roots. In Bordeaux, in Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, it was called Bouchet; in the Loire, Breton. Around that last name runs a charming story: Cardinal Richelieu is said to have had cuttings brought to the valley in the 17th century, entrusting them to the abbey of Bourgueil and an abbot named Breton. It is one version, not the official history, but the sources do accept that the grape was present in the Loire long before it reached Bordeaux.

## The character: freshness and leaves

Compared with its famous son, Cabernet Franc buds and ripens at least a week earlier. It sounds like an agronomic footnote and is in fact the key to everything: this is the grape that rescues cool vintages, the one that in Bordeaux secures a ripe crop when Cabernet Sauvignon struggles, and that is at ease in colder climates.

In the glass it is usually less massive than Cabernet Sauvignon, with red fruit more to the fore and that vegetal note which divides drinkers, from leaf to green pepper. When the grapes are unripe, the note becomes a fault and the wine tastes green. When they are ripe, it becomes a signature, a cool shadow that makes the fruit livelier. In warmer countries, not by chance, many growers delay picking precisely to soften it.

## The Loire: Chinon, Bourgueil and the bistro wine

In France Cabernet Franc covers about 33,000 hectares, according to OIV figures from 2017, a little over 4% of the national vineyard. Its spiritual home is the central Loire, where it dominates the reds of Chinon, Bourgueil, Saint-Nicolas-de-Bourgueil and Saumur-Champigny. Bourgueil and Saint-Nicolas-de-Bourgueil are effectively Cabernet Franc fiefdoms, while the Chinon area spreads over 26 communes on both sides of the Vienne, down to its confluence with the Loire.

This is where you grasp how much soil matters. The gravels and sands near the river give lighter reds with fine tannins, to drink young and cool, the classic Parisian bistro wines. On *tuffeau*, the soft pale limestone from which the valley’s cellars and châteaux are carved, the wine grows more powerful and rounded, able to age for a long time. It is the purest expression of the grape: no blending, no tricks, and an acidity that makes these some of the most food-friendly reds in France.

## Saint-Émilion: the secret of Cheval Blanc

On Bordeaux’s Right Bank Cabernet Franc plays second fiddle to Merlot, but in one famous case it takes the lead. Château Cheval Blanc, in Saint-Émilion, farms about 37 hectares on gravel and sandy-clay soils that run up to the border with Pomerol. The vines are 57% Cabernet Franc, 40% Merlot and the rest Cabernet Sauvignon and Malbec: unusual proportions for the Right Bank, and one reason the wine is considered a case apart. In 1998, after a century of family ownership, the estate passed to Bernard Arnault and Albert Frère. Anyone who has seen *Sideways* will remember the 1961 bottle drunk in a fast-food joint, an episode we recounted when writing about Merlot.

## In Italy, under an assumed name

Italy has a long and slightly muddled relationship with Cabernet Franc. By 2000 plantings exceeded 7,000 hectares, concentrated in the north-east: Friuli above all, then the Veneto, where it was once called Bordo. For decades many bottles were labelled simply “Cabernet”, and what was inside was often mostly Cabernet Franc. To complicate matters, the grape was long confused with Carmenère too, so the real extent of the two varieties in Italy is not precisely known. For a foreign reader it is a telling detail: in Friuli the herbaceous, easy-drinking “Cabernet” of the local *osteria*, the neighbourhood tavern, was almost always a Franc in disguise.

In Tuscany the story is different and more recent. At Bolgheri, on the coast, the rules allow Cabernet Franc alongside Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, and Bolgheri Sassicaia includes it as a complement to Cabernet Sauvignon. Further inland, Carmignano DOCG, near Florence, sets aside a share for it next to Sangiovese. The grape is increasingly prized there as a varietal wine too: among the names most often cited are Le Macchiole at Bolgheri, with Paleo, and Tenuta di Trinoro in the Val d'Orcia, while plantings have grown in the Maremma as well. The warm Tuscan climate gives it fuller fruit and softer tannins than in the Loire: a different wine, but a recognisable one.

## Our view

Cabernet Franc pays for a fault it does not have: it is not muscular enough for those who want power, nor exotic enough for those who want discovery. Yet it is perhaps the red that best expresses the climate it grows in, cool and taut in the Loire, deep in Saint-Émilion, sunny in Tuscany. In an age of ever hotter summers, a grape that ripens early and keeps its acidity is no supporting player: it is an insurance policy on the future. The famous son took the name. The father, in his own good time, may yet have the last word.
