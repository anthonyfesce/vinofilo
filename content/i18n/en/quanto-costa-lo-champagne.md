In front of the festive shelf the question is always the same: why does a Champagne cost so much more than a good Italian sparkling wine? The short answer is that Champagne starts from very high costs before it even reaches the cellar, and that part of the price is the name. The long answer is a journey through vineyards, grapes, time and full warehouses, and in the end it also helps you spend better.

## What it really costs

There is no “fair” average price for Champagne, because it ranges from supermarket labels to prestige cuvées costing hundreds of euros. There are, however, a few verifiable reference points. In France, in December 2023, the consumer association [Que Choisir](https://www.quechoisir.org/actualite-inflation-champagnes-des-bulles-de-plus-en-plus-couteuses-n114434/) found in supermarkets an average price of €34.50 for bottles from the big brands, up 16% in two years, and €24.30 for supermarket own-label bottles. For Italy we have not found a recent and reliable public survey of the average shelf price, and we would rather not make one up.

The market, meanwhile, is cooling. According to the Comité Champagne, the region’s interprofessional body, 266 million bottles were shipped in 2025, 1.8% fewer than the year before, as the French farming publication [Pleinchamp](https://www.pleinchamp.com/actualite/les-ventes-de-champagne-en-baisse-pour-la-3eme-annee-consecutive) reported. Italy received 7.8 million, 7% fewer, according to industry data reported by [Vinetur](https://www.vinetur.com/it/italys-champagne-shipments-fell-7-to-78-million-bottles-in-2025.html). And at the end of 2025 the consultancy Wine Lister estimated that average Champagne prices worldwide had fallen by about 12% in three years.

## Land and grapes

The first reason for the price lies underfoot. According to data from the French land agency SAFER reported by [wein.plus](https://magazine.wein.plus/news/prices-for-french-vineyards-fall-again-in-2025-only-burgundy-champagne-and-loire-increase), in 2025 a hectare of vineyard in the Champagne appellation was worth on average about €1.13 million, slightly up while prices were falling across most of France. It is some of the most expensive farmland in the world, and those who own it sell their grapes accordingly.

The grapes, in fact, are extremely expensive. A few years ago the trade press, such as [wein.plus](https://magazine.wein.plus/news/grape-prices-in-champagne-rise-sharply-average-price-at-6-20-euros-per-kilo-for-the-first-time-due-to-high-demand), reported for the first time an average price of €6.20 per kilo, and making one bottle takes about 1.2 kilos, according to the [technical sheet from Champagne Education](https://champagne.education/sites/default/files/2024-05/Factsheet-Champagne-Making-Process-EN.pdf). With those numbers, the grapes alone were worth more than €7 a bottle, before any work in the cellar. And it is a figure from a few years ago: we have not found a more recent official average.

Then there is the yield rule. Every year the region sets how many grapes per hectare may become Champagne: for the 2025 harvest the marketable limit was 9,000 kilos per hectare, down for the third year in a row, as [Pleinchamp](https://www.pleinchamp.com/actualite/le-rendement-commercialisable-du-champagne-fixe-a-9000-kg-ha-en-2025-en-baisse-pour-la-3eme-annee-consecutive) again reported. Fewer grapes on costly land means fixed costs spread over fewer bottles.

## Time in the cellar

The second reason is time. The harvest is by hand, in whole bunches, and pressing follows strict rules: from 4,000 kilos of grapes at most 2,550 litres of must are obtained. Then the wine referments in the bottle and stays on its lees for at least 15 months if non-vintage, at least 36 if vintage; many houses go well beyond. Every bottle takes up space in the cellar, and must be riddled, disgorged, dosed and labelled.

Above all, Champagne lives on reserves. The houses keep wines from previous vintages to keep the style of their blends consistent, and that means huge stocks: according to data reported by [Vinetur](https://www.vinetur.com/en/2025121994390/champagne-prices-drop-12-globally-as-sales-slide-and-inventories-reach-five-year-high.html), at the end of 2024 stocks stood at about 1.279 billion bottles, almost five years of sales. It is tied-up capital, and somebody pays for it.

## The name

The third reason is less tangible. Champagne is a collective name built up over the centuries, and the big houses invest heavily in communication, events and sponsorship. Part of the price, especially for the best-known brands, pays for this: the name on the label and what it evokes. It is not a moral failing, it is the economics of luxury, but it is worth knowing.

## How to spend better

Three practical pointers follow from all this. First: Champagne at a rock-bottom price, below the thresholds seen above, is hard to explain by production costs, and deserves a little suspicion or at least a careful reading of the label. Second: growers’ Champagnes, made by those who farm their own grapes, often let you pay for more vineyard and less marketing. Third: if your budget is that of a sparkling wine, buy an excellent sparkling wine. A Franciacorta, a Trento DOC, an Alta Langa or a Crémant use the same method and, at the same price, in our view often give you more.

Our view is that Champagne is not expensive on a whim: it is expensive because everything up there costs a lot, and because the world is willing to pay for its name. But that is exactly why it is worth buying with intent, not out of reflex. A bottle chosen because you know what is inside is worth its price. One bought only because “that is what you do at New Year” risks being the most expensive and least remembered toast of the year.
