For much of the twentieth century, Zinfandel was the most American wine there was. It did not come from France like Cabernet, nor did it need a foreign name to be pronounced with trepidation: it was the pioneers’ grape, planted in the Gold Rush years, its gnarled old trunks having survived everything. Then genetics sent in the bill. Zinfandel turned out to be the same variety as Puglia’s Primitivo, and both had come from a third country altogether, Croatia. America’s most American wine was carrying a Dalmatian passport.

## A discovery in three stages

We told the scientific story in our piece on Puglia, so a summary will do here. In the late 1960s an American plant pathologist, Austin Goheen, noticed the resemblance between Primitivo and Zinfandel and had Apulian cuttings sent to California. In the 1990s Carole Meredith’s team at the University of California, Davis confirmed by DNA that they were the same grape (the dates given by different sources vary by a few years). In the early 2000s, combing the old vineyards of Dalmatia with colleagues from Zagreb, the researchers found at Kaštel Novi, near Split, a handful of Crljenak Kaštelanski vines with the same genetic profile.

The oldest name, as far as we know, is Tribidrag, recorded in Dalmatian documents of the fifteenth century. On the neighbouring coast of Montenegro the same grape is called Kratošija, and the Montenegrins, for their part, claim the cradle of the variety. The most intriguing detail comes from the Venetian archives, cited by several scholars: a barrel of Tribidrag shipped in 1488 from Dalmatia to Puglia. If the record holds, the grape had already crossed the Adriatic, at least in liquid form, three centuries before anyone called it Primitivo.

## How to become American

The road to California ran, curiously, through the East Coast. In the 1820s a Long Island horticulturist, George Gibbs, received several shipments of vines from Europe, and shortly afterwards a table grape called “Zenfendal”, spelt in assorted ways, was on sale in Boston. In the 1850s the nurserymen who followed the Gold Rush carried it west. The name remains a small mystery: nobody knows for sure where it comes from.

In California the grape found a climate to its liking. According to an 1888 census, more than a third of the state’s vines were Zinfandel. Then came Prohibition, from 1920 to 1933, which partly spared the vineyards thanks to a legal loophole: families were allowed to make wine at home, and the grapes travelled by rail to improvised cellars back East. But Zinfandel’s thin skin did not take kindly to the journey, and many growers replaced it with sturdier varieties.

The second rescue was even more paradoxical. In the 1970s, as the market for red wine slowed, some California wineries began turning Zinfandel into a sweetish pink wine, White Zinfandel, which became a mass phenomenon. Many enthusiasts have always looked down on it, yet it is thanks to White Zinfandel that a good number of old vineyards were not grubbed up and grafted over to more fashionable varieties. Today, on 2019 figures, Zinfandel covers some 39,500 acres in California, around 16,000 hectares, a little over a tenth of the state’s vineyards.

## Same DNA, different vines

If Primitivo and Zinfandel are the same grape, why don’t the wines resemble each other? The first reason is that “same variety” does not mean “same plants”. After centuries of selection in different places, the two populations are distinct clones, lines of vines chosen and propagated for different traits. Primitivo, by definition, is the one that ripens early: that is where the name comes from, *primo* meaning first. Some California growers who farm both report that Primitivo ripens a few days earlier, more evenly and in looser bunches. Zinfandel, by contrast, is notorious for a flaw anyone who works with it knows well: the same bunch can carry berries that are still green alongside others already shrivelling into raisins.

The second reason is landscape. In California, Zinfandel is at its best in places such as Sonoma, the Sierra Foothills, Lodi and Paso Robles, often from old head-trained vines. In Puglia, the heel of the Italian boot, Primitivo lives between two worlds: Primitivo di Manduria, on the hot plain near the sea, and Gioia del Colle, up on the Murge plateau at around 350 metres, with more freshness. In the Italian system both are DOCs, appellations whose rules fix the area, yields and production methods; Primitivo di Manduria Dolce Naturale, the sweet version, has climbed to the top rung, DOCG.

The third reason is culture. California Zinfandel was for a long time the wine of extreme ripeness, with high alcohol and fruit verging on jam. Primitivo felt the same temptation, but with a different history behind it: for decades it travelled north in tankers to beef up other regions’ wines. In both places the newest generation is trying to take weight off, not pile it on.

## The label wars

DNA settled the botanical question and opened the commercial one. In 1999 the European Union recognised Zinfandel as a synonym for Primitivo, and since then a number of Apulian producers have sold bottles in the United States with the word Zinfandel on the label. In the US things are more tangled: the federal authority accepts both names but does not treat them as synonyms, and a proposal to recognise them as such was withdrawn in 2008. For Californians, Zinfandel is national heritage, and the idea of an Italian Primitivo using it as a shortcut has never gone down well.

In Croatia, meanwhile, the variety has come back from almost nothing. From a couple of dozen rediscovered vines it has grown, on a count from a few years ago, to around a hundred hectares, much of it labelled Crljenak. And even some California producers have started writing Tribidrag on their bottles, a nod to the long-lost grandmother.

## Our view

The lesson of this story is not that Primitivo and Zinfandel are “basically the same thing”. It is the opposite. DNA tells you where a grape comes from, not who it has become. Two centuries in Puglia and a century and a half in California have produced two recognisable characters, and Dalmatia is now building a third. Anyone looking for an identical wine under three names will be disappointed. Anyone looking for a natural experiment in how much place, people and time matter has before them one of the finest cases in the world of wine.
