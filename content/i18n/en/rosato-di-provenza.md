Few wines in the world can be recognised from across the room, before anyone has read the label. Provence rosé is one of them: a pink so pale it looks like water barely tinted, onion skin, petal, white peach. This is no mere aesthetic detail. Here, colour has become a promise of style, a kind of collective trademark that nobody ever registered and the whole world copies. And that is the most intriguing paradox of all: a French region has turned into a global model a wine that for decades, almost everywhere, was dismissed as an unambitious halfway house.

## A region that chose pink

Provence was making wine long before France existed. The first vines arrived with the Greek settlers who founded Massalia, today’s Marseille, around 600 BC; the Romans came later, some five centuries on. But today’s identity is far more recent, and it amounts to an almost radical choice. According to figures from the Provence wine trade body, rosé accounted for 89% of the output of the region’s appellations in the 2023 harvest. Whites and reds together come to less than 12%.

An Italian comparison helps put that in perspective. Imagine Piedmont deciding to make almost nothing but Barbera, or Tuscany living off a single style of wine. No major Italian region has ever bet so heavily on one colour. The leading appellation is the AOC Côtes de Provence, recognised in 1977 and alone responsible for about 70% of the region’s volume: a little over 857,000 hectolitres in 2023. Alongside it sit smaller appellations, among them Coteaux d’Aix-en-Provence, Coteaux Varois and, on the coast, Bandol.

The grapes are those of the Mediterranean: Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah, Mourvèdre and Tibouren, a variety found almost nowhere but Provence. Italian drinkers will spot some old friends: Grenache is the same grape as Sardinia’s Cannonau, and Rolle, which in some appellations goes in small amounts into the rosés too, is Italy’s Vermentino.

## Pallor as a method

A rosé’s colour depends on how long the juice stays in contact with the skins. In Provence there are two main approaches: direct pressing, in which the grapes go straight into the press and give up only the little colour released as they are squeezed, and short maceration, from a few hours up to about twenty, at low temperatures. *Saignée* – rosé made by bleeding juice off a tank destined for red wine – is viewed with suspicion: the underlying idea is that a great rosé is designed in the vineyard, not salvaged as a by-product. We have covered how rosés are made, and the Italian schools of thought, in another piece; what matters here is the Provençal point of view.

That approach has an institutional backbone, too. In 1999 a research centre devoted entirely to rosé opened in Vidauban, something that looked almost eccentric at the time. Studying the colour, aromas and stability of a wine many considered minor has proved, in hindsight, a far-sighted investment. Nor are the shades random: according to observations gathered in the region, vineyards near the sea tend to give yellower tones, those inland pinker ones.

## Bandol, the exception that ages

If Côtes de Provence is the rule, Bandol is the exception. On terraces overlooking the sea, west of Toulon, the star is Mourvèdre, a late-ripening, tannic grape that needs sunshine and the proximity of the sea to ripen. A substantial share of Mourvèdre is required in the rosé too; some sources put it at no less than half the blend, though the precise figure should be checked against the current rules. The result is a more structured, more savoury rosé, with a texture that stands up to time. It is the clearest proof that pink does not mean slight: a good Bandol rosé, after a few years in bottle, behaves more like a serious white than a poolside wine.

## A boom in numbers

The international success can be measured. In 2023 Provence exported more than 411,000 hectolitres of rosé – over 55 million bottles once all exports are counted – worth around €347 million. The leading market is the United States, with 37% of export volume and 40% of value; the United Kingdom and the Netherlands follow. Over the past five years, according to the same source, volumes grew by a mere 2% while value rose by 22%: a sign that the world is not simply drinking more Provence, but paying more for it.

And yet the region produces only around 5% of the planet’s rosé, and between 40 and 45% of France’s appellation rosés. Its cultural weight far outstrips its volume. The luxury groups have noticed: Moët Hennessy took a majority stake in one of the region’s best-known estates, a move that would have been unthinkable when rosé was seen as the wine for people who could not make up their minds. The flip side is imitation. Across half the world, from Lake Garda to California, rosés have grown paler and paler, sometimes out of conviction, sometimes simply to look like Provence on the shelf.

## Not just a summer wine

One prejudice dies hard: rosé as a seasonal drink, opened in June and forgotten by September. It makes little sense. A dry, savoury rosé with acidity and a modicum of structure is one of the most versatile wines at the table: it works with fish and Mediterranean cooking, but also with spiced dishes, with vegetables, with any meal that calls for freshness without tannin. In winter, when the table grows richer, a rosé with some body, such as those from Bandol, can be a smarter choice than a heavy red.

My view is that Provence has won one important battle and risks losing another. It has shown that rosé can be a wine of place, with rules, research and identity. But it has also turned pallor into a uniform, and a uniform is easy to copy. The most interesting future lies not in ever paler pink but in the differences: between sea and hillside, between Grenache and Mourvèdre, between a rosé to drink now and one to wait for. And anyone who drinks it only in summer, frankly, is missing half the story.
