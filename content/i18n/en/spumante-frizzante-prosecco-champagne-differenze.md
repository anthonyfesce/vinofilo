In December the question comes up in every supermarket and every wine shop: are *spumante* and Prosecco the same thing? And *frizzante*? And is Champagne just a more expensive spumante? The confusion is understandable, because these words sit on different levels. “Spumante” and “frizzante” are product categories, the Italian terms for sparkling and semi-sparkling wine, defined by the pressure of the bubbles. “Prosecco” and “Champagne” are designations of origin, that is, names of places with rules of their own. A Prosecco can be spumante or frizzante; a Champagne is always a sparkling wine; not every spumante is Prosecco or Champagne.

## Spumante and frizzante: a matter of pressure

The difference between the two categories is made by European law, with a pressure gauge. [Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/IT/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013R1308) defines sparkling wine as wine which, in a closed bottle at 20 degrees, has an excess pressure due to carbon dioxide of at least 3 bar; for quality sparkling wine the threshold rises to 3.5 bar. Semi-sparkling wine, by contrast, has an excess pressure between 1 and 2.5 bar, and at least 7% actual alcohol.

In terms of sensation: spumante has a real mousse, bubbles that rise dense and persistent, and a mushroom cork held by a wire cage. Frizzante has lighter, less insistent bubbles, and often an ordinary cork or a screwcap. Champagne, to give an idea, referments in the bottle up to about 6 bar.

Then there is a lesser-known distinction. “Aerated sparkling wine” and “aerated semi-sparkling wine” are those in which carbon dioxide has been added, wholly or partly, as in a soft drink, and they must say so. In true sparkling and semi-sparkling wines, by contrast, the bubbles come from a fermentation.

## Traditional method and Martinotti

Within sparkling wines, the great divide concerns how the bubbles are born. In the traditional method, *Metodo Classico* in Italian, the method of Champagne, Franciacorta and Trento DOC, the base wine referments in the very bottle in which it will be sold, and stays for a long time in contact with the dead yeasts, which give it notes of bread crust and fine bubbles. In the Martinotti method, which abroad is called Charmat, the second fermentation takes place in large steel pressure tanks, in a shorter time, and the wine above all keeps the fresh fruit of the grape. It is not a hierarchy of quality but of style: we told the story [in our comparison of the two methods](/en/articles/2026.10.05/metodo-classico-vs-charmat-sparkling-wine/).

## Prosecco: one denomination, three versions

Prosecco is a designation of origin from north-eastern Italy, between Veneto and Friuli Venezia Giulia, with the hills of Conegliano Valdobbiadene and of Asolo as its historic DOCG zones. The main grape is Glera. Under the Prosecco DOC rules it must make up at least 85%; in the rosé, introduced in 2020, Glera accounts for 85 to 90% and the rest is Pinot Nero.

The point that explains the confusion is that the rules provide for three types: Prosecco spumante, by far the most widespread, made in pressure tanks; Prosecco frizzante, which may also referment in the bottle, in which case the label must say so; and still Prosecco, without bubbles, which exists but is rarely found. So yes, Prosecco is almost always a spumante. But saying “spumante” to mean “Prosecco” is like saying “cheese” to mean “Parmigiano Reggiano”.

## Champagne: a place, a method, a time

Champagne is a French designation, in the region of the same name east of Paris, and its name is protected: no other wine may use it in the European Union. It is made by the traditional method, mainly from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. The grapes are pressed as whole bunches, and the rule is precise: from 4,000 kilos of grapes at most 2,550 litres of must are obtained, according to the [technical sheet from Champagne Education](https://champagne.education/sites/default/files/2024-05/Factsheet-Champagne-Making-Process-EN.pdf), the region’s training programme. Before release, a non-vintage Champagne spends at least 15 months in the cellar on its lees, a vintage one at least 36.

Champagne, then, is a sparkling wine, but not every traditional-method sparkling wine is Champagne. A Franciacorta, a Trento DOC, an Alta Langa, a French Crémant or a Spanish Cava use the same method, in other places and with other rules.

## And the other frizzanti?

Italy is full of semi-sparkling wines that have nothing to do with Prosecco: Lambrusco, in its various denominations, Gragnano from the Sorrento peninsula, the frizzante Gutturnio of the Colli Piacentini, Moscato d’Asti, which may not exceed 2.5 bar. They are wines with a history and a cuisine of their own, often better suited to the table than many sparkling wines, and they deserve to be called by name.

## How to choose

To find your way, it is enough to keep the hierarchy of words in mind. On the label, the category (spumante, frizzante) tells you how many bubbles to expect; the denomination (Prosecco, Franciacorta, Champagne) tells you where it comes from and under which rules it is made; the dosage term (brut, extra dry) tells you how sweet it is; the method, when stated, tells you how the bubbles were born.

Our view is that the question “spumante or Champagne?” is badly put. The right question is: what kind of bubbles do I want tonight? Fresh and fruity for the aperitif, creamy and complex for dinner, light and gently fizzy for a plate of cured meats. At that point the words on the label stop being an exam and become a map, and Champagne goes back to being what it is: one of the world’s great sparkling wines, not the only serious way to raise a glass.
