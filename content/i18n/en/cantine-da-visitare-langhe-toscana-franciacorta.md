For many Italians, the long weekend of the Immaculate Conception on 8 December is the last chance of the year for a trip among the vines. The leaves have fallen, the harvest is a memory, the new wines are resting in tank and cask, and the hills have that low, sharp light you never see in summer. It is also the moment when the question “which wineries should I visit?” becomes concrete. We will not draw up a ranking of estates, which would inevitably be a list of preferences: instead we will try to suggest where to go, what to see besides the wineries, and how to organise yourself in three of Italy’s most sought-after areas.

## Langhe: start from a castle

Since 2014 the vineyard landscapes of Langhe-Roero and Monferrato have been a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and first-time visitors do well to begin with a vantage point rather than a winery. The most obvious is Barolo, where the municipal Castello Falletti houses [WiMu](https://www.wimubarolo.it/), an interactive wine museum designed by François Confino, with the Enoteca Regionale del Barolo, the regional wine shop, in the castle cellars. In winter the opening hours are shorter, and the museum is closed on 24 and 25 December: it is worth checking before you set off.

A few kilometres away, the castle of Grinzane Cavour houses the Enoteca Regionale Piemontese and is linked to Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, who was mayor of the village for years; at its foot lies the Vigna Gustava, the vineyard that belonged to him. For an introduction to the wines of the whole area, the regional *enoteche* are an excellent start: you taste, you compare, and you choose which wineries to see afterwards.

Barolo is made in eleven municipalities, Barbaresco in a smaller area north-east of Alba: moving between La Morra, Monforte, Serralunga and Barbaresco takes only a few minutes by car, but the roads are narrow and in winter fog is frequent. This year’s long weekend also coincides with the last weekend of the Alba International White Truffle Fair, which closes on 6 December.

Those with an extra half-day can push on to Canelli, the historic heart of Moscato, known for the cellars dug beneath the hill, which are also part of the UNESCO site.

## Tuscany: three areas, three different trips

Wine Tuscany cannot be seen in a weekend. Better to choose one area.

**Chianti Classico.** It is the easiest to reach from Florence and Siena. According to the [Consorzio Vino Chianti Classico](https://www.chianticlassico.com/), the denomination fully includes the municipalities of Castellina, Gaiole, Greve and Radda in Chianti, and partly those of Barberino Tavarnelle, Castelnuovo Berardenga, Poggibonsi and San Casciano in Val di Pesa. The SR 222, the Chiantigiana road, runs through the heart of the area and is the natural thread of any itinerary.

**Montalcino.** Further south, a single hill and a single great wine, Brunello. The [Consorzio del Vino Brunello di Montalcino](https://www.consorziobrunellodimontalcino.it/) has its headquarters in the town, in Via Boldrini, and the hilltop town with its fortress is already a reason to go. In November the Consorzio presents the new vintages with Benvenuto Brunello; in December the town is quieter, which is an advantage for those visiting wineries.

**Bolgheri.** On the coast of the province of Livorno, it is the home of the Super Tuscans. This is where the story of Sassicaia began, the wine of [Tenuta San Guido](https://www.tenutasanguido.com/) that became an autonomous denomination, Bolgheri Sassicaia DOC, in 2013. The landscape is different from that of the inland hills: plain, Mediterranean scrub, the light of the sea and the long avenue of cypresses leading to the village of Bolgheri, made famous by the verses of Carducci.

For those who also want to reach Montepulciano, the [Consorzio del Vino Nobile](https://www.consorziovinonobile.it/) is the starting point for understanding the denomination.

## Franciacorta: sparkling wine half an hour from Brescia

Franciacorta is an amphitheatre of moraine hills between the southern shore of Lake Iseo and Brescia, very convenient from Milan. Its wine history is recent and precisely documented: in 1961 [Guido Berlucchi](https://www.berlucchi.it/), with the oenologist Franco Ziliani, produced the first bottles of what was still called Pinot di Franciacorta. The DOC came in 1967, the Consorzio in 1990, and the DOCG, reserved for sparkling wines, in 1995. Many wineries offer visits by appointment, and the lake a few minutes away lets you combine a day in the vineyards with a walk by the water or a trip to Monte Isola.

Visiting a traditional-method winery has one more advantage when it is cold outside: the most fascinating part of the process, among riddling racks, *remuage* and disgorgement, takes place indoors, underground, among the bottles.

## How to organise your visit

Rule number one applies everywhere: always book. Italy’s national wine-tourism guidelines of 2019 ask for it, and it is in your interest, because you can find out in advance what the visit includes, how long it lasts and whether the tasting is paid. We covered this in detail [in our guide to visiting a winery without embarrassing yourself](/en/articles/2026.11.03/how-to-visit-a-winery/).

For those who prefer events, the [Movimento Turismo del Vino](https://www.movimentoturismovino.it/), which brings together more than 800 wineries across all Italian regions, organises several special openings during the year: the best known is Cantine Aperte, at the end of May, but there is also a Christmas edition. And then there is the question no one wants to face: whoever drives does not drink. A designated driver, a taxi or a transfer service, and a spittoon used without shame, are as much a part of a good visit as the glass.

Our view is that winter is the most underrated season for wine tourism. Fewer people, more time for questions, wineries where real work is going on rather than just tour groups being welcomed. The bare vineyard shows things that summer foliage hides: the slopes, the exposures, the shape of the rows. Anyone who wants to understand why a wine is the way it is will see more clearly in December.
