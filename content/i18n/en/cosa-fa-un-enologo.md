At every family lunch there is someone who, on learning that you are an *enologo*, hands you the bottle and asks: “So, what’s it like?” As if your job were handing out marks. In reality the enologo does taste, yes, but above all decides, measures, corrects and signs off. This is the person who turns a harvest into a wine and answers for what ends up in the bottle, legally included. It is worth understanding the role better, because in Italy mixing up the enologo, the sommelier and the agronomist is almost a national sport.

## A title with more than a century of history

The profession has old roots, and Italian ones. The country’s first school of oenology opened in Conegliano in 1876, and in 1891 Arturo Marescalchi, Antonio Carpenè and 46 colleagues founded the Società degli Enotecnici Italiani, which in 1991 took its current name, Assoenologi, the Association of Italian Oenologists and Oenological Technicians. That same year Law 129 recognised the title of enologo. Before then, as historical accounts recall, the qualification was mostly earned through a period of practice in a winery, usually three years.

Today there are two main routes. There is the *enotecnico*, trained at technical agricultural institutes with a specialisation in viticulture and oenology. And there is the enologo proper, who goes through university: several Italian universities offer degrees in viticulture and oenology which, after three years of study, give access to the qualification. The finer points of the requirements set out in the 1991 law, including the transitional rules for those already practising, should be checked against the official text: here I stick to what I have been able to verify.

One point is often misunderstood: there is no professional order of oenologists with a state examination, as there is for doctors or lawyers. The profession falls among those not organised into orders, governed by Law 4 of 2013, and Assoenologi has been recognised within that framework as a representative association. According to the association, it has almost 4,500 members, about 85% of the wine technicians working in Italy. Since 1893 it has published the magazine now called L'Enologo, born as L'Enotecnico, and it has held a national congress every year since 1946.

## From vineyard to bottle

Assoenologi describes the work as a chain running from growing the vine to selling the wine. It is a broad definition, and it matches reality in small and medium-sized wineries, where the same person oversees almost everything.

It starts in the vineyard, before harvest: the enologo assesses grape quality, samples the berries, measures sugars and acidity, and helps choose the picking date, a subject we covered in our piece on how the harvest day is decided. Then comes the transformation: crushing or pressing, fermentation, temperature control, racking off the skins, malolactic, blending, ageing and bottling. Every choice, from the type of yeast to the vessel, bears the enologo’s signature.

This is where responsibility comes in. European rules on oenological practices, under Delegated Regulation 2019/934, require these operations to be carried out under the supervision of an oenologist or a qualified technician. This is no bureaucratic detail: it means that if a wine is corrected, fined, stabilised or acidified, there is a competent person who answers for it. The same spirit runs through the analytical and sensory examinations that wines with a denomination must pass, governed by a 2019 ministerial decree, a field in which the enologo is entirely at home.

## The laboratory, where wine becomes numbers

The least romantic and most decisive part of the job happens among test tubes and instruments. Chemical, physical and microbiological analyses are used to follow fermentation, dose sulphur dioxide, keep volatile acidity in check and head off faults before they become irreparable. Alongside them sits sensory analysis, which the enologo practises with a different aim from the sommelier’s: not to describe a finished wine for the person drinking it, but to understand a wine in the making in order to decide what to do next.

Large estates have in-house laboratories; small ones often rely on outside labs. Either way, traceability, food safety and compliance are as much part of the work as taste. Anyone who pictures the enologo as an artist with a nose in the glass forgets that a good part of the day is spent with an analysis sheet in hand.

## In-house and consultant

A useful distinction concerns the relationship with the estate. There is the in-house enologo, an employee who lives the cellar every day. And there is the consultant, who works with several estates, turns up at key moments, sets the style and leaves day-to-day execution to the in-house team. In Italy some consultants have become as famous as the wines they signed: the most often cited is Giacomo Tachis, credited by several sources as decisive in the birth of Bolgheri’s great reds, as we recount in the dedicated article.

The consultant model has obvious advantages, such as experience gathered across many regions, and an equally obvious risk: technically flawless wines that all taste a little alike. The debate over the homogenisation of styles, which has followed Italian wine for at least thirty years, runs through here too.

## Enologo, agronomist, sommelier

The three roles intersect, but they do not overlap. The agronomist, the *dottore agronomo* registered with the professional order, deals with land and plants in the broad sense: soils, planting, crop protection, vineyard management, often project work and expert reports too. The enologo knows the vineyard, but the field is the transformation of grapes and the quality of the wine. The sommelier works downstream, in restaurants and communication: choosing, storing, serving, pairing and explaining wine to the customer. We have explained how to become a sommelier in a separate piece, and the difference in path is stark: association courses on one side, technical and scientific study on the other.

In small wineries the boundaries blur, and the same grower can be a little of all three. But when it comes to technical responsibility for the wine, there is only one right name.

## A profession that deserves more credit

My view is that the enologo is the most important and least celebrated figure in Italian wine. When a wine moves us we tend to thank the place, the grape or the producer, and almost never the person who decided when to pick, how much to extract and when to stop. Perhaps because the best work in a cellar is the work nobody notices. Next time someone at the table asks you “so, what’s it like?”, remind them that the enologo is not there to give it a mark. The enologo is there so that the wine exists at all.
