There was a moment, in the 1960s, when Malvasia delle Lipari was more legend than wine. The production rules themselves, not usually given to drama, put it in black and white: from around 10,000 hectolitres in the nineteenth century, output had fallen to a low of about 200. A handful of barrels scattered across the islands, for a wine that a century earlier had been praised by travellers and given an award in Paris. That we can still drink it today is down to collective stubbornness, and to a few outsiders who fell in love with an island.

## Seven islands, one wine

The denomination embraces the whole Aeolian archipelago, off the Tyrrhenian coast of Sicily in the province of Messina: Lipari, Salina, Vulcano, Panarea, Stromboli, Filicudi and Alicudi. In practice, though, the vines are mostly on Salina, followed by Lipari and Vulcano. Salina is the greenest island of the group, formed by two long-extinct volcanoes, its slopes long terraced with vineyards.

The soils are volcanic, loose, rich in pumice and ash. Sun is plentiful, the wind constant, summer rain scarce. These are ideal conditions for a grape destined to be dried, and here *appassimento* is not an imported technique but the most natural way to make wine.

## Malvasia, but which one?

In Italy the word Malvasia is a trap. It covers a family of different grapes united by a name that probably harks back to Monemvasia, the Peloponnese port from which the Venetians traded sweet wines. Malvasia from Collio is something else, Malvasia di Candia from Lazio something else again. The Aeolian grape is called Malvasia di Lipari, an aromatic variety with an intense scent of apricot, flowers and candied citrus. Some genetic studies have linked it, to the point of identity, with Malvasia di Sardegna and Calabria’s Greco di Bianco: an appealing hypothesis that would trace a Mediterranean route, but one we flag here with caution.

The rules allow up to 95% Malvasia di Lipari and require a small share of Corinto Nero, between 5 and 8%. It is a curious detail, a red grape in a sweet white wine, which island tradition has chosen to preserve in writing.

## The canizzi and the sun

The heart of Malvasia delle Lipari is the passito. The production rules describe the method with almost recipe-book precision: overripe grapes, selected bunches, drying in the sun on cane racks, the *canizzi*, for between 10 and 20 days, then pressing and long fermentations in small vessels. Anyone who has read our story of Pantelleria will recognise the logic but not the landscape: there it is the wind that dries the Zibibbo, here it is above all the sun.

The rules convey concentration better than any adjective. For the passito the maximum yield of wine is 45% of the weight of the grapes, against 70% for the basic wine: more than half the fruit disappears as evaporated water. Total alcohol must reach at least 18% vol, counting both fermented alcohol and residual sugar, and the bottle may not be released before 1 June after the harvest.

There are two further versions. The white, which the rules simply call Malvasia delle Lipari, is made from overripe grapes without the long drying in the sun, and is the lighter style. The liquoroso, fortified with alcohol, must reach at least 20% vol and rest for at least six months after fortification. Yet it is the passito that made the name famous.

## From phylloxera to revival

The history runs deep. Greek colonists arrived on Lipari around 580 BC, and the island’s coins of the fifth and fourth centuries BC bear vine shoots and bunches of grapes. Over the centuries Malvasia drew the attention of naturalists and writers: the rules recall, among others, the physician Andrea Bacci in 1596 and Guy de Maupassant in 1890. In 1900 came an award at the Paris Exposition.

Then the collapse. At the beginning of the twentieth century phylloxera, the insect that devastated Europe’s vineyards, reached the Aeolian Islands too, and recovery was slow. The terraced vineyards, gruelling to work, were abandoned. That is how output came to that low of 200 hectolitres in the 1960s.

The figure most closely tied to the revival is Carlo Hauner, born in Brescia in 1927, a painter who exhibited at the Venice Biennale, later a designer, with a spell in Brazil alongside the architect Lina Bo Bardi. Having settled on Salina, he began making wine and became its best-known face, to the point that some credit him with inventing modern Malvasia. He died in 1996. The exact dates of his arrival on the island and of the start of his winemaking vary between sources, and we prefer not to pin them down. The DOC was recognised by a decree in 1973, and the rules place the real recovery in production from the late 1980s; the most recent text puts output at around 800-900 hectolitres. Still small numbers, but a long way from the brink of extinction.

## A sweet wine that is not a dessert

In the glass, passito Malvasia is amber, scented with dried apricot, fig, honey, candied orange peel and Mediterranean scrub. Its virtue, when well made, is the freshness that keeps the sweetness from turning cloying. It suits almond sweets and dry Sicilian pastries, but it also stands up to aged and piquant cheeses.

My view is that Malvasia delle Lipari should be rescued from its role as a souvenir. Too often it is bought on holiday on Salina and forgotten in the larder, like a memory of summer. Instead it is one of Italy’s great sweet wines, bound to a landscape that without the vine would already have disappeared. Drinking it is the simplest way to keep those terraces standing.
