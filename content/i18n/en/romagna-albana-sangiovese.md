For many Italians, Romagna wine is a summer memory: the house Sangiovese in a bar on the Adriatic riviera, the sweetish Albana that grandmother served with her ring cake, a flask passed round the table with nobody asking where it came from. It is an affectionate picture, and precisely for that reason a hard one to shake off. Yet you only need to drive a few kilometres up from the via Emilia towards the Apennines, somewhere between Imola and Rimini, to realise that those hills are telling another story, far more ambitious than the riviera would have you believe.

## Albana, the top of the class that nobody understood

Let us begin with the white, because it holds a record few people remember. Albana gained its DOC in 1967 and, in 1987, became Italy’s first white DOCG, under the name Albana di Romagna at the time. Since 2011 it has been called Romagna Albana, a detail that looks like bureaucracy but says a good deal: place before grape.

That 1987 promotion was greeted with more raised eyebrows than applause. As we noted when writing about tortellini in brodo, there are still those who see it as a political decision rather than a qualitative one. The point is that Albana was then mostly a soft, easy wine, and the highest rung of the Italian quality pyramid seemed excessive for a white you drank with an afternoon snack.

Today the rules require at least 95% Albana and allow a range few Italian whites can match: dry, off-dry, sweet, *passito* and passito Riserva. The last is the most demanding: the grapes must be affected by noble rot and reach a minimum potential alcohol of 24%. The dry version, though, is where the reputation is won or lost. That is where you find out whether Albana is a teatime wine or a proper dinner white, able to sit alongside the stuffed pastas and white meats of Romagna’s kitchen.

The problem is scale. According to figures compiled by Italian Wine Central, in 2021 Romagna Albana covered about 232 hectares, with average production of around 7,440 hectolitres. Those are small-denomination numbers, which explain why it is so hard to find outside the region, and why those who make it well treat it almost as a rarity.

## The Sangiovese that no longer wants to be the poor relation

The red is another matter: here the numbers are large and the reputation, for decades, small. Sangiovese di Romagna has always suffered by comparison with Tuscany, as if serious wine were made on the other side of the Apennines and drinking wine on this side. A hierarchy, it must be said, built more on fame than on what is in the glass: the same grape, on different soils and at different altitudes, gives different wines, and Romagna simply took longer to explain itself.

The formal turning point came with the 2011 harvest, when the single Romagna denomination came into force, merging the old DOCs and requiring at least 85% Sangiovese for the basic wine. It is a useful simplification: one name, one territory, one identity.

## Sixteen subzones and a map worth learning

The most interesting part, however, lies beneath the denomination. Romagna Sangiovese may carry on its label one of the additional geographical mentions, the subzones, which single out hills with a character of their own. There were twelve: Serra, Brisighella, Oriolo, Marzeno, Modigliana, Castrocaro, Predappio, Meldola, Bertinoro, Cesena, Mercato Saraceno and Longiano. A ministerial decree of 6 June 2022 raised the number to sixteen, adding Imola, Verucchio, Coriano and San Clemente. Each takes its name from the largest commune in the area.

For subzone wines the rules raise the bar: at least 95% Sangiovese and lower maximum yields, 90 quintals per hectare and 80 for the Riserva. These are not spectacular constraints, but they point the way: less fruit, more identity.

Anyone living in Italy can try a simple exercise. Take two bottles from different subzones, perhaps one from the higher hills towards Tuscany and one nearer the sea, and drink them side by side. Geography that looks like a list of villages on paper tends to become recognisable in the glass: often more tension and finesse on one side, more warmth and fruit on the other. It is the same logic that made the Langhe or Chianti Classico great, applied to a region that until recently had not bothered to tell its story this way.

## The revival has a collective name

In 2022 the Consorzio launched Rocche di Romagna, a collective trademark designed for subzone and subzone Riserva Sangiovese. The name is not new: since the 1970s people had spoken of “Rocche” to mean Romagna’s crus, a turn of phrase that summons up the fortresses on the hilltops and, with them, the idea of a wine that stands tall. Later the Consorzio had also registered a “Rocca” mark, to be used on labels of wines scoring over 80 points in the blind tastings of the certification panels.

Trademarks alone do not make wines, of course. But this insistence on the hill, on the subzone, on the name of the village, signals a change of mindset that regular visitors to the region have sensed for years: growers working specific vineyards, cutting yields, giving up the chase for soft, concentrated reds and seeking instead the transparency Sangiovese is capable of. The past fifteen years, with the single DOC of 2011 and the expansion of the subzones in 2022, have given that work a frame.

## A view, for those who live two hours’ drive away

My view is that Romagna is today one of the Italian regions where the image lags furthest behind the wines. For drinkers that is an advantage, and it will not last for ever.

For anyone living in Italy it is also an easy trip: a weekend in the hills of Brisighella, Bertinoro or Modigliana costs less than a journey to Burgundy and teaches, in its own way, the same lesson about the value of place. Subzone Sangiovese deserves to be drunk with the same respect we keep for the Tuscans. And dry Albana deserves a second chance from those who dismissed it as the sweet wine of the holidays: Italy’s first white DOCG, almost forty years on, is finally starting to earn the title.
