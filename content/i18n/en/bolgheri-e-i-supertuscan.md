There is a paradox that nobody in Bolgheri tires of telling: for years, the wines that made this stretch of Tuscan coast famous were not, on paper, even quality wines. Their labels carried the humblest wording in the Italian repertoire, "vino da tavola", table wine. The system judged them out of line. At a certain point, the market decided they were the line to follow.

## The original sin: the wrong grapes in the wrong place

Until the early 1970s, the Maremma around Bolgheri produced ordinary reds and whites and was hardly a place anyone looked to for great red wine. Then there is the story of a marquis. Mario Incisa della Rocchetta, at Tenuta San Guido, planted Cabernet Sauvignon with the idea of making a wine in the Bordeaux manner. Sources disagree on the exact year of planting (the 1940s, somewhere between 1944 and 1948); they agree on one fact: for years the wine stayed in the family, and the first vintage to reach the market was 1968, released in 1971.

The point is that, in the regulatory design of the day, that wine had nowhere to go. Tuscan rules discouraged non-native grape varieties, and historic denominations such as Chianti imposed recipes that the most ambitious producers found confining. In that same year, 1971, Piero Antinori released Tignanello as a table wine, having given up the white grapes that Chianti Classico would have required. Two different roads, one paradox: to do better, you had to leave the denomination.

It was an English journalist, Nicolas Belfrage, who named the phenomenon in the mid-1980s: Super Tuscan. A convenient label, never a legal category, and indeed the term appears in no production rulebook.

## 1978 and a coast that changes its reputation

To understand how a table wine could earn international credit, you need a date: 1978. At a tasting organised by Decanter, a panel that included Hugh Johnson, Serena Sutcliffe and Clive Coates placed the 1972 Sassicaia first among thirty-three wines from eleven countries. It was not the only reason for the success, but it was a calling card that no advertising campaign could have bought.

In its wake, the coast filled with wineries and vineyards. The why matters here: a Mediterranean climate tempered by the sea, the breezes, the variety of soils, from gravels to clay and sandier ground, allow the Bordeaux varieties to ripen with regularity. Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc and Merlot found in Bolgheri a second home, far less improbable than it seemed. The work of the oenologist Giacomo Tachis, who collaborated with San Guido, is also cited by several sources as decisive in defining the style.

## From table wine to DOC: bureaucracy gives chase

The Italian system, meanwhile, had to acknowledge that facts had overtaken it. In 1992 the indicazione geografica tipica, the IGT, was created precisely to give a home to wines outside the traditional rulebooks; IGT Toscana followed by decree in 1995. But in Bolgheri the game was played at another table.

The Bolgheri DOC already existed as a denomination for whites and rosés (sources give 1983 or 1984; I have no certain date on that detail). The decisive step came in 1994, when the rules for the reds were approved: the area's great wines, until then without a denomination, finally entered the legal frame. The rulebook leaves producers wide latitude: the Rosso can be built from Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc and Merlot in free proportions, with limits on Sangiovese and Syrah. The Superiore requires at least two years of ageing, one of them in wood.

What strikes you on rereading the rulebook is its nature: it imposes no recipe, it welcomes a practice. It is a denomination written after the fact, to describe what was already being done.

## The Sassicaia case: a DOC for a single estate

In 1994 the most singular episode also occurred. Sassicaia obtained a subzone of the Bolgheri DOC, and in 2013 it became an autonomous denomination: Bolgheri Sassicaia DOC. The rulebook calls for at least 80% Cabernet Sauvignon, with the remainder Cabernet Franc, and 24 months of ageing, at least 18 of them in barrels under 225 litres. The peculiarity, acknowledged by every source consulted, is that only one estate has the right to use it, Tenuta San Guido: a denomination that coincides with a single winery. Italy has no other like it.

You can argue about whether it is an anomaly or a masterpiece of realism. I lean towards the second: for once, the law chose to certify a fact rather than deny it.

## After the myth: what remains of Bolgheri

The most lasting effect of the Super Tuscans was not a single wine but a change of mentality. They showed that rules could be rewritten by those who had put them to the test. Today Bolgheri has a planted area that sources put at around 970 hectares (2021 figure), a small denomination compared with the great Tuscan names, but with a disproportionate recognisability.

There is, however, a risk, and it is the risk of every myth: uniformity. A Bordeaux-style blend on the Tuscan coast is a brilliant idea the first time and a formula the hundredth. The interesting question for the coming years is not whether Bolgheri can make Cabernet and Merlot (it has proved that for fifty years), but whether it can make something that resembles itself more than it resembles Bordeaux. The history of the Super Tuscans teaches that, in Bolgheri, the rules have always arrived after the wine. It would be nice if, this time, the wine did not stop to wait for them.
