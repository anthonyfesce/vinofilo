Few wines carry such a demanding name. Lacryma Christi, the tears of Christ: a label that promises the sacred, grows on the slopes of the most famous volcano in the world and looks down on the ruins of a city buried in ash. With credentials like these, the risk is that the wine becomes a souvenir, the bottle you take home after a day trip to Pompeii. For a long time that is exactly what happened. Today it deserves a second, more attentive look, because beneath the legend lies a real territory, and a rather extraordinary one.

## Two legends and a name

There are at least two stories about the origin of the name, and they contradict each other with true Neapolitan cheerfulness. In the first, Lucifer, cast out of Heaven, makes off with a piece of it out of spite, and from the tear Vesuvius rises; Christ, recognising in the Bay of Naples the paradise that was lost, weeps, and where his tears fall the vines spring up. In another, simpler version, it is God himself who, looking down on this land, is moved by its beauty and makes it fertile with his weeping.

Neither explains very much, but both say something true: those who lived here always felt Vesuvius to be a place both blessed and dangerous. The name is old and famous; the denomination, by contrast, is recent. The Vesuvio DOC, within which Lacryma Christi is a specific designation, was recognised in 1983, and until then the wine had a fairly modest reputation, for all the fame of its name.

## Piedirosso and Caprettone, the leading players

The production rules clearly distinguish two souls. Lacryma Christi red and rosé must contain at least 50% Piedirosso, with Sciascinoso, Olivella and Aglianico up to a maximum of 30% and other grapes up to 20%. Piedirosso is the most typical red of the Naples area: it is also called *Per’ e Palummo*, pigeon’s foot, because its reddish stalks recall the feet of pigeons. It gives wines of moderate colour, gentle tannin, scents of cherry and geranium and an almost smoky note that many associate with volcanic soil. It is no muscle-bound red, and that is precisely its charm: it is drunk cool, alongside a pizza or a plate of oily fish, with no inferiority complex whatsoever.

The white rests on Caprettone and Coda di Volpe, which together must make up at least 45%, with Falanghina and Greco up to 35%. Here there is a curious story. For decades Caprettone was considered a mere variant of Coda di Volpe, the grape whose elongated bunch recalls, as the name says, a fox’s tail. DNA analysis then showed they are two distinct varieties, and today Caprettone can appear on its own on the label, with at least 85% of the grapes. It is a white that holds moderate acidity even in the Campanian heat, with notes of citrus, Mediterranean herbs and a saltiness that seems to come from the stone.

The rules also provide for sparkling, fortified (*liquoroso*) and passito versions, plus a Superiore category with lower yields: a sign that the denomination has tried to raise the bar, at least on paper.

## Vines on a volcano that does not sleep

The detail not to forget is that Vesuvius is an active volcano. Its last eruption was in 1944, and the cone that towers over Naples is monitored day and night. The DOC’s vineyards are spread across a ring of communes around the mountain: the whole of Boscotrecase, Trecase and San Sebastiano al Vesuvio, and parts of a dozen others, from Ercolano and Torre del Greco to Terzigno, Ottaviano and Somma Vesuviana.

The soils are sands, ash and lapilli, loose and free-draining, rich in minerals and poor in clay. The rules also describe a higher band, above 200 metres, called the Alto Colle Vesuviano, where the air is breezier and the nights cooler. Anyone who has read our portrait of Etna will find certain affinities: lava, altitude, viticulture that is often heroic and fragmented. But Vesuvius is a lower, more urban volcano, besieged by one of the most densely populated areas in Europe, and its vineyards live alongside houses, roads and warehouses in a way that is hard to imagine on Etna.

## Pompeii, where wine returned to the excavations

Then there is Pompeii, at once the memory and the proof of Vesuvian wine. Before the eruption of AD 79 the volcano’s slopes were planted with vines, and wine held a prominent place in the life and trade of the city. In 1996 the archaeological authority of Pompeii entrusted a historic Campanian winery, Mastroberardino, with replanting some vineyards inside the excavated area, on about one and a half hectares of volcanic soil. Piedirosso and Sciascinoso were planted, later Aglianico too, with training systems inspired by Roman techniques. Since 2001 that experiment has produced a wine, Pompeiano IGT Villa dei Misteri.

Beyond the single label, the project has enormous symbolic value: it reminds us that today’s grapes are the heirs, direct or not, of a viticulture that has existed here for at least two thousand years and that not even a catastrophe could erase.

## Time to stop treating it as a souvenir

My view is that Lacryma Christi has suffered precisely because of its name. Too beautiful, too well known, too easy to sell to tourists: for years the label was enough, and the wine in the glass could be anything at all. Today things are different, partly thanks to more precise rules and to grapes finally recognised for what they are, such as Caprettone. But the decisive leap will come when we stop buying it as a memento of a day out and start ordering it in restaurants, in Naples as in Milan, for the same reason we order an Etna: because it tastes of a place like no other.
