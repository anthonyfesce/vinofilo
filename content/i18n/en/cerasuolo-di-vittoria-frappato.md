Ask someone to picture a Sicilian red and they will almost always describe a dark, warm wine, full of ripe fruit and sunshine. That is the image Nero d'Avola has built around the world, and it is not wrong. But in the south-eastern corner of the island, between Ragusa and Gela, another idea of Sicily has existed for centuries: a pale, fragrant, almost translucent red that asks for an ice bucket rather than a fireside. It is called Cerasuolo di Vittoria, and it is the only DOCG in a region with dozens of denominations. It is no accident that the honour fell to it.

## A town born to make wine

Vittoria has a precise date of birth, 1607, and a founder: Vittoria Colonna Enriquez, Countess of Modica, who governed the fief as regent after her husband’s death. According to the story handed down in the town, settlers who agreed to move there were granted a plot of land on condition that part of it was planted with vines. If the tale has the outline of a founding legend, the landscape confirms its substance: here the vine did not arrive later, it was the very reason the town existed.

For a couple of centuries the local wine travelled far, by sea as well. Then came phylloxera, trouble in its markets and, later, competition from more profitable crops: anyone crossing the plain of Vittoria today sees expanses of greenhouses, and understands at once which alternative took ground from the vineyard. The rediscovery of the vines began in the mid-20th century. Cerasuolo di Vittoria became a DOC in 1973 and a DOCG in 2005, and it is still Sicily’s only one.

## Two grapes, two characters

The production rules are clear: Nero d'Avola from 50 to 70%, Frappato from 30 to 50%. Two varieties that seem chosen precisely to correct each other.

Nero d'Avola, often still called Calabrese around here, brings body, colour, dark fruit and tannin. We have written about it at length in its own article; suffice it to say here that on its own it leans towards power.

Frappato is its opposite. It gives wines of pale colour, with intense aromas of flowers, red fruit and spice, a light body and fine tannins. It is an almost exclusively Sicilian grape, tied above all to this area, so much so that it is also known as Frappato di Vittoria. There is an intriguing clue about its origins: a 2008 Italian genetic study found a close relationship with Sangiovese, suggesting that Frappato may descend from a cross between Sangiovese and another, unidentified variety. It is a hypothesis, not a certainty, but it neatly explains a certain family resemblance: the same acidity, the same cherry.

The name of the denomination tells you the result. *Cerasuolo* evokes cherry red, a bright red that has nothing to do with Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo, which is a rosé made from Montepulciano. Same adjective, different wines: best not to confuse them on a wine list.

## Red sand over limestone

The secret of Cerasuolo lies underfoot. The typical soil here is a thin layer of iron-rich red sand lying over compact limestone: the *terra rossa* that strikes anyone seeing the vineyards for the first time. The roots push through the sand to the rock, where they find a little coolness and moisture even in the driest months. Towards Acate, by contrast, the sand alternates with layers of tuff.

The climate is Mediterranean, hot and dry, but less brutal than you might expect. The vineyards run from sea level up to about 500 metres, constant breezes keep disease at bay, and in summer the gap between day and night is considerable, reaching 13–14 degrees in the inland hills. It is this combination of free-draining sand, limestone and cool nights that allows a southern red to keep its perfume and acidity.

The production zone is broad and spans three provinces: Ragusa, Caltanissetta and Catania. The historic heart, however, is entitled to the Classico designation, which covers the communes of Vittoria, Comiso, Acate, Chiaramonte Gulfi and Santa Croce Camerina, plus certain *contrade*, the locally named vineyard districts, in neighbouring territories. The rules are simple: at least 13% alcohol, a maximum yield of 8 tonnes of grapes per hectare, release no earlier than 1 June of the year after the harvest for the basic Cerasuolo, and no earlier than 31 March of the second year for the Classico. It is a small DOCG: according to the figures available for 2019, it covered about 150 hectares and just over 6,400 hectolitres.

## Frappato, served cool

Alongside Cerasuolo, many producers in the area also bottle Frappato on its own, and that is where the grape’s character becomes fully clear. A pale, crunchy wine with tannin you barely notice: exactly the kind of red that, as we explained in our piece on reds to drink chilled, shows its best a few degrees below room temperature. At around 14 degrees the aromas stay sharp and the alcohol does not take over. Left at room temperature in a warm dining room, it can seem flat.

The same goes, to a lesser extent, for younger Cerasuolo, which does not need to be treated like a red for a grand roast. It works with oily fish, with caponata, with a plate of pasta in tomato sauce, with Sicilian street food. The Classico, which spends longer in the cellar, can take more substantial dishes and a few years in bottle. Some estates even age it in terracotta amphorae rather than wood, the sign of an area keen to experiment without losing its identity.

## The DOCG that proved lightness right

My view is that Cerasuolo di Vittoria is the best proof that Sicily is not condemned to power. For years the market asked southern reds to be dark, concentrated and soft, and many obliged. Here they did the opposite, and were proved right a few decades early: now that everyone is looking for fresher, more drinkable reds, the island’s only DOCG suddenly seems thoroughly modern. Not because it has changed, but because the rest of the world has finally caught up with its pace.
