*Secco, abboccato, amabile, dolce*: dry, medium dry, medium sweet, sweet. They are words we have all read on a label and that almost nobody could translate into numbers. Yet numbers is what they are, set by European law to the gram. And they hide a trap that wrong-foots even experienced drinkers: the same word does not mean the same thing on a still wine and on a sparkling one. A “dry” wine with bubbles can contain eight times the sugar of a still “dry”.

## The sugar that remains

Everything starts from residual sugar, that is, the grape sugar that the yeasts have not turned into alcohol. In a wine fermented to the end only a few grams per litre remain; if fermentation stops earlier, because the must was extremely rich, because the wine was chilled or because alcohol was added, more remains. In sparkling wines there is an extra step: after the second fermentation a syrup of wine and sugar can be added, the dosage, which decides how sweet the finished product will be.

The words used to describe the result are regulated by [Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/IT/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0033), Annex III. For sparkling wines stating them on the label is mandatory; for other wines it is optional, but if they are used they must respect the thresholds.

## Still wines: the scale few people know

For still wines the thresholds are these.

| Term | Residual sugar |
|---|---|
| Secco (dry) | up to 4 g/l, or up to 9 g/l if total acidity is high |
| Abboccato (medium dry) | above dry and up to 12 g/l, or up to 18 g/l if acidity is high |
| Amabile (medium sweet) | above medium dry and up to 45 g/l |
| Dolce (sweet) | at least 45 g/l |

The most interesting detail is that clause on acidity. A wine can be called dry up to 9 grams of sugar per litre if its total acidity, expressed in grams of tartaric acid, is not more than 2 grams lower than the sugar; for medium dry the limit rises to 18 grams if acidity is not more than 10 grams lower. It is the law acknowledging a fact of the palate: acidity masks sweetness. A razor-sharp Riesling with 8 grams of sugar can taste drier than a flat white with 4.

*Abboccato*, then, is a wine with barely perceptible sweetness, far from dessert: you find it in some traditional Orvieto, Lambrusco or Gutturnio. *Amabile* is already distinctly soft. *Dolce* is the territory of *passito* wines from dried grapes, of noble-rot wines, of Moscato.

## Sparkling wines: where “dry” is not dry

For sparkling wines the scale is different, and the names are the ones that confuse half of Italy at every toast.

| Term | Residual sugar |
|---|---|
| Brut nature, pas dosé, dosaggio zero | below 3 g/l, and only with no sugar added after the second fermentation |
| Extra brut | from 0 to 6 g/l |
| Brut | below 12 g/l |
| Extra dry | from 12 to 17 g/l |
| Secco (dry, sec) | from 17 to 32 g/l |
| Abboccato (demi-sec) | from 32 to 50 g/l |
| Dolce (sweet) | above 50 g/l |

Put the two tables side by side and the paradox is plain. On a still wine “secco” means at most 4 grams (9 with the acidity exception); on a sparkling wine it means 17 to 32. “Abboccato” on a still wine indicates a hint of sweetness; on a sparkling wine it corresponds to demi-sec, between 32 and 50 grams, almost a dessert wine. And extra dry, whose name promises “extra dryness”, is sweeter than brut. These names were born when average taste was much sweeter than today: the words have stayed, the taste has changed.

For sparkling wines the law allows a margin of 3 grams per litre between the actual figure and the term; for other wines, one gram.

## Sweet sparkling wine

Those looking for a “sweet sparkling wine” for panettone therefore have two routes. The first is sparkling wines labelled dolce or demi-sec. The second, often more interesting, is bubbles that are sweet by nature, fully sparkling or semi-sparkling, such as Asti, Moscato d’Asti or Brachetto d’Acqui, where the sweetness comes not from the dosage but from a fermentation stopped early, with little alcohol and the scent of the grape intact. With Christmas cakes they work far better than a brut, which next to sugar tastes bitter and sour.

## How to tell when the label is silent

On many still wines the term is absent, because it is not mandatory. A few clues help. Since December 2023 the nutrition declaration, on the label or via the QR code, gives sugars per 100 ml: multiply by ten to get grams per litre. A low alcohol level in a wine from very ripe grapes, such as a Moscato at 5.5%, signals residual sugar. And the words of the production rules, *passito*, late harvest, *recioto*, already say a lot.

Our view is that these four words deserve more attention than they get. The taste for bone-dry wine is a recent fashion, and it has passed off as a defect a sweetness that in many wines is balance. A well-made *abboccato*, with the right acidity, is not a wine for beginners: it is a wine that has chosen not to be severe. And knowing how to read it on the label spares you both disappointment and prejudice.
