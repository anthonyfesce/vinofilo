Chardonnay has a reputation problem it does not deserve. For a generation of drinkers it was the fat, buttery, vanilla-laden white of the 1980s, the wine that gave rise in the United States to a movement with its own acronym, ABC, “Anything But Chardonnay”. The New York Times critic Frank Prial was already charting its spread in 1995. And yet the same grape gives us the most razor-edged Chablis, the most celebrated Montrachet, a good part of all Champagne and the backbone of Franciacorta. The point is not Chardonnay. The point is what the people who grow it do with it, and where.

## A grape that lets itself be written on

A few figures to set the scene. Worldwide, Chardonnay covers some 210,000 hectares: according to the most widely quoted data it is the fifth most planted grape of all and, among whites, second only to Spain’s Airén. The name, in the most common version of the story, comes from a small village in the Mâconnais, in Burgundy, called Chardonnay. DNA analysis carried out at the University of California, Davis, showed that it is the offspring of a cross between Pinot and Gouais Blanc, a rustic and little-regarded grape: aristocratic on one side of the family only.

Its most precious quality is also the one that has made it vulnerable: Chardonnay has a discreet aromatic character. It does not have an unmistakable scent, as Sauvignon or Moscato do, and so it records two things with great fidelity, the place and the winemaker’s hand. Cool climate or warm, limestone or clay, steel or new oak, malolactic fermentation or not: everything shows in the glass. It is a blank page, and some have written poetry on it while others have written advertising copy.

## Burgundy, the starting point

In Chablis, the northernmost part of Burgundy, Chardonnay grows on Kimmeridgian marls and limestones, rich in marine fossils. The seven *grands crus*, gathered on a single hillside above the river Serein, cover about a hundred hectares. Tradition calls for wines with little or no new oak, high in acidity, with the saline, almost chalky profile the French sum up in the word *minéralité*. Some producers use oak, and in Chablis this is still a matter of debate: when it is there, it is usually old and discreet.

Further south, in the Côte de Beaune, the same grape changes register. Meursault gives the roundest whites, Puligny-Montrachet the tautest and sharpest, Chassagne-Montrachet sits in between, and above them all stands the Montrachet family, with some of the most famous and most expensive Chardonnay grands crus in the world, alongside Corton-Charlemagne. Here oak is part of the recipe, but as seasoning rather than main course. We have already covered the Burgundian pyramid, with its *climats* and its labels that read back to front: suffice it to say that this is where the rest of the world copied the model from.

## California, from butter to restraint

California is the chapter that made the most noise. In 1976, at the Judgement of Paris, a Napa Valley Chardonnay, Chateau Montelena 1973, beat the Burgundians in a blind tasting and gave American wine sudden credibility. Within a few years the grape exploded: according to figures gathered by industry sources, by 1988 California’s Chardonnay plantings had already overtaken France’s.

The style that conquered the market could be spotted a mile off: very ripe grapes, new oak, full malolactic fermentation, which turns malic acid into lactic acid and brings buttery notes with it. Broad wines, sweet with fruit, often high in alcohol. People loved them, and for that very reason they were imitated everywhere to the point of nausea, and then to the ABC backlash.

The turn came from within. In 2011 the grower Jasmine Hirsch and the sommelier-turned-winemaker Rajat Parr founded In Pursuit of Balance, a series of tastings devoted to Californian Pinot Noir and Chardonnay in a fresher style, less marked by oak. The group wound up its tastings in 2016, having taken them abroad as well. Not all of California has changed, but today the classic rich Chardonnay sits alongside tauter wines from cool areas such as the Sonoma Coast and the Russian River Valley.

## Champagne and Franciacorta, Chardonnay with bubbles

In Champagne, Chardonnay is one of the three main grapes, alongside Pinot Noir and Meunier, and its kingdom is the Côte des Blancs, the band of chalk hills south of Épernay. Avize, Cramant, Le Mesnil-sur-Oger are names enthusiasts pronounce with respect. When a Champagne is made from Chardonnay alone it is called Blanc de Blancs, white from white grapes: wines that are tense and floral in youth and capable of surprising depth with age. Here the high acidity of a cold climate is a virtue, and Chardonnay is the grape that carries it best through time.

For a reader outside Italy, Franciacorta is perhaps the news of this journey. It is an area of glacial moraine hills near Lake Iseo, in Lombardy, between Bergamo and Brescia, making traditional-method sparkling wine, that is, with a second fermentation in bottle as in Champagne, and where Chardonnay is the leading grape, with Pinot Nero, Pinot Bianco and a possible small share of Erbamat, a local grape with lively acidity. It was the first Italian traditional-method denomination to be awarded DOCG, the top tier of the Italian system. Its most original invention is Satèn, a Chardonnay-based blanc de blancs (Pinot Bianco is allowed up to 50%) with bottle pressure capped at 5 atmospheres instead of the usual 6 or so: a finer, almost silky bead, hence the name. We have told the story of its rivalry with Trento DOC, Italy’s other capital of traditional-method fizz, separately.

## Australia, the pendulum swings back

Australia lived through the same arc as California, only faster. Chardonnay arrived as early as 1832 with James Busby, but took off only in the second half of the twentieth century: in the boom of the 1980s and 1990s plantings grew fivefold, and by 1990 it was already the country’s most planted white grape. The style of the day was golden, oily, very ripe. Then the pendulum swung back, sometimes too far, towards paler, more structured, tauter wines with notes of white peach and nectarine. Cool areas such as the Adelaide Hills, the Yarra Valley and Tasmania are now the ones producing the Australian Chardonnays closest to the Burgundian model.

## The grape that tells the truth

My view is that Chardonnay is the finest teacher wine has. Precisely because it imposes no scent of its own, it shows with ruthless clarity what happens when climate, soil and cellar choices change. ABC was right about the wrong target: it was not Chardonnay that was tired, it was a single style, repeated everywhere. Anyone who lines up a Chablis, a Meursault, a coastal Californian, a Blanc de Blancs, a Satèn and a white from the Adelaide Hills is not drinking the same wine six times. They are drinking six places. And they understand, in a single evening, why the word terroir is not a French affectation.
