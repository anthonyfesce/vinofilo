For a long time the geography of the wine trade was simple: in spring you went to Düsseldorf for ProWein, then to Verona for Vinitaly, and Paris was the city where you drank, not where you bought. The map has since been redrawn. In mid-February the halls at Porte de Versailles become the place where a good part of the global wine business meets, and anyone in the industry has learnt to book a hotel months ahead. From 15 to 17 February 2027 it all starts again.

## What exactly it is

Wine Paris, which also carries the Vinexpo Paris brand in its official name, is not a wine festival. It is a trade fair: importers, distributors, restaurateurs, wine merchants, sommeliers, buyers for supermarkets and duty free. Entry requires a personal badge, and the enthusiast public stays outside. It is a detail that matters, because it sets the tone: nobody comes here to taste for pleasure. They come to sign contracts, to find an importer for Japan or a new Champagne supplier for a chain of bistros.

The history of the show, born from the coming together of several events with different names and dates, is told in ways that do not always agree, and we prefer not to wade in. Better to look at the official figures for the 2026 edition: 6,537 exhibitors from 63 countries, 63,541 visitors, 169 nations represented among attendees, almost 26,000 registered business meetings and 239 sessions of conferences and masterclasses. Roughly half of both exhibitors and visitors came from abroad. For 2027 the venue is announcing figures on the same scale, with more than 6,500 exhibitors and over 63,500 visitors expected.

## Why Paris overtook Düsseldorf

For a couple of years an almost unanimous view has been doing the rounds in the trade: Wine Paris has replaced ProWein as Europe’s benchmark fair. We do not offer a number-by-number comparison here, because we have no official figures from the German fair to set side by side with the Parisian ones. The reasons for the shift, though, can be argued without tables.

The first is the calendar. February comes before spring, when the year’s price lists are still being written and buyers have budgets to spend. The second is the city itself: Paris is a global air and rail hub and, above all, a drinking capital, with restaurants, *caves* and wine bars that become an extension of the fair. The organisers know it and make the most of it, with a programme of evening events spread across town. The third reason is subtler: France is the country that, more than any other, sets the vocabulary of fine wine, and being in Paris gives every foreign producer the sense of standing before the strictest judge. True or not, it is a feeling that sells plane tickets.

Then there is an almost ironic fact. A French fair built its success by becoming thoroughly international: half the exhibitors come from elsewhere, and alongside the great home regions (Bordeaux, Champagne, Burgundy, Languedoc, Provence) there are Argentina, Australia, California and a pavilion for Georgia, the cradle of amphora wine.

## What to expect in February

Wine remains the core, but the perimeter is widening. Next to the main halls sits Be Spirits, devoted to spirits and mixology, which in 2026 counted 370 exhibitors from 39 countries. And there is a space for no- and low-alcohol drinks, a sign that even the most traditional have stopped treating this market as a passing fad. The programme of conferences and masterclasses is dense, and climate change, tariffs and falling consumption among young drinkers are set to dominate the corridors, because they are the issues dominating the balance sheets.

The advice for anyone going on business is obvious but decisive: arrive with your appointments already booked. Three days sound like plenty, but the floor space is vast and the distance between halls is measured in kilometres. The last day, Wednesday, closes earlier.

## Italy in Paris

After France, Italy has the strongest presence among exhibitors, ahead of Spain, Portugal, the United States and Germany. This is not a courtesy detail. For an Italian producer, Paris has become the shop window where, in three days, you meet buyers who would never come to Verona, because Vinitaly, the great Italian wine fair held every spring in Verona, remains above all an Italian affair, however crowded with foreigners.

International visitors would do well to know how to read the Italian pavilions. They are often organised by region: Piedmont with Barolo and Barbaresco, the great reds from Nebbiolo; Tuscany with Chianti Classico and Brunello di Montalcino, both from Sangiovese; the Veneto with Prosecco and Amarone. The labels DOC and DOCG denote designations of origin, the latter with stricter rules. The most useful tip is to look beyond the famous names: Etna, Irpinia, Valtellina and Alto Adige are where Italy is telling the liveliest part of its story today.

For those following from home, opening day deserves a bottle that bridges the two sides of the Alps. An Italian traditional-method sparkler, a Franciacorta or a Trento DOC, alongside a grower Champagne, makes a small comparison that explains better than many a conference why Italy and France have been studying each other for centuries.

## Our view

The success of Wine Paris carries a lesson that goes beyond the fair: in wine, place matters, even when the talk is of business. Düsseldorf offered efficiency; Paris offers efficiency and a reason to stay for the evening. The risk, for a show that has grown so fast, is becoming too big to be useful, a labyrinth in which small producers get lost among the stands of the big groups. For now the balance holds. But the wine that sells best in Paris in February is still the one with something to say, not the one with the biggest stand.

## Practical information

- Dates: from Monday 15 to Wednesday 17 February 2027. According to the venue’s schedule, Monday and Tuesday from 9am to 7pm, Wednesday from 9am to 5pm.
- Venue: Paris Expo Porte de Versailles, 1 Place de la Porte de Versailles, Paris (15th arrondissement).
- Access: trade only, with a personal badge.
- Official website: wineparis.com
