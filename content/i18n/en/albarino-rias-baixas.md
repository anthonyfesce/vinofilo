There is a Spain that looks nothing like the Spanish postcard. No blazing sun, no scorched red earth, no flamenco: in Galicia, in the north-western corner of the Iberian peninsula, it rains as it does in Ireland, the fog rolls in off the sea each morning and the houses are built of granite. It is here, facing the Atlantic, that Albariño grows, the white that over recent decades has persuaded half the world that Spain can also make wines that are fresh, saline and taut as a wire. Anyone who knows Italy’s coastal whites, from the Vermentino of Liguria and Sardinia to the Verdicchio of the Marche, will recognise a distant cousin: the same calling for fish, a completely different climate.

## Rías Baixas, where the sea runs into the land

The name of the appellation already describes the landscape. The *rías* are deep inlets, river valleys flooded by the ocean, something between a fjord and an estuary; *baixas* means low, in other words the southern ones along the Galician coast. Rías Baixas became a Denominación de Origen in 1988, taking over from a dedicated Albariño designation created in 1980. Today it has five subzones, four in the province of Pontevedra and one in the south of A Coruña: Val do Salnés, O Rosal, Condado do Tea, Soutomaior and Ribeira do Ulla.

The figures say a great deal about the place. A little over 4,300 hectares, yet split, according to 2022 data, into some 22,800 plots: a patchwork of holdings reminiscent of certain Italian hillsides, where every family has its own scrap of vineyard. There are about 180 wineries and Albariño accounts for more than 90% of the vines, although the rules allow other local varieties, whites such as Loureira, Treixadura and Caíño Blanco, and a handful of reds. The climate is Atlantic in the fullest sense: wet winters, mild summers that rarely top 30 °C, sea mists and, in the more inland areas, rainfall that can exceed 2,000 millimetres a year.

Each subzone has its own voice. Val do Salnés, the most exposed to the sea, has rocky, alluvial soils; O Rosal, along the river Miño that marks the border with Portugal, is alluvial; Condado do Tea, further inland, is the warmest area, on granite and slate.

## Granite pergolas

The first thing that strikes a visitor is how the vines are trained. Not low rows as in Burgundy or Chianti, but high pergolas, the *parrales*, held up by granite posts and wires strung overhead. Anyone who knows Italy will think of the pergolas of Trentino or the old ones of Valpolicella, and the comparison holds: here too the system keeps the fruit away from the damp ground, lets air move through the bunches and reduces the risk of rot, which with so much rain is enemy number one. Beneath the pergola there was traditionally room for a vegetable patch or animals, in a region where land was scarce and nothing was wasted.

The maximum permitted yield for Albariño is 12,000 kilos per hectare, a generous limit. Quality, in short, depends far more on who is farming, and where, than on the rulebook.

## Albariño or Alvarinho, one grape under two flags

Cross the Miño and the name changes. On the Portuguese bank the grape is called Alvarinho, and its heartland is the Monção e Melgaço subregion, the northernmost part of the Vinho Verde region. Here the wines are usually richer and higher in alcohol than classic Vinho Verde, the light, often faintly spritzy white that many know as an undemanding summer glass. It is the same variety, divided by a political border rather than any botanical difference, rather as Ribolla is shared between Friuli and Slovenia.

On its origins, certainties are few. A widespread tradition holds that the grape was brought by the Cistercian monks of the monastery of Armenteira in the 12th century; another legend credits the monks of Cluny, and therefore Burgundy, but there is no genetic link with Burgundian varieties. A supposed kinship with the Jura’s Savagnin has also been ruled out by DNA analysis. The first written mention of Alvarinho dates from 1843. The rest is probably the story of an old grape native to this Atlantic strip straddling Galicia and the Minho, which only in the 20th century, as cellar techniques improved, became a recognisable single-varietal wine.

## In the glass, and around it

The classic style is the one that made Albariño’s name abroad: bright straw-yellow, aromas of citrus, peach and white flowers, high acidity and a finish many describe as saline. Most of it never sees oak. Many producers leave the wine on its fine lees, the spent yeasts, to build texture; others experiment with concrete tanks or even granite eggs. There is also a Barrica version, aged in wooden vessels of no more than 600 litres, and the O Rosal subzone provides for wines in which Albariño, Loureira and Caíño Blanco must together make up at least 70%.

The food match almost writes itself, and Galicia wrote it first. The rías are among Europe’s great shellfish grounds: mussels farmed on floating rafts, oysters, clams, goose barnacles, boiled octopus served with paprika and olive oil. A white with this acidity scrubs the palate clean of the salt and richness of the sea, and it works with soft cheeses too. For an Italian reader the obvious reference is *crudo di mare*, raw seafood, or a plate of fried small fish; for everyone else, a dozen oysters or properly made fish and chips are an equally honest test.

Albariño has now begun to travel beyond the Iberian peninsula, with plantings in California, for example. It is a sign that the grape has left its regional niche and earned a place in the international vocabulary of white wine.

## A white that need not become important

My view is that Albariño’s greatest risk is its own success. When a wine becomes shorthand for “crisp white with fish” on wine lists the world over, the temptation is to mass-produce it, all alike, all citrus and no place. Rías Baixas, by contrast, has a rare inheritance: thousands of small vineyards, subzones with distinct characters, a climate that punishes laziness. The more ambitious versions, aged longer on their lees, set out to show that Albariño can also stand the test of time. But its strength remains what it has always been: putting the Atlantic, the rain and the granite in the glass. It does not need to become a great collector’s wine. It only needs to stay faithful to that stretch of coast.
