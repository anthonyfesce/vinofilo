Every self-respecting grape has a legend, but few can boast one signed, at least in spirit, by Homer. Nero di Troia can. According to the tale handed down in northern Puglia, once the Trojan War was over the Greek hero Diomedes sailed up the river Ofanto, his ship ballasted with stones from the walls of the ruined city. With those stones he is said to have marked out the borders of a territory, the Campi Diomedei, and on the riverbanks he planted the vine cuttings he had brought with him. From them came the *uva di Troia*, the grape of Troy. It is a lovely story whose only flaw is that it cannot be proved. But it says a great deal about how this land regards its red: not as just another wine, but as an inheritance.

## One legend, four theories and a document

Vinofilo has already covered Nero di Troia within a broader portrait of Puglia. Here it is worth digging deeper, starting with the name, because there are at least four theories and they contradict one another cheerfully. There is the Greek one, tied to Diomedes. There is the one that makes it an ancient grape of the Daunians, the people who lived in the area before Greek colonisation. There is the more prosaic one, linking it to the small town of Troia, in the province of Foggia, which tradition holds was founded by Diomedes himself: a neat way of squaring myth with geography. And there is the Albanian one, according to which the first vines arrived from the Albanian coast, from a village called Cruja, which in local dialect supposedly became Troia.

To these can be added a version that brings in the Spanish and a governor of the d’Avalos family, said to have planted the first vines in the area. None of these stories rests on solid documentary evidence. The one firm fact is more modest: the variety appears in official records from 1875.

The synonyms tell a geography of their own. Uva di Canosa, Uva di Barletta, Tranese, Troiano, Sumarello: every town in the north of the Bari province and in Daunia has tried to put its own name on it, a sign that this grape was at home everywhere, from the Tavoliere plain to the Murgia plateau.

## A character that does not ask permission

In the glass Nero di Troia is a deeply coloured red with firm tannin and livelier acidity than you might expect from a southern wine. It is precisely that tannin that has shaped its history. For decades it was considered too angular to drink on its own: it was blended with Montepulciano to soften it, or sent north, where it lent colour and structure to slighter wines. A blending-wine destiny shared by many southern reds.

On its ripening time the sources disagree: some describe it as a late grape, others as a mid-season variety. More widely shared is the verdict of producers, who call it a difficult grape to work with, ungenerous and delicate. Not exactly the qualities anyone looks for when planting for volume.

And so, in recent decades, Nero di Troia came close to vanishing from whole areas. In the countryside around Troia vineyards gave way to wheat, and the few surviving rows were kept for homemade wine. The comeback began when some producers decided to bet on precisely what had once been hidden: the variety on its own, its tannin tamed in the vineyard and the cellar rather than diluted with other grapes.

## Castel del Monte and the other appellations

The centre of gravity remains Castel del Monte, the DOC created in 1971 in the shadow of the octagonal castle built for Frederick II on the Murgia. In 2011 the appellation gained three separate DOCGs. Castel del Monte Nero di Troia Riserva requires at least 90% Nero di Troia and 24 months’ ageing, at least 12 of them in wooden barrels. Castel del Monte Rosso Riserva drops to 65% Nero di Troia, with the same minimum ageing. The third DOCG is devoted to another grape, Bombino Nero. The zone covers the whole commune of Minervino Murge and parts of Andria, Corato, Trani, Ruvo di Puglia, Terlizzi, Bitonto, Palo del Colle, Toritto and Binetto.

Beyond Castel del Monte, Nero di Troia is the main grape of the Rosso Barletta and Rosso Canosa DOCs, features in Rosso di Cerignola and Orta Nova (up to 40%), and makes up 35 to 60% of Cacc’e Mmitte di Lucera, a DOC since 1975 with an irresistible name which, according to the most common explanation (undocumented), means something like “take out and put in” in dialect, after the rhythm at which the wine was drunk and topped up. In the province of Foggia there is also the Tavoliere DOC, which puts it on the label too.

It is a map that says something simple: Nero di Troia belongs not to a single *terroir* but to a whole band of northern Puglia, from the Adriatic coast up to the 430 metres of Troia, the ancient Roman Aecae, crossed by the Via Traiana.

## The Puglia red for people who don’t like Puglia reds

My view is that Nero di Troia is the right grape for anyone who thinks they already know everything about Puglia. Those who associate the region with warm, soft reds of ripe fruit and generous alcohol will find here a more upright, more severe wine that asks for patience and a meal. It is no accident that its admirers compare it with certain reds from the north rather than with its Salento neighbours. The risk, now that it is back in fashion, is to see it tamed to please everyone, softened until it loses the bite that makes it recognisable. That would be a mistake: Diomedes, if he ever really planted it, did not bring it all this way to make an obliging wine.
