For centuries, anyone who said Maremma got a one-word reply: malaria. Folk songs did not describe it as a land of vineyards, but as a place you came back from sick, if you came back at all. Today the same word conjures up farm stays, horses, beaches and a red found on almost every Tuscan wine list. In between lies one of the most radical transformations of the Italian landscape, and Morellino di Scansano is, in its own way, the final balance sheet.

## Before the wine, the water

The plain around Grosseto was long a hydraulic problem before it was an agricultural one. As early as the late sixteenth century the Medici had set up an office in Grosseto to manage canals and ditches. In the eighteenth century, under Pietro Leopoldo of Lorraine, the mathematician Leonardo Ximenes planned to regulate the great lake of Castiglione rather than drain it. The turning point came with his grandson, Leopoldo II: on 27 November 1828 the project to fill in the lake was launched, with an intake on the Ombrone river built the following year, and around 1830 the Grand Duke had the pine forest planted that still shelters the fields behind the coast.

The struggle with the marshes and with malaria, however, would go on for a long time yet, well into the twentieth century. And once the water had been tamed, another obstacle remained: who owned the land.

## The reform that drew the hills

At the start of the 1950s the Maremma was still a country of great estates. According to figures gathered for the land reform, in the Tuscan-Lazio Maremma district some eight hundred landowners, less than one per cent, held almost three quarters of the productive land in estates of more than a hundred hectares. Half the land was bare arable, nearly a third woodland, and specialised crops, vineyards and olive groves included, barely reached 4%.

The 1950 land reform law and the Ente Maremma, the reform agency founded in 1951, changed everything within a few years. Across the whole district, spanning Tuscany and Lazio, expropriations exceeded 170,000 hectares, divided into smallholdings of between 10 and 18 hectares, each with a house and a stable. Many of the farmhouses dotted around the Grosseto countryside, now often turned into *agriturismi*, farm-stay guesthouses, were born of that project. With the families came vegetable plots, olive groves and vines: not yet vineyards for great wine, but a sign that this land could be farmed differently.

## Morellino, or Sangiovese with a Maremma accent

Morellino is, first of all, a name. It is what Scansano calls Sangiovese, Italy’s most widely planted red grape, which, as we have written, changes identity with every Tuscan hill. Two explanations circulate for the nickname: some link it to the *morella* cherry, dark and tart, others to the *morelli*, black-coated horses that were once plentiful in the Maremma. The second is the one also given by the Tuscan Region’s tourism website; neither can be proven, and perhaps it is right to let them live side by side.

The appellation was born as a DOC in 1978 and became a DOCG by a decree of November 2006, effective from the 2007 harvest. The rules require at least 85% Sangiovese, with the rest given over to other non-aromatic red grapes permitted in Tuscany. The zone covers the whole commune of Scansano and parts of Manciano, Magliano in Toscana, Grosseto, Campagnatico, Semproniano and Roccalbegna, all in the province of Grosseto. Vineyards must be on hillsides, valley floors excluded: the average altitude is around 250 metres, the highest reaches 566.

Two styles, two rhythms. Basic Morellino may be released from 1 March after the harvest, with no oak requirement: it is a wine built for freshness, often drunk just a few months after picking. The Riserva must age for at least 24 months, twelve of them in cask, and reach at least 13% alcohol. Maximum yields are 9 tonnes of grapes per hectare.

## You can taste the sea, but not where you think

Compared with the Sangiovese of Chianti or Montalcino, Scansano’s lives in warmer conditions and closer to the coast. The result, broadly speaking, is riper, more immediate fruit, less severe tannin, a certain generosity that makes young Morellino easy to understand. But the sea does not work only by warming. The breezes that climb up from the coast dry the vines and temper the extremes, and the altitude of the hills brings less sultry nights that hold on to some acidity even in the toughest summers. It is this balance, when it comes off, that separates a good Morellino from a Sangiovese simply baked by the sun.

That, indeed, is exactly where the risk lies. An appellation that has built its success on drinkability can easily slide towards soft, fruity, interchangeable wines, perhaps rounded off with a little international grape within the permitted 15%. It is the same temptation half the Maremma knows, and one that from Bolgheri southwards has produced both great bottles and a good deal of sameness.

## Our view

Morellino di Scansano is often presented as the “little brother” of Tuscany’s great Sangiovese wines, the trattoria red that asks for less patience than Brunello. It is an ungenerous portrait, though not entirely wrong, and that is precisely the point: the Maremma has no need to imitate anyone. This is a land that seventy years ago was made of great estates, pasture and wheat, and that learned wine in a hurry. Its strength lies in bright, direct Sangiovese, not in the race for concentration. The day Scansano’s producers stop asking themselves whether they are “serious” enough and start telling the story of their hills, one by one, Morellino will have finished its own reclamation.
