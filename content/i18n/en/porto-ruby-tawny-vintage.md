Port is the wine everyone thinks they know and hardly anyone knows how to order. For many it is the dark bottle of the after-dinner hour, opened at Christmas and returned to the sideboard for months. In truth that name hides styles that have little in common, aged in opposite ways, with very different prices and destinies. Getting to grips with them takes ten minutes, and they are well spent.

## A valley of schist and an English story

Port is not made in Porto. The grapes come from the Douro Valley in northern Portugal, where the river carves dizzying terraces out of schist, a crumbly rock the roots can split in search of water. The valley falls into three zones, from west to east: the rainier Baixo Corgo, the Cima Corgo around Pinhão, traditionally the source of the best grapes, and the Douro Superior, the hottest and driest. A great many varieties are permitted, but five do most of the work: Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, which is Spain’s Tempranillo travelling on another passport (we covered it for Tempranillo Day), Tinta Barroca and Tinto Cão.

The wine then travelled downstream to Vila Nova de Gaia, across the river from Porto, where it still ages today in the *lodges*, the warehouses of the shipping houses. For centuries it was carried by *rabelos*, flat-bottomed wooden boats; the dams built on the river in the twentieth century brought that era to an end, and today it goes by lorry.

The English names of so many houses are no affectation. Under the Methuen Treaty of 1703 Portugal won favourable duties on its wines in England, which was at war with France in those years and needed an alternative to claret. English and Scottish merchants settled in Porto and made it a family business, generation after generation. It is a story an Italian reader already knows: the same pattern that saw a Liverpool merchant, John Woodhouse, launch Marsala in Sicily in the late eighteenth century.

## Stopping time with grape spirit

The technical heart of Port is fortification. The must begins to ferment, often after vigorous crushing, traditionally done by foot in *lagares*, stone treading tanks. After a few days, while some of the sugar is still intact, a neutral grape spirit of around 77% alcohol is added: the yeasts stop, the residual sugar stays, and the finished wine usually lands between 19 and 22% vol. The traditional proportion is roughly one part spirit to four parts wine. That is the difference from Sherry, which is fortified after fermentation, once the sugars have already been consumed.

A much-loved tale is told about the method’s origins: in 1678 two sons of a Liverpool merchant, visiting a monastery at Lamego, are said to have learned from an abbot the habit of adding spirit during fermentation, and bought up the entire stock. It is more a founding legend than historical proof. The certain fact lies elsewhere: in 1756, under the Marquis of Pombal, the Douro was demarcated by law and placed under a state company that controlled its quality and trade. It is one of the oldest demarcated wine regions in the world, preceded, among others, by a name Italians know well: Chianti, with Cosimo III de' Medici’s decree of 1716.

## Two families: reds in bottle and ambers in wood

To find your bearings, one distinction is enough. On one side are the Ports that age mainly in large vats or in bottle, sheltered from oxygen, keeping their dark fruit and purple colour: the ruby family. On the other are those that spend years in smaller casks, where oxygen works slowly, the colour turns to amber and the fruit gives way to walnuts, caramel and dried figs: the tawny family.

**Ruby** is the starting point: young, fruity, a blend of several vintages to drink straight away. **Ruby Reserve** is a more ambitious selection in the same style. Basic **Tawny** is the entry-level oxidative version, while **age-indicated Tawnies**, 10, 20, 30 and 40 years old, are blends of several vintages: the number does not state the age of every drop but the profile the wine must show, which the denomination’s regulatory institute has to approve. **Colheita** is a single-vintage tawny that must spend at least seven years in wood, and carries its harvest year on the label.

## LBV and Vintage, the declared years

**Vintage** is the aristocracy of the ruby family. It is made from the grapes of a single year, only when the house decides to “declare” it, which happens on average in just a few years per decade. It is bottled after two or three years and then left to evolve in bottle for decades, throwing a deposit that makes decanting essential. It is a small share of production, but it is the one on which the houses build their reputations. There are also Vintages from a single estate, the **Single Quinta** wines.

**Late Bottled Vintage**, or LBV, is the clever compromise: also from a single year, but it stays in wood for between four and six years before bottling. It reaches the market readier and cheaper than Vintage. Some LBVs are filtered and drink at once; others are not, and go on developing in bottle: the label tells you, and it is worth reading. Finally there is **Crusted**, a blend of several vintages bottled unfiltered, which like Vintage forms its “crust” of sediment, and white Port, increasingly used as the base for long aperitifs with tonic water.

At the table the practical rule is simple. Ruby and young Vintage love blue cheeses and dark chocolate, as we explained when writing about wine and chocolate. Tawny, served slightly cool, goes well with dried fruit and nuts, caramel desserts and aged cheeses. An open bottle of tawny, used as it is to oxygen, usually lasts longer; a Vintage, once decanted, should be drunk quickly.

## The most democratic of the great wines

My view is that Port, among the world’s great wines, is the one that gives most to those who spend least. A good 10-year-old tawny or a well-made LBV gives a serious idea of what a great fortified wine can be without demanding either a cellar or decades of waiting. Vintage remains the legend, and rightly so. But the best way to understand the Douro is not to wait twenty years for a legendary bottle: it is to put a ruby and a tawny on the table side by side, and discover that the same wine, given a different cask and a different span of time, can tell two opposite stories.
