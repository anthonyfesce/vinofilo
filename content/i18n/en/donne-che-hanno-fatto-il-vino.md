In 1805, at twenty-seven, Barbe-Nicole Ponsardin found herself a widow, with a small daughter and a modest wine house in Reims that nobody expected to outlive her husband. A family business in the hands of a young woman, in Napoleonic France, was more a matter of pity than of commerce. She took it seriously instead. The rest is the history of Champagne, and it is worth telling without the brochure varnish it is usually served with.

## The widow and the riddling

François Clicquot died in October 1805, of a fever. Barbe-Nicole, born in Reims in 1777 and married in 1798, persuaded her father-in-law Philippe to let her run the business, with a winemaker, Alexandre Fourneaux, at her side. The company name we know today, Veuve Clicquot-Ponsardin, dates from July 1810. Widow, precisely: a word that in those days meant above all one thing, that a woman could sign contracts.

Her most-cited contribution is technical, and deserves a proper explanation. Champagne referments in the bottle and, in the process, leaves a deposit of dead yeast that clouds the wine. Removing it used to be a clumsy, costly operation that lost liquid and pressure. The system attributed to the widow, around 1816, has the bottles placed neck down in perforated desks and turned a little each day, so that the sediment slides slowly towards the cork. This is *remuage*, or the pupitre, and for generations it was the signature gesture of every Champagne cellar. A historian's caveat: as so often, exact authorship is debated, and tradition remembers her cellar collaborators alongside her. But that the technique became standard practice under her direction, nobody disputes. Her cellars are also credited with a first vintage Champagne in 1810 and, in 1818, one of the first rosé Champagnes made by adding still red wine.

## Lily Bollinger and the long patience

A century and a half later, another widow. Élisabeth Law de Lauriston-Boubers, known to everyone as Lily, married Jacques Bollinger in 1923. When he died in 1941, with the war under way, she took over the presidency of the house and kept it until 1971. Thirty years in which the business survived a war, an occupation and time itself, which for Champagne is a raw material like grapes.

Under her direction the house launched, in 1961, the R.D. formula, Champagne left on its lees far longer than usual and disgorged only at the moment of sale, and in 1969 a cuvée from ungrafted French vines. Both choices reward patience rather than quarterly turnover, and say a good deal about her idea of wine. She is credited with a famous line about drinking Champagne in every mood: the quotation circulates so widely that I would rather point to the sources than pretend to know its exact wording. She died in 1977, childless; the house passed to her nephews.

## When women decided to organise

Individual exceptions do not change an industry. Associations do. In Italy the date is the spring of 1988, when the Associazione Nazionale Le Donne del Vino was presented at Vinitaly, born of some twenty members with the Tuscan Elisabetta Tognana as its first president. The underlying idea was to bring together people working in wine at every level, not only producers: oenologists, restaurateurs, sommeliers, journalists, those in communications and sales. From that core the association has grown past a thousand members.

The interesting thing is not the figure but the method: instead of looking for a symbolic patron saint, they chose to build a network. Italian wine, after all, was already full of women who had always worked in vineyard and cellar; what was missing was the name on the door.

## The first Master of Wine

The Master of Wine title, created in 1953 in Britain, was for almost twenty years an all-male club. The first woman to pass the examination was Sarah Morphew Stephen, in 1970. Born in 1942 in Somerset, she had studied oenology in Bordeaux under Émile Peynaud, one of the great masters of twentieth-century winemaking, and cut her teeth in Portugal. The story goes that a Port producer, early in her career, turned her away explaining that the wine trade had no place for a woman. If the detail makes us smile today, it is because she, in the meantime, was proved right. After her, it took six years before other women passed the exam. She died in 2023.

## A story not yet finished

Telling these lives as a gallery of heroines is convenient and a little false. Clicquot and Bollinger shared something unromantic: the death of a husband put them in a position to command, in eras when a married woman could not. Morphew had to win her title in an examination nobody had designed with her in mind. The associations of Le Donne del Vino were, above all, a practical answer to a closed door.

My opinion is simple. The day we stop adding "female" to every success story will be the day these names stand in the textbooks beside those of Peynaud and Dom Pérignon, without footnotes. We are getting there; the cellar, fortunately, has a habit of doing justice with time.
