Everyone who arrives in San Gimignano looks up. The towers are the postcard, the reason for the coaches, the image the world holds of this corner of the Valdelsa. Yet if anything predates mass tourism and will outlast it, it is the wine made on the hills beneath those towers. Vernaccia di San Gimignano holds a record few people remember and a history documented for more than seven centuries. It lacks only one thing: for Tuscany, wrapped up in its reds, to truly notice it.

## The first name in the Official Gazette

The record is this. When Italy gave itself rules on denominations of origin in 1963, somebody had to inaugurate them, and the first wine to gain DOC status was Vernaccia di San Gimignano, by a presidential decree of 3 March 1966, published in the *Gazzetta Ufficiale* on 6 May of the same year. In 2026 the denomination turned sixty. DOCG followed with a decree of 9 July 1993.

It was no accident. Vernaccia already had a name recognised beyond its own territory, and a recognised name is exactly what a denomination exists to protect. But the record has an ironic flavour too: Italy’s first protected wine is not a Barolo or a Chianti but a white from a single commune, which on many wine lists still turns up at the bottom of the page, just before the sparklers.

## Seven centuries of paperwork, and a line from Dante

Vernaccia’s history is not left to legend alone. The oldest document naming it dates from 1276: a register of the *gabelle*, the duties levied by the Commune of San Gimignano, which set a tax of three *soldi* on anyone selling a *soma*, a pack-load, of Vernaccia outside its territory. The first written record, in other words, is an export tax, which says a great deal: even then it was a wine worth taking elsewhere.

Then there is Dante. In Canto XXIV of the *Purgatorio*, among the gluttons atoning by fasting, the poet meets Pope Martin IV, a Frenchman from Tours, who “purga per digiuno / l'anguille di Bolsena e la vernaccia”, purging by fasting the eels of Bolsena and the vernaccia. According to the commentators, the pope liked his lake eels drowned in the wine before they were cooked. The line has become the denomination’s calling card, and the Consorzio, the producers’ consortium, celebrates it with relish.

Here, however, honesty is required. In the Middle Ages the word vernaccia was applied to several wines of different origins, and some have suggested a link with Liguria. That Dante had Vernaccia di San Gimignano in mind is plausible, given the wine’s fame in the Tuscany of his day, but it cannot be proved. On firmer ground are the later Tuscan references: Cecco Angiolieri names it in a sonnet, and Francesco Redi, in his *Bacco in Toscana* of 1685, has words of praise for it.

## A grape related to no one

The name still causes confusion today. Italy has other Vernaccias, such as that of Oristano in Sardinia or that of Serrapetrona in the Marche, which makes a sparkling red. As far as ampelographers can tell, they are not related to Vernaccia di San Gimignano at all: they are different grapes sharing an old name. The origins of the Tuscan one remain disputed, with theories ranging from the eastern Mediterranean to Italy itself.

The production rules are strict about territory and relatively open about composition. The grapes must come from hillside vineyards in the commune of San Gimignano alone, in the province of Siena, at no more than 500 metres above sea level. Vernaccia must make up at least 85%; the rest may come from other non-aromatic white grapes, with a combined cap of 10% for Sauvignon and Riesling and the explicit exclusion of varieties such as Traminer and Moscato. The maximum yield is 9 tonnes per hectare. The Riserva must age for at least 11 months from 1 January after the harvest, plus at least three months in bottle, and start from a minimum natural alcohol of 12%. Oak is allowed, not required.

In the glass Vernaccia is a dry white of good acidity, with a faintly bitter finish that its admirers regard as the grape’s signature and its detractors as a flaw. On the yellow sands and Pliocene clays of these hills, the legacy of an ancient seabed, the best versions have a tension that keeps them interesting even after a few years, the Riservas above all.

## The white of a red-wine region

Vernaccia’s problem is its address. Tuscany is the land of Sangiovese, of Chianti Classico, of Brunello, of the wines of Bolgheri: visitors come looking for reds, and the local white becomes the thing to drink at lunch, on a terrace, gazing at the towers. San Gimignano, moreover, has been on UNESCO’s World Heritage list since 1990, and a huge share of Vernaccia is sold to people passing through for a few hours. It is a commercial blessing and an image trap: the wine of the towers risks remaining a day-trip wine.

The twentieth century did not help. In the first half of the century the grape, a shy cropper and harder to grow, lost ground to the more productive Trebbiano and Malvasia. The revival began in the 1960s, around the time of the DOC, and since then a number of producers have worked on selections, single vineyards and Riservas to prove that Vernaccia can stand comparison with Italy’s great whites. On price it remains approachable: in 2026 Gambero Rosso devoted a selection to the best Vernaccias under 20 euros, a sign that the shelf still offers plenty of choice in that bracket.

For those living in Italy there is an obvious opportunity. San Gimignano is less than an hour from Florence or Siena, and it pays to go on a weekday, away from the tour-group season, visiting a winery in the hills rather than the wine shops beneath the walls. At the table Vernaccia asks for nothing complicated: it suits *crostini*, vegetables from the garden, freshwater fish, and its bitter edge copes well with artichoke, which wrong-foots almost every other wine.

## A record to be earned every day

My view is that Vernaccia di San Gimignano is one of Italy’s most underrated whites, and that its own tourist success is partly to blame. A sixty-year-old record is not enough to be taken seriously: those who drink it need to stop thinking of it as the day-trip white and start seeking it out the way they would a Verdicchio or a Fiano. The paperwork is all there, from 1276 to Italy’s first DOC. The rest is up to the producers in the vineyard, and to us at the table.
