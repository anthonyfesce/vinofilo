Some vineyards you visit; others you climb. On the Mosel, between Trier and Koblenz, the river traces slow meanders and the vines rise almost vertically up walls of dark slate, so steep that an Italian immediately thinks of the terraces of the Cinque Terre or the dry-stone walls of Valtellina. From those slopes comes the most recognisable Riesling in the world: light in alcohol, taut as a violin string, sometimes sweet, sometimes dry. And it is precisely that “sometimes” that floors anyone who picks up a German bottle.

## A valley built on slate

The Mosel is considered Germany’s oldest wine region: it was the Romans who brought the vine here on a large scale, and several Roman-era wine presses have been found in the valley. Today it covers just under 8,300 hectares, according to the German Wine Institute’s 2025 figures, and Riesling accounts for more than 5,100 of them, roughly six vines in ten. The rest is mostly Müller-Thurgau and Elbling, an ancient grape of the upper valley.

The region also takes in two small tributaries, the Saar and the Ruwer, which give even more nervy, cool-toned wines. But the emblematic landscape is the Middle Mosel, where the south-facing slopes are covered in Devonian slate. The stone holds the day’s warmth, drains water and, according to a cliché that is hard to prove but impossible to uproot, can be “tasted” in the glass.

About half the region’s vineyards lie on steep or terraced slopes, where machines cannot go and almost everything is picked by hand. The extreme case is the Bremmer Calmont, which the German Wine Institute describes as Europe’s steepest vineyard, with gradients of up to 60%: a hill 290 metres high where only 13 of 22 hectares are still planted, and where manual work can reach 1,800 hours per hectare a year. In Italy we would call it *viticoltura eroica*, “heroic viticulture”, and it would be no exaggeration.

## The Prädikat ladder, without a dictionary

The German wine law of 1971 built a hierarchy based not on place, as Italian or French appellations are, but on the ripeness of the grapes at harvest, measured in degrees Oechsle, that is, the density of the must. The more sugar in the berry, the higher you climb. There are six rungs of Prädikatswein:

- **Kabinett**: the first level, grapes ripe but not overripe. On the Mosel it is the Riesling of lightness, often with a touch of sweetness and very little alcohol.
- **Spätlese**: literally “late harvest”, more concentration and more body.
- **Auslese**: “selection”, chosen bunches, sometimes touched by noble rot.
- **Beerenauslese (BA)**: berries selected one by one, almost always botrytised.
- **Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA)**: berries shrivelled by botrytis, the top of the ladder, with a minimum of 150 degrees Oechsle.
- **Eiswein**: grapes frozen on the vine, picked and pressed at a temperature of at least minus seven degrees, with ripeness comparable to Beerenauslese.

The minimum thresholds for Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese vary by region and by grape variety, which is why we avoid quoting an exact figure for the Mosel here. Vinofilo has already covered noble rot and Germany’s great sweet wines in its piece on botrytis; the point here is a different one. The Prädikat tells you how ripe the grapes were, not how sweet the wine is. A Spätlese can be dry; a Kabinett can be off-dry. It is the misunderstanding generations of drinkers have tripped over.

## Trocken, the word that really matters

To know whether a wine is dry you need to look for another word: **trocken**. By law a trocken wine has no more than 4 grams of residual sugar per litre, or up to 9 if total acidity is no more than 2 grams below the sugar. It is a rule that suits Riesling very well, since its high acidity makes a wine taste dry when on paper it is not entirely so. Between dry and sweet sit the terms halbtrocken and feinherb, and here German clarity begins to wobble: feinherb, in particular, has no precise legal definition.

The practical result is that the Mosel holds two worlds. On one side is the traditional fruity Riesling, with residual sweetness and alcohol often around 8 degrees, balanced by an acidity that makes it surprisingly easy to drink. On the other is an ever more widespread dry Riesling, which has changed the face of the region over the past twenty years, partly in pursuit of domestic taste.

## Grosses Gewächs and the revenge of place

While the law looked at sugar, the most ambitious producers started looking at the ground. The VDP, the association that brings together a share of Germany’s most prestigious estates, introduced a vineyard classification that recalls Burgundy: at the top are the Grosse Lage, the finest sites, just below them the Erste Lage, then village wines (Ortswein) and estate wines (Gutswein).

In 2002 the members defined the **Grosses Gewächs**, or GG: the dry wine from a Grosse Lage, recognisable on the bottle by the grape-cluster symbol. Resolutions passed in 2006 set the ceiling at 9 grams of residual sugar. The first GGs came from the 2000 vintage. For the sweeter wines of VDP members the Prädikat words remain, which thus go back to meaning what the public already expected: Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese with sweetness.

For an Italian reader the closest analogy is Barolo’s path with its *menzioni geografiche*, the officially mapped vineyard names: there too, at some point, the name of the site became more important than any other indication. The difference is that in Germany the hierarchy by place was born from a private association, not from the appellation rules, and it coexists with the legal one. Two overlapping maps, then, which do not always match. The law, however, is moving in the same direction: the reform that came into force in January 2021 brings in rules that give more weight to origin, applied from the 2026 vintage, while leaving the Prädikat rungs unchanged.

## Riesling beyond the Mosel

Riesling, it should be said, is not only German. It is grown in Alsace, in Austria, in the Clare and Eden Valleys of Australia, in New York State’s Finger Lakes, and in Italy too, from Alto Adige to the Oltrepò Pavese. But none of these places has made sweetness and lightness so precise an identity. Elsewhere Riesling leans dry and towards higher alcohol; on the Mosel it can stop below 9 degrees and still last decades in the cellar, thanks to an acidity that acts as its backbone.

## Why it is worth learning German

The German system is complicated, and anyone who defends it to the hilt ought to admit as much. But it has one rare merit: it forces you to ask what is really in the glass, beyond the name. In my view the great lesson of the Mosel lies in Kabinett, the lowest and most underrated rung: a wine of few degrees, lively and precise, which in a world of ever more powerful whites looks almost like an act of rebellion. If climate change makes it harder to keep it that light, as one hears feared more and more often, we will lose one of the few things no other region can imitate.
