The name is the first misunderstanding, and the luckiest. Vinho Verde means “green wine”, yet there is nothing green in the glass, and some of it is even red, a purple bordering on ink. For half a century the world knew it as the light, barely sparkling, inexpensive white to drink ice-cold in summer. That was true, but it was never the whole story. And today it is less true every year.

## A name that means something else

The most common explanation links “verde” to youth: a wine for early drinking, released a few months after the harvest rather than aged at length like the reds of the Douro or the Dão. Others think of the landscape, because the Minho, in Portugal’s far north-west, really is the country’s greenest region, soaked by Atlantic rain. The appellation’s own regulatory body cuts the debate short: the name refers neither to colour nor to unripe grapes. It is an identity, not a description.

The region was demarcated in 1908 and today has nine sub-regions, from Monção e Melgaço on the Spanish border to Baião and Paiva further inland. According to the regional body, vineyards cover around 16,000 hectares, although some sources give higher figures: the gap probably comes down to how you count the scattered family plots, of which there are a great many. To put it in terms an Italian would recognise: rather than a large, compact appellation such as Chianti Classico, picture a vast patchwork of vine-planted kitchen gardens.

## Vines up high, maize down below

The Minho landscape explains the style of the wine better than any technical sheet. For centuries the land was divided into tiny parcels, and every metre had to earn its keep. When maize spread through the region in the 16th century, vines were pushed to the edges of the fields, trained up trees and hedges and picked from tall ladders. Later came the high pergolas, with vegetables grown beneath them.

An Italian reader might think of the pergolas of Trentino or Valpolicella, but here the principle was more radical still: the vine lived upstairs, everyday life downstairs. Lifted away from the damp soil, the grapes got more air but ripened with difficulty, and the wines stayed light and sharp. Today many modern vineyards are trained on lower, more manageable trellises, and the difference shows: riper fruit, more flavour, less need for correction.

## Alvarinho, Loureiro and the rest

Classic Vinho Verde is almost always a blend. The recommended white grapes are a handful: Alvarinho, Loureiro, Arinto, Avesso, Azal, Trajadura, Batoca. White wine accounts for the vast majority of production, more than eight bottles in ten according to the most widely quoted estimates.

The international star is Alvarinho, which many readers will know by its Spanish name, Albariño: the same grape that, on the far bank of the Minho river in Galicia, made the fortune of Rías Baixas. In Monção e Melgaço, the northernmost sub-region and the one best sheltered from the Atlantic, it gives fuller, stronger wines than the regional norm, between 11.5% and 14% against the 8.5–11% of classic whites. Thick skins, high acidity, citrus and peach aromas: this is the Vinho Verde that ages, and it proves that “light” is not a law of nature.

Loureiro is the other face: more floral, more delicate, tied above all to the valleys closest to the ocean. And then there is Vinhão, the region’s most planted red, dark, tannic and fiercely acidic, which in northern Portugal traditionally goes with home cooking. For an Italian, the nearest comparison might be a Lambrusco Grasparossa without the softness: disconcerting, and all the more interesting for it.

## The spritz, honest and otherwise

That famous prickle has a curious history. Originally it came from malolactic fermentation restarting in the bottle, and dark bottles also helped hide the haze it left behind. With industrial production, the faint fizz became a trademark, and today it is mostly achieved by adding carbon dioxide. Pressure stays below one bar, so technically this is not a sparkling wine at all: it is closer to a barely-there *pétillant* than to a Prosecco.

Here lies the real dividing line. Mass-market Vinho Verde, slightly sweet and artfully gassed, built the region’s reputation abroad and, at the same time, its trap. The more ambitious producers often work in the opposite direction: still or nearly still wines, dry, from a single grape or a single sub-region, sometimes with a few months on the lees.

## The new quality

Meanwhile the regional body is investing in reviving old, almost vanished grape varieties and in vineyard research, with climate change in mind. That is a signal that goes beyond marketing: a region asking what it should plant in twenty years’ time is no longer thinking only about volume. The weight of commercial history matters too: according to historical accounts, the first documented shipments to England date from 1788, and as early as the 14th century, the regional body notes, wines from this area were leaving for other European ports.

My view is that Vinho Verde suffers from the same ailment as so many Italian wines born popular, from Soave to Lambrusco: the success of the simple product has eclipsed the serious one. The cure is the same, too. Stop asking it to be nothing more than a cheap summer white, and start reading its labels with the attention we would give a Riesling. The green, in the end, was a promise of freshness. Keeping it, with a little more substance, is all it takes.
