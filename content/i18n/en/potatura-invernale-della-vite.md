Drive past a vineyard in January and all you see is dry wood, wire and someone bent over the rows with secateurs. It looks like the dead season. In fact it is the moment when, more than at any other time of year, it is decided how much fruit there will be, where it will ripen and how many more years that vine will stay standing. The harvest is the party; pruning is the contract.

## Why winter

Once the leaves have fallen the vine goes dormant: the sap stops, the buds sleep, the plant wastes no energy. This is when almost all the wood grown during the year, often the great majority of the canes, can be cut away without wounding an organism in full activity. That is why in Italy dry pruning is usually concentrated between December and February, with stragglers into March.

Within that window, though, the calendar is not neutral. The technical bulletins of Italy's regional agrometeorological services put it clearly: pruning early, in December or January, can bring budburst forward by more than a week and leaves wounds open for a long time, exposed to fungal spores. Pruning late, in February or March, causes what Italians call *pianto*, the “weeping” of sap from the cuts as the plant wakes, but it delays budburst. And where April frosts are a real risk, a few days' delay can save a crop.

The result is a common-sense rule followed by many growers: start with the late-budding varieties and leave the early ones until last. Avoid days that are very cold or very damp, the latter being the pathogens' favourites. Nothing romantic about it: this is logistics, with one eye on the weather forecast.

## Guyot and spur-pruned cordon, two philosophies

In Italy the two most widespread trellised training systems are Guyot and *cordone speronato*, spur-pruned cordon, and you can tell them apart from a distance.

In **Guyot** the vine has a trunk from which, each year, one long cane is chosen, the fruiting cane, which is bent along the wire and carries the buds that will bear the grapes; beside it a short spur is left, with a couple of buds, to supply next year's cane. Every winter the old cane is removed and the cycle starts again. It is long pruning, demanding a good eye and a steady hand, and it suits varieties whose buds near the base are not very fruitful: to get bunches you have to go looking further along the cane.

In **spur-pruned cordon** the horizontal arm is permanent and runs along the wire; on it are left many short spurs of two or three buds. It is short pruning, more regular and easier to partly mechanise, suited to varieties with fruitful basal buds. Technical guides list Sangiovese, Trebbiano Toscano and Pecorino among them.

Neither is better in absolute terms. Guyot, with its curve, can give somewhat uneven shoots and ripening along the cane; the cordon, if the spurs are left too long, ages quickly and fills up with old wood. The choice depends on the variety, the vigour of the soil, the climate and, frankly, on local habit and the cost of labour.

## Pruning that follows the sap

Over the past thirty years the debate has shifted from what shape to give the vine to how to cut it. The best-known name is Marco Simonit, who began promoting his approach in Friuli in the early 1990s and who, with his childhood friend Pierpaolo Sirch, founded a consultancy now working in many countries. What Italians call *potatura ramificata*, branched pruning, or more colloquially *potatura dolce*, “gentle pruning”, starts from a simple idea: the vine is a plant with flows of sap, and every cut should be planned so as not to interrupt them.

The principles are few. Build the plant in stages, first the trunk and then the arms, letting it branch in an orderly way. Cut preferably on young wood, one or two years old. Avoid large cuts flush to the trunk: every wound leaves behind a cone of drying wood, according to Simonit roughly one and a half times the diameter of the cut, and if that dead wood sits on the sap's path the vine suffers. The same goes for “willow heads”, the swollen knots created by years of cutting in the same spot, which the regional bulletins advise against precisely because they lead to necrosis of the internal vessels.

This is not a fashion: it is a form of prevention. And it leads us straight to the real problem.

## Trunk diseases, the slow enemy

Esca is today one of the main worries of Italian growers. It is caused by a complex of fungi, including *Phaeomoniella chlamydospora*, *Phaeoacremonium aleophilum* and *Fomitiporia mediterranea*, whose spores, carried by rain, enter the plant precisely through pruning wounds. The most recognisable symptoms are “tiger-striped” leaves, yellow between veins that stay green, and in acute cases apoplexy, the sudden collapse of a whole vine in high summer.

The most insidious thing is that the symptoms come and go: a vine can look cured one year and collapse the next. For decades the only truly effective remedy was sodium arsenite, withdrawn from sale on toxicological and environmental grounds; since then, as the technicians themselves admit, nothing equally decisive has been found.

What remains is prevention, and almost all of it comes down to the secateurs: few, small cuts, disinfected tools, symptomatic vines marked in summer and pruned last, large cuts protected at once, prunings removed from the vineyard. There are also treatments to protect fresh wounds, including some based on antagonistic fungi permitted in organic farming. Where a vine is already infected, the Simonit school practises genuine trunk surgery, cutting out the rotten wood to give the plant a second chance.

## A craft you cannot improvise

My view is that pruning is the most underrated act in the whole chain of wine. We talk about yeasts, oak, amphorae, and almost never about the people who spend the winter in the rows deciding, vine by vine, what to keep and what to take away. Yet a bad cut today can cost a vine ten years from now, and an old vineyard, with its character, is exactly what no winery can buy in a hurry. If there is one serious investment to be made in viticulture, it is teaching people to prune well. The rest, often, follows.
