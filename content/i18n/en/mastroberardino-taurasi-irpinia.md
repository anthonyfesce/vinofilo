In post-war Irpinia, the most sensible advice to a young grower would have been: plant something that sells. Cabernet, Merlot, Chardonnay, the grapes the world was asking for. Antonio Mastroberardino did the opposite, and almost alone. This is the fourth portrait in the "Italian Excellence" series: the house that kept Aglianico, Fiano and Greco alive when nobody was looking for them.

## A craftsman's title and ten generations

The surname already tells a story. According to the account published by Decanter, in the mid-eighteenth century one Pietro di Berardino received the title of *mastro*, master craftsman, and the family name followed. The [house](https://mastroberardino.com/la-storia/) places its origins in Irpinia in the Bourbon land registry of the mid-1700s and counts ten generations at the head of the business. In Atripalda, just outside Avellino, the historic cellars still stand. In 1878 Angelo Mastroberardino officially registered the company with the Chamber of Commerce.

Then came the twentieth century, and with it everything that could go wrong: economic crisis, phylloxera, war. During the conflict the Atripalda cellars served as an air-raid shelter.

## Antonio's choice

In 1945 Michele Mastroberardino died and the business passed to his son Antonio, who was eighteen. He is the decisive figure: he rebuilt the estate and chose to replant the local grapes, Aglianico, Greco and Fiano, instead of international varieties. For decades, the house itself acknowledges, the family was practically alone in Irpinia in defending that heritage, until the 1990s.

The house's 1968 Taurasi Riserva became legendary and helped earn Taurasi its nickname, "the Barolo of the South", whose merits and limits we discussed in [Aglianico, the Barolo of the South](/en/articles/2026.09.29/aglianico-barolo-of-the-south/). In 1993 Taurasi became a DOCG, the first red of southern Italy to do so; today Irpinia has three, with Fiano di Avellino and Greco di Tufo. Antonio Mastroberardino died in January 2014, aged 86. In the mid-1990s his brother Walter had left to found another winery; since 1997 the business has been led by his son Piero, now chairman.

## Pompeii and the meaning of continuity

One project sums up the house's philosophy well. In 1996 the archaeological authority of Pompeii entrusted Mastroberardino with replanting vines inside the excavations, with varieties and training systems inspired by ancient ones. Since 2001 that work has produced a wine, Villa dei Misteri, a red that is less a product than a statement: Campanian viticulture comes from far away, and those who look after it have a duty to remember. The website of the [Archaeological Park of Pompeii](https://pompeiisites.org/) is the place to start for anyone wanting to see those vines in context.

The risk for a house so bound to memory would be turning into a museum. Mastroberardino avoids it with a range built precisely on its vineyards: Taurasi Radici, first made in 1986 from the grapes of the Montemarano estate; Fiano di Avellino Radici, from the Santo Stefano del Sole vineyard, which climbs to 600 metres; Greco di Tufo Novaserra, from a 16-hectare vineyard at Montefusco.

## What to taste first

The whites are straw yellow, leaning to gold in the Greco; Taurasi is a deep ruby red that turns garnet with age. To get to know the house:

- **Fiano di Avellino Radici**, the white that best expresses Irpinia's altitude and its capacity to age;
- **Greco di Tufo Novaserra**, more saline and taut;
- **Taurasi Radici**, a wine to wait for: young Aglianico is severe and rewards patience.

On prices the series' rule applies: they vary by vintage and shop. The whites sit among good appellation whites, Taurasi among age-worthy reds, with riservas and older vintages considerably dearer. For the two Irpinian whites there is more in [Fiano and Greco](/en/articles/2026.11.01/fiano-greco-irpinia-ageworthy-whites/).

## How to visit

Visits to the Atripalda cellar, with its painted vaults, are arranged through [Mastroberardino Experience](https://www.mastroberardinoexperience.it/). The family's history, with documents and old vintages, is gathered in the company museum, [MIMA](https://museomima.it/).

Mastroberardino's lesson is simple to state and very hard to practise: sometimes the most innovative thing you can do is not to change. In the years when Italian wine was chasing international taste, someone in Irpinia held firm. The southern Italian wines we now drink with curiosity owe him a great deal.
