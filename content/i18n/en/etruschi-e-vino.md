Twenty-five centuries ago, in a port on the French coast just south of present-day Montpellier, someone was unloading amphorae of wine shipped from Cerveteri. Inside was a resinated red scented with herbs, and the buyer was no refined Greek colonist but a Gaul, for whom wine was still an imported luxury. It is a curious thought for anyone living in Italy: before France became the France of wine, its first teachers, or at least its first suppliers, spoke Etruscan.

## Amphorae that talk

The place is called Lattara, today Lattes. In 2013 a team of French and American researchers led by Patrick McGovern of the University of Pennsylvania Museum published in the journal PNAS the chemical analysis of three Etruscan amphorae found intact and unwashed in a sealed context in the town’s merchant quarter. Their date: roughly 525 to 475 BC. Their shape shows they were made at Cisra, that is, Cerveteri.

The residue of the contents was still on the bottom. And the residue spoke: tartaric acid, the chemical marker of grapes and wine; compounds from pine resin, used to seal and preserve; traces of herbs such as rosemary, basil or thyme. A wine, in short, that would strike us as closer to an aromatic preparation than to a modern red. No surprise there: anyone who has read our piece on wine in ancient Rome knows that the ancient taste for “pure” wine was anything but a given.

The most interesting part, though, lies elsewhere. At Lattara archaeologists also found a limestone press, dated to around 425 BC, bearing traces of tartaric acid, and nearby thousands of seeds from domesticated vines. According to the study, it is the oldest biomolecular evidence of winemaking in France. The sequence is clear: first the Etruscan amphorae arrive, then the Gauls learn to make wine for themselves.

## A ship sixty metres down

If Lattara tells of the arrival, the sea tells of the voyage. In 1999, during a survey off Hyères that had an entirely different purpose, the wreck now known as Grand Ribaud F was located. It lies about 60 metres down and was excavated between 2000 and 2003 by the underwater archaeologists of the French Ministry of Culture. Its cargo dates from the beginning of the 5th century BC, around 500, and included thousands of Greek and Etruscan amphorae, along with metal tableware and Greek black-figure pottery.

A ship like this is a snapshot: it shows an organised trade, with cargoes that were enormous for the time, in which wine travelled together with the objects for drinking it. Because the point was not simply to sell a drink, but an entire way of behaving at table.

## The banquet, with women

We know that way of behaving well, because the Etruscans painted it on the walls of their tombs. At Tarquinia, in the Monterozzi necropolis, the Tomb of the Leopards, dated to around 470–450 BC, shows three elegantly dressed couples, men and women, reclining at a banquet, attended by serving boys and musicians. From the 6th century diners reclined on low couches, as at the Greek symposium, and wine was mixed with water in the krater, poured from the oinochoe and ladled with the kyathos. Some of the names have reached us in Etruscan: the wine cup the Greeks called kylix was *culichna*.

The difference from Greece lies precisely in those couples. In Athens, respectable women had no place at the symposium. In Etruria they did, and it scandalised the Greeks: the historian Theopompus, in the 4th century BC, in a passage preserved by Athenaeus, describes Etruscan women who drink heavily, toast whomever they please and do not even sit beside their husbands. It is a foreigner’s gossip and should be read as such; but it tells us how far wine in Etruria was a social affair rather than an exclusively male rite.

There was a sacred dimension too. From the mid-6th century the Etruscan god Fufluns takes on the iconography of the Greek Dionysus, with the kantharos and vine shoots. Wine was used in libations and funeral rites, and banqueting sets often ended up in tombs, as if the party were meant to carry on elsewhere.

## When the customer becomes the competitor

The commercial story, however, has no happy ending for the Etruscans. Around 600 BC Greeks from Phocaea founded Massalia, the future Marseille. After a couple of generations the colonists began producing their own wine in a recognisable type of amphora, and that Massaliot wine came to dominate southern France until the arrival of the Romans at the end of the 2nd century BC. According to the archaeologist Michael Dietler, who has dug at Lattes for many years, the Gauls had no wish to become Greeks: from Greek culture they took the wine and the cups, and fitted them into their own feasts, on their own terms.

The Etruscans, in other words, opened a market and then lost it. A script Italian wine knows well: you teach others to drink, then the others learn to produce.

## What remains

Little that can be measured remains, and it is worth saying so. We do not know for certain which grape varieties they used, nor how their wine was made beyond what the chemical residues reveal. Tradition credits the Etruscans with the vine “married” to trees, still visible in parts of central Italy and Campania, but it is a legacy more often told than proven.

And yet, in my view, the Etruscan legacy is more concrete than it seems. It lies not in a grape or a recipe but in an idea: wine as something to share, at table, between men and women, with beautiful objects made for the purpose. And in a fact that might occasionally be mentioned to our cousins beyond the Alps: among the first amphorae of wine to reach the coasts of Gaul, many came from Italy. They had been made some fifty kilometres from Rome, in a city we know today mostly for its tombs.
