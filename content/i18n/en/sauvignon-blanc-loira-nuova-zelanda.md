Few wines can be identified blindfold as easily as a Sauvignon Blanc. You only have to bring your nose to the glass: freshly cut grass, tomato leaf, grapefruit, sometimes passion fruit, sometimes that whiff of gunflint the French call *silex*. It is a grape that cannot keep quiet, which may be why the world has split into two camps: those who love it for its energy and those who find it a touch too loud. Yet the recent history of Sauvignon is not a matter of taste. It is the story of how an island at the other end of the earth took a French grape and turned it into a national brand.

## The Loire, where everything is named after a village

Sauvignon’s spiritual home is the central Loire, where two appellations eye each other from opposite banks of the river. On the left bank lies Sancerre, an AOC for white wine since 1936, spread across 14 communes, with a planted area of around 2,600 hectares in 2006. On the right bank, in the Nièvre department, lies Pouilly-Fumé, an AOC since 1937 and smaller, at around 1,200 hectares.

The name Pouilly-Fumé comes, according to the most widely accepted explanation, from the greyish, smoke-coloured bloom that covers the ripe berries, and also from the smoky, mineral aroma many associate with the local soils. In Sancerre, growers distinguish three main soils: the chalky *terres blanches*, which give fuller wines; the stony *caillottes*, which produce more immediate, fruit-forward whites; and *silex*, flint, credited with the tautest and longest-lived wines.

One historical detail explains a great deal. Before phylloxera, the insect that ravaged Europe’s vineyards at the end of the 19th century, Sancerre also grew Pinot Noir and Gamay. When the vineyards were rebuilt, growers largely chose Sauvignon, partly because it took well to grafting onto American rootstock. Success came after the Second World War, when Sancerre became the easy-drinking white of Parisian bistros, much as Beaujolais was for reds. A humble origin that today’s prices tend to make people forget.

## Marlborough, the plot twist

Then came New Zealand. In 1973 Montana Wines planted the first large-scale vineyards in Marlborough, at the northern tip of the South Island, between the Wairau and Awatere valleys. Sunny weather, cool nights brought in by the sea, a long growing season. In 1985 Cloudy Bay’s Sauvignon caught the attention of international critics, and from there the world discovered a new style: more aromatic, more tropical, more explosive than any Sancerre.

The figures tell of an unparalleled rise. According to the data reported for Marlborough, the area planted to Sauvignon Blanc grew from about 4,500 hectares in 2003 to more than 23,000 in 2018. In 2020 the region produced around three quarters of New Zealand’s wine, with 70 per cent of the country’s vineyard area and 85 per cent of its exports. Today New Zealand has around 42,000 hectares of vines, produces just over 1 per cent of the world’s wine, exports about 90 per cent of it and earns more than NZ$2.1 billion a year. The UK, the United States and Australia together take more than 70 per cent of those exports.

It is a rare case: an entire country identified with one grape, and almost with a single region. The strength is obvious; so is the fragility. When a model works this well, the risk is that it becomes a formula, and that Marlborough Sauvignon comes to stand for the very idea of Sauvignon, erasing everything else.

## Bordeaux, where Sauvignon is a team player

In Bordeaux, Sauvignon does a different job: it works as part of a team. In the dry whites of Graves and Pessac-Léognan, as in the great sweet wines of Sauternes, it is almost always paired with Sémillon, which brings body, a waxy texture and ageing potential, while Sauvignon supplies freshness and perfume. The result is a less exuberant, more structured white, often aged in oak.

Bordeaux is also bound up with the most surprising genetic discovery linked to this grape. In 1997 Carole Meredith and John Bowers, at the University of California, Davis, used DNA analysis to show that Cabernet Sauvignon is the offspring of a cross between Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc. The world’s most famous red, in other words, has a white parent, and its name had been saying so for centuries without anyone paying attention.

## Alto Adige and Friuli, the Italian way

It may come as a surprise to readers outside Italy, but the country has a serious Sauvignon tradition, concentrated in the north-east. In Alto Adige, the mostly German-speaking province at the foot of the Dolomites (also known as Südtirol), the grape arrived from France around the turn of the 20th century, and in 1956 Cantina Terlano bottled the area’s first varietal Sauvignon. The vineyards climb the slopes, at Cortaccia between 425 and 600 metres, on dizzying gradients. The result is taut, cutting whites in which tropical fruit gives way to mountain herbs and stone.

In Friuli Venezia Giulia, on the border with Slovenia, Sauvignon is at home in the Collio hills and on the gravelly plains of the Isonzo, alongside Friulano and Ribolla Gialla. Here the style is often broader and more textured, with some producers giving it long ageing and treating it as a wine for the cellar. We told this story when writing about the great whites of the Collio: it is a region that learned early to let local and international grapes live side by side without any inferiority complex.

Neither area has New Zealand’s volumes, and neither is looking for them. At its best, Italian Sauvignon sits halfway between the Loire and Marlborough: cooler and more mineral than the New Zealanders, at times more generous than Sancerre.

## The grape that risks becoming a caricature of itself

My view is that Sauvignon Blanc pays a price for being so recognisable. A wine you can identify at first sniff is a huge commercial advantage, and Marlborough has proved that better than anyone. But it is also a trap, because it pushes producers to make the Sauvignon the public expects rather than the one the place could give. The most interesting things today are happening at the margins: on the flint of Sancerre, on the slopes of Alto Adige, in New Zealand vineyards trying to be a little less loud. The best Sauvignon is the one that, for once, does not shout.
