There comes a moment every year when half of Europe decides wine should be drunk piping hot, sweetened and out of a mug. It happens between late November and New Year, among the wooden huts of the Christmas markets, and it unites nations that argue about wine on every other count. Even the sternest drinker, the kind who measures serving temperature to the degree, surrenders to a steaming mug in Bolzano or Nuremberg. And rightly so: hot wine is not lesser wine, it is something else entirely.

## A very old habit

The idea of heating and spicing wine was not born at the Christmas market. The earliest mention of spiced, heated wine is usually traced to a comedy by Plautus, the *Curculio*, from the second century BC. Rome's legions carried wine, vines and recipes as far as the Rhine and the Danube, and the climate did the rest.

In the Middle Ages the luxury version was called hippocras: red wine, sugar and ground spices, cinnamon, ginger, cloves, grains of paradise. An English cookbook of 1390, *The Forme of Cury*, preserves the formula. As for the Germans, tradition credits the oldest documented Glühwein tankard to a Count of Katzenelnbogen, around 1420: gilded silver, complete with a lock. A detail that says a great deal about how precious spices were at the time.

## One wine, many names

The German name, Glühwein, means roughly “glowing wine”, after the temperature it is brought to. The canonical recipe calls for red wine, cinnamon, cloves, star anise, orange, sugar and sometimes vanilla. Order it *mit Schuss* and you get a dash of rum or another spirit.

In the Nordic countries and Estonia it is called glögg (in Sweden) or glögi (in Finland), and it comes with a precise ritual: the spices are heated with the wine to 60–70 °C, left to infuse for at least an hour and then reheated. It is served with raisins and blanched almonds in the glass, alongside ginger biscuits. In France, vin chaud is usually red wine with honey, cinnamon and orange, and it is the natural companion of Alpine ski slopes; in Geneva it is a fixture of the Escalade, the city's December festival.

In Britain mulled wine has no single recipe, just a broad licence with spices, from cardamom to fennel. Dickens mentions a period variant, *smoking bishop*, which hardly anyone drinks today. Further east the map widens: svařák in the Czech Republic, grzaniec in Poland (more often sweetened with honey), kuhano vino in the Balkans, vin fiert in Romania and Moldova, greyano vino in Bulgaria, with honey and peppercorns. In Russia and Ukraine the usual name, glintveyn, comes straight from the German. Russian sbiten, often included in such lists, was originally a hot honey-and-herb drink, usually without alcohol: some replace the water with wine, but it is not the same thing.

And Italy? Here hot wine is mostly a habit of the North and the mountains, and it is called vin brulé, “burnt wine”. The name is French, yet the French never use it: they say vin chaud. It is one of those small linguistic paradoxes that make wine such good conversation.

## What the law says

Hot wine looks like the very essence of DIY, yet in Europe it has strict rules. The EU regulation on aromatised wine products (No 251 of 2014) states that Glühwein must be made exclusively from red or white wine, with an actual alcoholic strength of no less than 7% vol., and that adding water is forbidden. If it is made from white wine, the label must say so. A similar definition, again with a 7% minimum, applies to Nordic glögg.

For anyone making it at home these rules hardly matter, but they explain one thing: what you buy ready-made, in a bottle or a carton, is a defined product, not just any wine warmed up.

## Which wine to use

This is where it gets practical. The golden rule is simple: no grand bottles. Heat and spice wipe out exactly what you pay for in a serious wine, its aromatic finesse and the complexity of age. But nothing faulty either: a corked or vinegary wine stays that way, cinnamon or not.

The ideal wine is red, young and fruity, with soft tannins. Hard tannins, once heated and sweetened, turn bitter and coarse. Among Italian options, a young Barbera, a Merlot, a current-vintage Montepulciano d'Abruzzo or an unoaked Sangiovese all work well. White is a legitimate variation, sanctioned even by law, provided it is aromatic and not too sharp.

For the basic recipe you need one 0.75-litre bottle, a strip of unwaxed orange or lemon peel, a couple of cinnamon sticks, a few cloves, one star anise and sugar or honey to taste. The delicate part is the heat: hot wine must never boil. Ethanol boils at around 78 °C, well below water, and a wine left to simmer loses alcohol and gains a stewed taste. The sweet spot is the glögg range, around 60–70 °C, when the wine steams but does not stir.

## Long live wine in a mug

My view is that hot wine deserves more respect than wine criticism gives it, which is usually none at all. It is not a way to get rid of your worst bottles but one of the few wine rituals genuinely shared across Europe, from Stockholm to Sofia. All it asks is a little care: an honest wine, fresh spices, no boiling. The cold, the square and the company do the rest.
