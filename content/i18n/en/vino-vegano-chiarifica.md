It sounds like a quiz question: wine is made from grapes, so it is plant-based, so it is vegan. Not quite, or at least not always. The reason lies not in what is in the wine, but in what has passed through the wine while it was being made: a range of substances of animal origin used to make it clear and stable, which are then almost entirely removed. Almost, precisely. And that “almost” is where the question is decided.

## Fining, or how to make the haze fall

A young wine is full of suspended particles: fragments of yeast, grape proteins, polyphenols, small colloids that make it hazy or that, over time, may form deposits and cloudiness in the bottle. Many of these particles settle on their own with rest and racking. Others do not, and this is where fining comes in.

The principle is old and rather elegant. A substance is added to the wine that binds to the unwanted particles, through electric charge or chemical affinity, and drags them down to the bottom of the tank. The clear wine is then separated from the deposit, and often filtered. The substance used, the fining agent, does its work and leaves: that is why the law treats it as a processing aid, not an ingredient.

Fining also serves to correct taste. Some fining agents bind preferentially to the harshest, most astringent tannins and are used to soften an overly rough red; others remove the compounds that yellow a white or make it taste bitter.

## Fining agents of animal origin

And here we come to the point. Traditional fining agents are largely animal proteins, and each has its own story:

- **Egg albumen.** Beaten egg white is the classic fining agent for great barrel-aged reds: it binds to the most aggressive tannins and makes them precipitate, rounding out the wine.
- **Casein and caseinates.** These are milk proteins, used mainly on whites to remove the compounds that make them oxidise and turn yellow.
- **Gelatine.** Obtained from animal tissue, it is one of the most widespread fining agents for reds and musts.
- **Isinglass.** Obtained from the swim bladders of certain fish, it is delicate and suited to whites.

None of these substances is meant to stay in the wine, and in the vast majority of cases very little or nothing measurable remains. But people on a vegan diet often reason about the process, not just the residue: if an animal derivative was used in production, the wine is not vegan to them. And that is a coherent position.

## What the label says, and what it does not

Here European law is only half precise. Eggs and milk are allergens, and since 2012 [Regulation (EU) No 579/2012](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/IT/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32012R0579) has required them to be declared on the label when their residue can be detected in the finished wine using official methods of analysis. The rule applies to wines from grapes of the 2012 harvest onwards, labelled after 30 June of that year, and provides for wording such as “egg”, “egg protein”, “milk”, “milk casein” or “milk protein”, possibly with a pictogram.

The tricky point is that word “detected”. A wine fined with albumen in which analysis no longer finds any trace does not have to declare anything. Gelatine is not an allergen that must be declared, and isinglass, although it comes from fish, is exempt from the obligation when used to fine wine and beer: neither ever appears on the label. Nor does the list of ingredients, mandatory since 8 December 2023 for wines produced from then on, help: processing aids that do not remain in the finished product are not listed.

The upshot: in most cases the back label will not tell you whether a wine is vegan.

## Vegan wine, and the missing seal

Alternatives to animal fining agents exist and are increasingly used. Bentonite, a clay, is the most common fining agent for removing proteins from whites, and it is mineral. PVPP is a synthetic polymer that binds polyphenols. There are plant proteins, from pea, potato and wheat, that replace gelatine, and chitosan, derived from fungi. Then there is the simplest route: not fining at all, letting time and gravity do their work and accepting a slightly less brilliant wine. It is the choice of many natural-wine producers, but also of classic wineries that prefer to intervene as little as possible.

As for the word “vegan” on the label, there is no legal definition in the European Union. The 2011 regulation on food information to consumers provided for the Commission to establish one, but so far this has not happened. Whoever writes “vegan” on the bottle does so on their own responsibility, often relying on a private certification, with standards that vary from one body to another and that usually look at the vineyard and the materials too, not just at fining.

## How to find your way

For those on a vegan diet, the practical advice is to look for a certification or ask the winery directly how it fines: it is a question a serious producer can answer in one line. Unfined and unfiltered wines often say so on the label, and that is a good clue, though on its own it guarantees nothing.

Our view is that the question says something interesting even to those who are not vegan. It is a reminder that wine is a processed product, not grape juice left to chance, and that the perfect clarity we take for granted has a cost and a technique. Asking how a wine was fined is a legitimate curiosity, like asking which vineyard it comes from. And the answer often tells you more about the winery than any medal.
