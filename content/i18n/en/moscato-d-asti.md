There is an Italian wine that sits below six degrees of alcohol, can be drunk at four in the afternoon without a shred of guilt and, at some point, found its way into American rap lyrics. Moscato d'Asti is one of wine’s more endearing paradoxes: a farm product that is brutally hard to make, born on hills too steep for tractors, which outside Italy has become almost a common noun. To understand it, you first have to clear up a confusion that trips up many foreigners, and a few Italians: Moscato d'Asti and Asti Spumante are not the same thing.

## Same grape, two wines

Both come from the same denomination, Asti DOCG, which covers 51 communes across the provinces of Asti, Cuneo and Alessandria in southern Piedmont, the north-western region best known abroad for Barolo. Both are made from Moscato bianco (Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains), at least 97% according to the rules, an aromatic grape that, unusually, really does taste of grapes once it becomes wine. The difference lies in the cellar.

**Asti Spumante**, which the rules also call simply Asti, is a fully sparkling wine. It ferments in autoclaves, large pressurised steel tanks: this is the Martinotti method, known abroad as Charmat and used for Prosecco too (we covered it in our piece on the traditional method versus Charmat). Its actual alcohol must be at least 6%, the bubbles are full, the mousse generous. There is also a traditional-method version, refermented in bottle, but it remains a niche.

**Moscato d'Asti** is a different creature. It is not a sparkling wine but a gently fizzy one, what Italians call *frizzante*: its carbon dioxide comes only from the fermentation itself, with no second fermentation, and its pressure may not exceed 2.5 bar, whereas under EU law a sparkling wine must have at least 3, and in practice usually a great deal more. Fermentation is stopped early, while much of the grape’s sugar is still there. The rules set actual alcohol between 4.5% and 6.5%.

And the famous 5.5%? It is the figure printed on a great many labels, but it is not a rule: it is simply where many producers choose to stop, halfway along the permitted range. The rest of the potential alcohol stays in the glass as sweetness, balanced by an acidity that, when the wine is well made, keeps it from cloying. For a reader used to wines at 13 or 14 degrees, it is almost a different drink.

The numbers show this is no curiosity. In recent years the denomination has swung between 75 and more than 90 million bottles a year; in 2025, according to Consorzio figures reported by the trade press, it was around 75 million, of which just under 50 million were Asti Spumante and about 25 million Moscato d'Asti. Only one bottle in ten stays in Italy.

## Canelli and the sorì

The historic heart of Moscato is Canelli, a small town on the Belbo river also famous for the cellars dug deep beneath its hill, part of the vineyard landscapes of Langhe, Roero and Monferrato that have been on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 2014. The Canelli production rules cite records of Moscato in the area as early as 1344.

This is where a word worth learning comes in: *sorì*. In Piedmontese dialect it means the sunniest slopes of a hill, the ones that catch the best light and have always been home to the most coveted vineyards. In Moscato country the sorì are often ferociously steep: across some 1,400 hectares gradients exceed 40% and reach 60%, too much for machines. One local grower has reckoned on almost a thousand hours of work per hectare per year, all of it by hand. And yet, according to the same report, grapes from these slopes often fetch the same price as those from flat vineyards. That is the economic knot at the centre of the whole Moscato story.

From that knot came the most recent move: Canelli has become a DOCG in its own right, covering 17 communes in the provinces of Asti and Cuneo. The rules are stricter: Moscato bianco only, harvesting exclusively by hand, and a Riserva that cannot be released before 30 months, at least 20 of them in bottle. Production is small, around one million bottles. It is a bet that Moscato can be a wine of place, not just a style. A bet with roots: as early as the mid-1970s Romano Dogliotti bottled the first estate Moscato d'Asti, turning a product of the big sparkling-wine houses into a grower’s wine.

## How Moscato went hip hop

And then there is America. Between 2010 and 2012 Moscato exploded in the United States, and the engine was not a critic but music. In 2005 Kanye West had served a Moscato d'Asti at one of his parties; mentions of Moscato followed in songs by several rappers, among them a remix by West himself, Lil' Kim and Ne-Yo. In early 2012 the trade press was reporting striking Nielsen figures: Moscato sales up more than 80% in a year, over 300 million dollars in 2011, more than triple the 2009 level, and a share that had risen from 1% to around 3% of all table wine sold in the country.

Honesty is needed here. Much of that boom was not Moscato d'Asti at all: it was sweet, fizzy wine labelled generically as “Moscato”, made in California and elsewhere and sold for under 10 dollars. The Italian name did the pulling, but the product was often something else. Still, something stuck: today the United States is Moscato d'Asti’s leading market, and according to the Consorzio’s 2025 figures it takes more than half of it, around 58%. That same year, however, American sales fell, while Asia, and China above all, grew.

## A light wine to take seriously

In my view Moscato d'Asti is still paying for its own charm. It is so easy to drink that it is hard to think of it as a fine wine, and its American success, with all its noise, has not helped. But climb up to Canelli and look at those gradients, and you understand that behind those five and a half degrees lies more toil than in many grand reds. The challenge for the coming years is to convince the world, starting with its American fans, that Moscato is neither a music genre nor a generic style: it is a precise place in Piedmont. If Canelli manages to say so on the label, it will have done more than ten advertising campaigns.
