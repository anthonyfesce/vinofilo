Of all the questions put to winegrowers, one has lately become almost a loyalty test: native yeasts or cultured? Answer “native” and you are promptly enlisted among the artisans; admit to the packet and you risk being taken for an industrialist. It is a convenient divide, but a poor description of reality. Yeast matters, very much so, just not in the way it is talked about across the wine-shop counter.

## Who is really at work in the must

Wine is born when a yeast turns the sugars in grape juice into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Nothing controversial there. The question is which yeast.

On the skins and in freshly crushed must, the most numerous population is not the one that will finish the job. The so-called non-*Saccharomyces* species dominate: above all the genus *Hanseniaspora*, alongside *Metschnikowia*, *Candida*, *Pichia* and *Torulaspora*. They get fermentation going, carry it through the first days and contribute to the aromas. But they have little tolerance for alcohol: many give up when the must reaches around 6%. At that point *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* takes over, hardier in the face of alcohol and sulphur dioxide, and carries the wine to dryness.

Here comes the first paradox. *Saccharomyces* is rare on grapes: an often-quoted estimate puts it at one berry in a thousand, and in some vineyards it can be almost entirely absent. So where does the yeast that finishes “spontaneous” fermentations come from? Largely from the winery itself: walls, tanks, pumps, barrels, the very air of a place where wine has been made for years.

## The winery paradox

This is where things get complicated. A study published in *PLoS One* in 2016, carried out in a Canadian winery and three Pinot Noir vineyards, genetically analysed the *Saccharomyces* strains present in spontaneous fermentations. The finding is awkward for both camps: those fermentations were not dominated by commercial yeasts as such, but by a diverse population of strains genetically similar to commercial ones, probably descended from them and now resident in the winery. Alongside these, however, there were also distinct populations, stable from one year to the next and unrelated to any strain on the market.

It is a single case, and it cannot be extended to every cellar in Europe. But it makes an important point: “native” does not automatically mean “from this vineyard and no other”. A winery that used cultured yeasts for years and then switches to spontaneous fermentation may find itself fermenting with the descendants of that packet. The link with place, where it exists, runs through the history of the cellar as well as through the soil.

## The packet, from science to market

Cultured yeasts are not a recent marketing invention. The thread starts with Louis Pasteur, who demonstrated the role of microorganisms in fermentation, and with Emil Christian Hansen, who developed the pure culture for beer in Copenhagen. In 1894 a station devoted to isolating and distributing pure wine yeasts opened at Geisenheim, in Germany. The commercial leap came much later: in 1974 Lallemand, working with Scott Laboratories in California, began producing its first selected wine yeast in active dry form, using strains from the University of California, Davis collection.

What does a winemaker look for in a cultured yeast? Reliability above all: a prompt start, a finish without stalling, little volatile acidity and few unpleasant odours. Then, sometimes, a style: some strains are chosen to boost certain fruity or floral aromas, and this is where the suspicion of a “constructed” wine arises, identical from Bordeaux to Bolgheri. The suspicion is not unfounded, but it concerns how the tool is used, not the tool itself.

## The risks of going wild

Spontaneous fermentation is a gamble with precise rules. In the first days, when alcohol is still low and the wild yeasts are working slowly, there is room for acetic bacteria, and therefore for volatile acidity. A slow or stuck fermentation leaves residual sugar, perfect food for unwanted microorganisms such as *Brettanomyces*, the culprit behind the notorious farmyard and sticking-plaster aromas. We covered this when discussing organic, biodynamic and natural wine: some faults turn up more often in low-intervention wines, and calling them “character” does not make them any better.

Those who work well with spontaneous fermentation know this and plan for it: perfectly healthy grapes, obsessive hygiene, temperature control, and often a *pied de cuve*, a small starter fermentation prepared a few days ahead with grapes from the same vineyard, which is then used to inoculate the rest. It is spontaneous fermentation with a guiding hand, and there is nothing wrong with that.

## The Italian middle way

In Italy there is a third route, rarely mentioned at the counter: *lieviti autoctoni selezionati*, selected native yeasts. Universities and research centres isolate strains from the vineyards of a given area, study them, pick the most reliable and multiply them. Work of this kind has been done, for example, on Aglianico and Fiano. It is a clever compromise, trying to hold safety and local identity together, though purists will not be satisfied: to them, a yeast multiplied in a laboratory remains a cultured yeast, whatever vineyard it came from.

For the buyer, the problem is more practical. The label usually does not say which yeast was used, and “spontaneous fermentation” or “native yeasts” are voluntary statements, not policed by any production rules. The only way to find out is to ask. Winery visits, such as the San Martino open days we have written about, are the right moment: the question is legitimate, and the answer also says a good deal about how a producer talks about their own work.

## Yeast is not terroir

My view is that the debate has loaded yeast with a moral weight it does not deserve. A successful spontaneous fermentation can give more nuanced wines, sometimes livelier ones; a cultured yeast, used with restraint, can give precise and honest wines. What really makes the difference comes earlier, in the vineyard, and later, in the cellar decisions and in time. A mediocre grape does not become great because it ferments on its own, and a great grape is not ruined by a packet. Be wary of anyone who sells you the word “native” as a guarantee: it is useful information, not a certificate of quality. The verdict, as ever, belongs to the glass.
