Every year, give or take a spring, someone announces that Italians have stopped drinking wine. Every year, give or take a Christmas, the supermarkets are stripped of sparkling wine. Both things are true at the same time, which is why Italy’s wine numbers need reading with a little patience. Here are the ones that stand up, with their sources, and a few warnings about those that circulate without one.

## The big picture: less, but hardly little

The most solid figure comes from the OIV, the International Organisation of Vine and Wine, which in May 2026 published its report on 2025. The world drank 208 million hectolitres, 2.7% less than the year before. The European Union lost 3.1%.

In 2025 Italy consumed 20.2 million hectolitres. We remain Europe’s second-largest market, behind France (22 million) and ahead of Germany (17.8). Converted into 75 cl bottles, that is around 2.7 billion bottles: hardly a teetotal nation. The direction, however, is downward for all three of the continent’s big markets, and the trend was not born yesterday.

Anyone after a per-capita figure will have to be patient: the calculations doing the rounds vary depending on whether you divide by the total population or the adult one, and I would rather not add another to the list.

## At the supermarket: reds retreat, bubbles hold

Large-scale retail is the best-measured channel, because every receipt ends up in Circana’s databases, which are also analysed by the Observatory of Unione Italiana Vini. The 2025 figures, presented in April 2026, describe a market that is shrinking but changing shape.

Still red remains the best-selling wine, at over 261 million litres. In 2024, though, it was 271: in a single year some ten million litres vanished. Still whites slipped from 248 to 243 million litres, a gentler fall. Beware, then, of a commonplace: whites are not growing, they are simply losing less ground than reds, and the result is that the gap between the two narrows year after year.

The real growth is in sparkling wine: over 109 million litres, up 1.5%, worth around €750 million. Prosecco alone tops 53 million litres, up 2.6%. Metodo Classico is smaller but moving faster: over 8 million litres, up 6.3% by volume. At the other end sits Lambrusco, which at 28 million litres is still a giant but lost 7.2%. I say this with affection: this magazine has been defending it for some time, but Italian shopping trolleys are not listening yet.

Some intriguing signals come from smaller grapes. Nebbiolo and Pinot Nero grew by almost 10% and 8% by volume, Vermentino by 2.5%. Small numbers in absolute terms, but they point to a shift towards more recognisable and, on average, pricier wines.

## Prices: drinking less, paying a little more

The average price of DOC, DOCG and IGT wines in 75 cl bottles in large-scale retail rose by 2.1% to €5.69 a litre, while volumes in the same category fell by 2.6%. Metodo Classico averages €18.44 a litre. It is the same pattern as in recent years: fewer bottles, but slightly dearer ones, partly because of inflation and partly because shoppers choose more carefully.

One figure also contradicts the idea of wine becoming ever more “low cost”: retailers’ own labels account for 19.3% of volumes but lost 6.1%. Those who stay in the market, in short, tend to trade up rather than down.

## Young drinkers: not gone, different

On the subject of the young, you can read almost anything. The only figures I found with a clear source come from studies by the UIV-Vinitaly Observatory.

According to the 2026 edition, Italy has just under 30 million wine drinkers, around 55% of the population, a figure broadly stable over the past five years. What is changing is the way people drink: occasional drinkers have risen from 44% to 61% of the total, and their share of volumes from 15% to 40%. Daily wine at lunch and dinner, the grandparents’ kind, is becoming a minority habit.

Among 18 to 24-year-olds, 47% drink wine, below the average. And price matters to everyone, though not in the way you might expect: 71% of Gen Z name it as important, against 82% of Boomers. The youngest, in other words, are the least obsessed with the price tag.

The previous year’s study, based on IWSR data, added a detail that overturns plenty of headlines: under-44s make up about 35% of wine consumers, in line with their share of the population, and 14% of them said they were drinking more than before, against 7% of over-44s. The share drinking less was identical in both groups, at 27%. The young have not abandoned wine: they have moved it from the dinner table to the aperitivo, the restaurant, the evening out with friends.

## What we don’t know, and what I think

Missing from this snapshot are out-of-home sales measured as precisely as supermarket ones, and data from wine shops and cellar doors. They are an important part of the market, especially for more expensive wines, and the estimates in circulation differ too widely to be taken at face value. Anyone reading a figure for “wine drunk in restaurants” would do well to ask where it comes from.

My view is that the fall in consumption is real, but that the word “crisis” describes poorly what is happening. Italians drink less wine every day and more wine on occasions, choose sparkling and whites over heavy reds, and are willing to spend a little more on a bottle they can recognise. For those who make wine, it is a serious problem of volumes. For those who drink it, it might even be good news.
