The spritz is the drink that got half the world drinking Prosecco, and also the one in which Prosecco is treated worst. It is poured without a glance at the label, drowned in ice, forgotten. And yet, of its three ingredients, wine is the one there is most of. The short answer to “which Prosecco for a spritz” is this: a young Prosecco DOC Brut or Extra Dry, well chilled, from a freshly opened bottle. The choice between Brut and Extra Dry depends on how sweet you like the result. The long answer is more interesting.

## The recipe, the official one

Let us start with the numbers. The [International Bartenders Association](https://iba-world.com/iba-cocktail/spritz/), which codifies the recipes of classic cocktails, specifies for the Spritz 90 ml of Prosecco, 60 ml of Aperol and a splash of soda, in a wine glass filled with ice, stirred gently, with a slice of orange. It is the proportion many sum up as three parts Prosecco, two parts bitter and one part soda.

Three things stand out. Prosecco is the main ingredient, more than half the glass. The bitter, [Aperol](https://www.aperol.com/it-it/) in the IBA recipe, born in 1919, brings sugar, bitterness and colour. And soda is there to lengthen the drink and add fizz, not to correct its flavour. In this balance the wine decides freshness and bubbles; the bitter decides sweetness.

## Brut or Extra Dry?

And here is the question everyone asks in front of the shelf. The words on the label indicate the sparkling wine’s residual sugar, according to European rules: Brut has less than 12 grams per litre, Extra Dry between 12 and 17. Yes, Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut, despite the name; we discussed this [when explaining Prosecco and its labels](/en/articles/2026.10.16/prosecco-doc-docg-cartizze-rive-explained/).

In a spritz, however, the difference counts for less than you might think, because the wine’s sugar adds to the far more abundant sugar of the bitter. The practical rule:

- **Brut** if you like a drier, more bitter spritz, a serious aperitif. The wine cuts the sweetness of the bitter and leaves the mouth cleaner.
- **Extra Dry** if you prefer a rounder, softer spritz. It is also the historic style of the Prosecco hills, the one in which Glera, an aromatic grape, best shows its scents of apple and pear.
- **Dry**, sweeter still, risks making the cocktail cloying: better to keep it for other uses.

One note for those seeking the driest possible version: Brut Nature, below 3 grams, also exists in Prosecco DOC, but in a spritz it tends to taste thin next to the bitter.

## DOC, DOCG, or the good Prosecco?

The Prosecco DOC rules require the sparkling version to be made in pressurised tanks, with at least 85% Glera, and Prosecco is almost always made sparkling with the Martinotti method, which preserves the grape’s fresh fruit. It is the ideal style for a cocktail: immediate aromas, lively bubbles, no yeasty notes to clash with the bitter orange.

Does it make sense to use a Conegliano Valdobbiadene DOCG or an Asolo DOCG? You can, but it is a bit like cooking a ragù with a wine built for ageing: the bitter covers most of the nuances that set them apart. Better a good DOC, well chosen, than a wasted DOCG. What really matters is freshness: a Prosecco from the latest vintage and a freshly opened bottle. A sparkling wine uncorked the day before has lost its bubbles, and in a spritz you notice straight away.

## Will another sparkling wine do?

Yes, with a few caveats. A dry tank-method sparkling wine, from aromatic or neutral grapes, works in much the same way. A traditional-method wine such as a Franciacorta or a Trento DOC holds up very well, but its notes of bread crust and finer bubbles are lost, and the cost cannot be justified. Champagne in a spritz is a waste that no serious bartender would recommend.

Then there is the older Venetian tradition, the one where it all began: the spritz made with still white wine and sparkling water alone, born, according to the most common account, from the habit of Austrian soldiers of “spraying” water into the local wines. With a fresh, light white it is an aperitif that needs no bitter to be good, and it is worth rediscovering.

## Three mistakes to avoid

The first is pouring the bitter first and then the Prosecco violently on top: the bubbles disappear. Better plenty of ice, then the Prosecco, then the bitter, the soda, and a single gentle stir. The second is too little ice, which melts quickly and waters everything down; the third is a lukewarm bottle, which forces you to use more ice.

Our view is that the spritz deserves a chosen wine, not just any wine. You do not need the most expensive Prosecco, you need the freshest, with the sweetness that suits your taste. It is curious that a cocktail born to lighten wine has become the first reason millions of people buy a bottle of Prosecco. You might as well choose it with the same care you give to the orange.
