There is a country at the heart of Europe that has been making wine for two thousand years, farms almost 15,000 hectares of vines on slopes where anyone else would reach for climbing gear, and sends roughly one bottle in a hundred abroad. This is no tourist-brochure paradox: it is Switzerland. A wine lover in London, New York or Milan can spend a lifetime of serious drinking without ever meeting a Dézaley or a Petite Arvine on a wine list. Not out of snobbery, but because those bottles almost never leave home.

## A wine that stays at home

The numbers tell the rest of the story. In 2023 Switzerland produced just over 100 million litres; in 2024, after a difficult vintage, the trade press recorded a fall of about a quarter. Exports, depending on the source and the year, hover around 1% of production, and in any case below 2%.

The reason is not that the world dislikes Swiss wine. It is that there is none to spare. In 2022 the Swiss drank around 236 million litres of wine, more than twice what the country produces, and domestic wine accounted for 37% of consumption, down from 44% some thirty years earlier. The rest comes mainly from Italy (24%), then France (14%) and Spain (11%). Put simply, for a grower in Vaud the easiest market is the restaurant in the next village, not an importer in Tokyo. Add production costs among the highest in Europe, vines often worked by hand on vertiginous terraces and a strong franc, and it is easy to see why promotion abroad focuses only on top-end labels.

For an Italian reader the nearest comparison is perhaps Valtellina or the Valle d'Aosta: mountain viticulture, heroic in the literal sense, with tiny volumes largely drunk locally. Except that here the scale is national.

## Chasselas, the grape that refuses to show off

If Switzerland has a signature grape, it is Chasselas. With around 4,000 hectares it is the country's leading white variety, just behind Pinot Noir, the most planted grape overall, and in the canton of Vaud it covers two thirds of the vineyards. A DNA study published in 2009 by José Vouillamoz and colleagues, comparing it with more than 500 varieties, places its origin on the shores of Lac Léman, Lake Geneva, rather than in Egypt or Burgundy as old legends would have it.

Outside Switzerland, Chasselas is often a table grape (France even has an appellation for the one from Moissac) or, in Germany, the Gutedel of Baden. That is why the world underrates it: on its own it is a neutral, barely aromatic variety of moderate strength, around 11.5–12%. Yet that very neutrality is its gift. Like certain whites Italians know well, from Verdicchio to carefully made Trebbiano, Chasselas acts as a lens on place: soil, exposure and vine age come through more than the grape itself. Small wonder that Vaud producers put the name of the village or vineyard on the label, Burgundy-style, rather than the variety.

And it ages better than people think. The finest versions from the great Lavaux sites can last twenty years and more, changing register as they go.

## Lavaux, a landscape worthy of world heritage

Between Lausanne and Montreux, the terraces of Lavaux tumble down towards the lake across some 800 hectares. There are more than 10,000 of them, held up by dry-stone walls that, laid end to end, would stretch beyond 400 kilometres. Benedictine and Cistercian monasteries began them in the Middle Ages, and in 2007 UNESCO inscribed them as World Heritage, a living cultural landscape still under vine.

Here lie Lavaux's two grands crus, Dézaley and Calamin, with gradients that in Dézaley reach 100%. Chasselas accounts for about 60% of plantings. The comparison that springs to an Italian mind is the Cinque Terre or the Amalfi Coast: places where the vineyard is, first and foremost, a feat of engineering. The difference is that Lavaux has a wealthy home market to justify the cost, something those places often lack.

## Valais, where Italy is just over the mountain

If Vaud is the kingdom of Chasselas, Valais is the laboratory. It is the largest wine canton, with about 32% of Swiss vineyards, more than 4,600 hectares along the Rhône, and it grows 55 varieties. Here Chasselas changes its name to Fendant, a word that alludes to berries that “split” rather than burst when squeezed between the fingers. The name was born in Vaud in the 18th century; Valais adopted it and has hung on to it ever since.

But the reason foreign sommeliers come back from Valais misty-eyed is the native grapes. Petite Arvine, on 259 hectares, gives citrusy, floral whites with an unmistakable saline finish. Italians already know it without realising: it is also grown in the Valle d'Aosta, just over the Great St Bernard Pass. Among the reds are Cornalin (163 hectares) and Humagne Rouge (143), niche varieties found almost nowhere else. And above Visp, at Visperterminen, vines grow at around 1,100 metres, among the highest in Europe.

Valais is also proof that Switzerland is not just about light whites: Pinot Noir, the country's leading variety with around 30% of the vineyard area, makes serious reds here and in Graubünden.

## Why it is worth seeking out

Swiss wine is expensive, scarce and rarely talked about. Three flaws, by the logic of the global market. I see it differently: in a world where the same Sauvignon Blanc greets you in every airport, a country that drinks nearly everything it makes and grows grapes found only in its own valleys is a rarity to cherish, not a problem to fix. If you find yourself on Lake Geneva or in Valais, order by the glass whatever the innkeeper suggests. It is the most honest way to drink a wine that, by choice or by fate, has no need to travel.
