Some families have a history; others have an archive. The Antinori belong to the second kind: their documented link with wine begins in 1385, when Giovanni di Piero Antinori joined the *Arte dei Vinattieri*, the Florentine guild of wine merchants. Since then, says the [house](https://www.antinori.it/en/family/), the business has passed through twenty-six generations without ever leaving the family. This is the second portrait in the "Italian Excellence" series, and perhaps the most paradoxical: the oldest house in the group is the one that broke the rules most of all.

## A palazzo, a guild, a city

The Antinori's first address is Florence, where the family settled in the first half of the thirteenth century. On 2 February 1506 Niccolò di Tommaso Antinori bought the Boni family's palazzo for four thousand florins; it has carried the family name ever since and is still its residence. For centuries wine was one of their trades, then it became the main one. The detail matters: the Antinori were merchants before they were growers, and that double nature (a feel for the market, patience with the land) explains many of their later choices.

## The table wine that changed Tuscany

The turning point everyone quotes has a precise date. In 1970 the Tignanello vineyard, in the heart of Chianti Classico, still produced a Chianti Classico Riserva, with 20% Canaiolo and 5% Trebbiano and Malvasia, as the rules of the time demanded. With the 1971 vintage Piero Antinori, together with the oenologist Giacomo Tachis, made what was then a heretical choice: the wine was released as a humble table wine, with a name of its own, Tignanello. From the 1975 vintage the white grapes disappeared completely; since 1982 the blend has been today's, mostly Sangiovese with a small share of Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc, aged in barriques.

The result, to use a term coined a few years later, was one of the first Super Tuscans: a wine that had to leave the appellation in order to be better. We told the wider story in [Bolgheri and the Super Tuscans](/en/articles/2026.11.15/bolgheri-super-tuscans-history/). Solaia followed in 1978, from the neighbouring vineyard, built instead around Cabernet Sauvignon. Two wines, two ideas: Sangiovese dressed in French clothes, and Cabernet speaking Tuscan.

Today's [Tignanello](https://www.antinori.it/vino/tignanello-en/) comes from a vineyard of about 77 hectares between 350 and 400 metres, facing south-west, on calcareous soils rich in *alberese* limestone and marl. The most revealing detail, though, is a list: according to the house, the wine was not made in 1972, 1973, 1974, 1976, 1984, 1992 or 2002. Skipping a vintage of a famous wine is expensive, and that list says more than any statement about quality.

## Why it is a benchmark

The Antinori own estates across much of Tuscany and in Umbria, from Bolgheri to Montalcino, from Montepulciano to the Maremma. But their importance is not measured in hectares. They showed, before others, that a historic house could innovate without selling off its name, and that international markets could be won with Sangiovese rather than against it. When Wine Enthusiast put Tignanello 2016 at the top of its 2020 list of cellar selections, it was certifying a reputation built over fifty years.

The criticism one can make is the mirror image: a group this large risks looking more like a portfolio of brands than a winery. The risk is real. The best answer so far has come from Chianti Classico, which the family chose as its home.

## What to taste first

The house's reds range from the lively ruby of young Chianti Classico to the deep, intense ruby of Tignanello. A sensible path:

- **Pèppoli**, Chianti Classico DOCG, to understand the house's Sangiovese at its most direct;
- **Villa Antinori Chianti Classico Riserva**, for a step up in structure and oak;
- **Tignanello**, IGT Toscana, for the history.

Prices vary widely: the first two sit among widely available Chianti Classico, while Tignanello is a collector's wine whose prices change considerably from vintage to vintage. For the appellation's rules, the reference is the [Consorzio Vino Chianti Classico](https://www.chianticlassico.com/).

## How to visit

The headquarters is the [Antinori nel Chianti Classico](https://www.antinori.it/en/tenuta/estates-antinori/antinori-nel-chianti-classico-estate/) winery at Bargino, in the municipality of San Casciano in Val di Pesa: a building designed by the architect Marco Casamonti and inaugurated in October 2012, largely hidden inside the hillside. Visits range from the Tinaia Tour, with three wines, to the Bottaia Tour with four, up to the CRU Tour with seven wines and lunch at the rooftop restaurant Rinuccio 1180. Prices and availability are shown on the website's booking pages.

It is worth going for the architecture alone. But the real point of the visit is another: to see how a family of Florentine merchants, after six hundred years, decided to put its headquarters underground, in the middle of the vineyards. A statement of intent more eloquent than any label.
