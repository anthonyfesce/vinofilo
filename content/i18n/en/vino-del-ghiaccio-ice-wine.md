There is a moment, on a January night in Niagara or a December night on the Mosel, when a thermometer decides the fate of a vintage. If it drops below the line and stays there long enough, pickers in head torches file into the vineyard to harvest bunches as hard as marbles. If it does not, grapes left hanging on the vine for months are lost. Ice wine, the Germans’ Eiswein, is perhaps the only wine in the world that depends on one precise weather event, and for that very reason it has become a kind of seismograph for global warming.

## The principle, explained in a glass of water

The idea is as simple as it is ruthless. Inside the berry are water, sugars, acids and aromas. When the temperature falls far enough, the water turns to ice crystals while the sugars and acids stay in solution. Press the grapes while they are still frozen and the ice stays behind in the press; what trickles out is a thin stream of extraordinarily dense juice, concentrated in every respect: sweetness, but acidity too. Hence the character of these wines, which at their best are intensely sweet and yet as taut as a bowstring.

The cost in yield is enormous. Only a few drops leave the press, fermenting a must so rich in sugar can take months, and it needs yeasts able to work in extreme conditions. The picking itself must be done within a few hours, usually at night or at dawn, because a grape that thaws loses everything and quickly spoils.

For Italian readers the obvious comparison is with *passito* wines. There, too, the aim is to take water out of the grape to concentrate what remains, but by the opposite route: *appassimento* dries the bunches in the air, in lofts known as *fruttai*, for weeks or months, as for Amarone or Vin Santo, both of which Vinofilo has covered. Ice does the same job in a single night, provided nature plays along. Different again is noble rot, botrytis, which concentrates the berry by perforating its skin: another path to sweetness, with an aromatic profile all of its own.

## Germany and Austria: the rules of the cold

The tradition is German. The first Eiswein of the modern era is believed to date from 1794, in Franconia, but the best-documented harvest is that of Dromersheim, in Rheinhessen, on 11 February 1830: growers had left their grapes on the vines as animal fodder and, after a bitter winter, tried pressing them. Throughout the 19th century it remained a rare exception. It took the arrival of the pneumatic press to make production truly practical, and German volumes began to grow from 1961.

Today German law places Eiswein on the Prädikatswein ladder, alongside Spätlese, Auslese and Trockenbeerenauslese. The grapes must be picked and pressed frozen at a temperature of -7 °C or lower, with a minimum must weight of between 110 and 128 degrees Oechsle depending on region and variety, in other words that of a Beerenauslese. Freezing the grapes after picking, in a cold store, is forbidden. In Austria the benchmark is the same Beerenauslese level, 25 degrees KMW, the scale used in the country, equivalent to roughly 127 Oechsle.

The grape of choice in Germany is Riesling, whose natural acidity stops the wine from turning into syrup.

## Canada, where winter keeps its word

If Germany invented Eiswein, Canada turned it into an industry. According to the most widely cited sources it now makes more than every other country combined, and around 90% of Canadian output comes from Ontario, with the Niagara Peninsula at its heart. Continental winters, reliably cold, guarantee what in Europe has become a lottery.

The story is a recent one. The first experiments date from the early 1970s in British Columbia’s Okanagan Valley; the first commercial production followed in 1984 in Ontario, from Vidal grapes. The breakthrough came in 1991, when a Canadian Vidal Icewine from the 1989 vintage won the Grand Prix d’Honneur at Vinexpo in Bordeaux. Since then the world has learnt to write “Icewine” as one word, as the term protected in Canada requires.

The rules of the VQA, the appellation system of Ontario and British Columbia, are stricter even than Germany’s: grapes frozen naturally on the vine, picked at -8 °C or below, with must of at least 35 degrees Brix. Artificial freezing is ruled out. Alongside Vidal, a cold-hardy hybrid that dominates production, the main varieties are Riesling and Cabernet Franc.

It is worth adding that elsewhere cryoextraction, the mechanical freezing of grapes after harvest, is permitted for making sweet wines. But in Germany, Austria, Canada and the United States those wines cannot be called ice wine. The distinction matters: the name promises a night of real frost, not an industrial freezer.

## When the cold fails to come

The trouble is that the night of frost comes less and less often. The 2019 vintage went down in German wine history as the first without any Eiswein at all: according to the Deutsches Weininstitut, the German Wine Institute, the -7 °C threshold was not reached in a single one of the country’s wine regions. Nor was it an isolated case. In 2013 just five producers managed to harvest frozen grapes; in 2017, seven. In many years Eiswein accounts for less than 0.1% of the German harvest.

And an hour of cold is not enough. Mosel growers explain that at least two consecutive nights of frost are needed, ideally a full day at -7 °C or below, for the berry to freeze right through. Meanwhile grapes left on the vine since October must survive rain, rot, birds and wild boar. Every mild winter turns that gamble into a straight loss, and it is no surprise that fewer and fewer estates are willing to take the risk.

Public data on what has happened since 2019 are patchy, and we would rather not hazard figures. The direction of travel, however, is plain to anyone who spends time in German cellars: Eiswein, once rare, risks becoming a memory. Canada, with its harsher winters, is holding up better for now.

## A rarity worth defending

It would be easy to dismiss ice wine as a tourist curiosity, sold in slender half-bottles in duty-free shops. That would be unfair. The best, above all from Riesling, are among the purest sweet wines there are: no hint of rot, no raisin, just concentrated fruit and cutting acidity. My view is that their very fragility is what makes them precious. A wine that exists only if winter does its job reminds us, better than any graph, that viticulture is a pact with the climate. And that when the climate reshuffles the cards, the most delicate wines are the first to disappear.
