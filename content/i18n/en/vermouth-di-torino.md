In the bars of London, New York or Tokyo, vermouth has always been a supporting actor: the bottle on the bottom shelf, opened to stretch a Manhattan and then forgotten in the fridge for months. In Turin, by contrast, it played the lead for two centuries, and the city has decided to remind the world of the fact with a set of production rules. Outsiders are always surprised by this: vermouth is not a liqueur, nor is it a spirit. It is wine, and there is a great deal of wine in it.

## Wine, first and foremost

European law is clear: a vermouth must contain at least 75% wine, must be flavoured with wormwood, that is, plants of the genus Artemisia, and is then fortified with alcohol and sweetened. The name says as much, if you know German: Wermut simply means wormwood. Technically it is an aromatised wine, a well-bred cousin of the herbal wine infusions that physicians and apothecaries had been preparing since antiquity.

The modern method, in short, works like this: herbs, roots and spices macerate for weeks in a solution of alcohol and water; the extract is blended into the wine, which comes out both scented and strengthened; then the sugar goes in, the blend rests, is filtered and bottled. The result depends on three variables, the botanical recipe, the sugar and the wine, and the cocktail world tends to gloss over the last of these.

## Turin, 1786, and a shop on the square

The date tradition gives is 1786. In a liquor shop on Piazza Castello, at the heart of the Savoy capital, Antonio Benedetto Carpano perfected a blend of Moscato and some thirty herbs and roots. The story goes that he sent a case to court and that King Vittorio Amedeo III approved. Like all founding myths, this one has had its edges smoothed by time, but the sources agree that modern vermouth was born in Turin at the end of the 18th century. We have already told the story of the aperitivo that grew out of it in our piece on aperitivo wines; here the interest lies elsewhere.

In the 19th century Turin became the industrial capital of vermouth. Names such as Cinzano, Cora, Cocchi and Martini & Rossi were founded or flourished, and bottles left for France, Spain, Latin America and the United States, carried by international exhibitions and emigration. This is the period in which vermouth stopped being a Piedmontese speciality and became a global ingredient, with everything that implies: imitations, industrial versions, and a name anyone could use.

## What the Geographical Indication says

A royal decree of 1933 had already laid down the first production rules, but the turning point came with the ministerial decree of 22 March 2017, which applied the European regulation on aromatised wines and defined Vermouth di Torino as a Geographical Indication. The main rules are these:

- it must be made in Piedmont, from one or more Italian wines;
- Artemisia, absinthium and/or pontica, is compulsory and must be grown or gathered in Piedmont;
- alcohol ranges from 16% to 22%;
- colour may come only from caramel, and runs from white to red;
- there are optional sweetness terms: Extra Dry below 30 grams per litre, Dry below 50, Dolce from 130 upwards;
- the Superiore version requires at least 17% alcohol, at least 50% Piedmontese wine and other herbs, besides the Artemisia, grown or gathered in Piedmont.

Alongside the recognition, a producers’ body was set up to protect the name, later becoming a consortium, which now oversees the whole supply chain.

## The base wine, the part nobody looks at

This is where things get interesting for wine lovers. For standard Vermouth di Torino the wine may come from anywhere in Italy, and it is often a neutral white, chosen precisely so as not to interfere with the recipe. It is a legitimate choice: for many producers the wine is a canvas, and the herbs paint the picture. The Superiore category, with its mandatory share of Piedmontese wine, heads the other way and turns the wine into a marker of identity. The rules also allow the wines used, and their origin, to be named on the label, provided they make up at least 20% of the finished product.

It is a technical detail, but it reveals a direction of travel: the top category pushes vermouth back towards its winemaking roots, towards a base wine with an origin and a voice of its own, as Carpano’s Moscato once was. The result, when it works, is a vermouth in which you can taste the wine beneath the herbs, and not just the sugar on top.

## From Milano-Torino to the Negroni

Vermouth owes its global fame to cocktails, and in particular to a thoroughly Italian family. The first member is the Milano-Torino, which marries the bitter from Milan with the vermouth from Turin. Lengthened with soda, it becomes the Americano. And according to the most widely told version, around 1919 in Florence, Count Camillo Negroni asked for the soda to be replaced with gin: the Negroni was born, equal parts gin, bitter and red vermouth. Its paternity is disputed, with a rival claim linked to a Negroni family of Corsican origin, and the date is best treated as tradition rather than established fact.

What is certain is the rest. The Negroni has become a global classic, on the list of almost every cocktail bar, and it has dragged the whole culture of the Italian aperitivo along with it. Vermouth, for decades the ingredient whose label nobody read, began to be chosen by name, sipped neat, given its own place on the menu. Bartenders discovered that a third of their most famous cocktail is wine, and that changing the vermouth changes a Negroni more than changing the gin does.

## A wine that deserves to be drunk on its own

My view is that vermouth is the Italian wine most drunk around the world by people who have no idea they are drinking wine. It is an amusing paradox, but also a missed opportunity. The Geographical Indication has done the most important work: it has tied a name to a place and to a plant. The next step is up to the drinker. A good Vermouth di Torino, served cold over ice with a twist of orange, needs neither gin nor bitter to be interesting. It only needs to be taken for what it is: a wine, with a history two hundred and forty years long.
