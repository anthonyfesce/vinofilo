Few things in wine are as immovable as a list drawn up in a hurry by a group of brokers in the spring of 1855. It was meant for a trade fair, to help foreign visitors find their way, and its authors can hardly have imagined they were carving tablets of stone. Yet since then it has changed in any meaningful way only once. To understand why Bordeaux still thinks in growths, in *crus*, you have to go back to those two weeks of work.

## A fair, an emperor and a price list

In 1855 Paris hosted the Exposition Universelle, France’s answer to London’s Great Exhibition four years earlier. Napoleon III wanted the wines of Bordeaux presented to the public in an orderly fashion, and the Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce handed the job to the people who actually knew the market: the *courtiers*, the brokers who acted as go-betweens for the châteaux and the négociants, the merchant houses that buy, age and sell the wine. The list is dated 18 April 1855.

The yardstick was not taste, nor soil, nor a blind tasting. It was, in practice, price: what each wine had fetched over the decades, an indirect but pragmatic way of measuring reputation. It is a detail worth keeping in mind, because it explains both the classification’s staying power and its limits. For an Italian reader the closest comparison is not an appellation but a price list: imagine someone fixing the hierarchy of the Langhe for ever on the basis of a single season’s auction results.

The red wines classified in the original list numbered 58, spread across five tiers, from *premiers* to *cinquièmes crus*. All came from the Médoc, on the left bank of the Gironde, with one exception. The right bank, Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, was left out entirely: at the time it counted for little in the market those brokers served.

## The top five, and the 1973 interloper

In 1855 there were four *premiers crus*: Lafite, Latour, Margaux and Haut-Brion. The last is the geographical outsider, since it lies not in the Médoc but in the Graves, on the edge of the city, in what is now Pessac-Léognan. Its presence says a great deal about how the yardstick worked: Haut-Brion was the name the English market had known for centuries. As early as 1663 Samuel Pepys noted in his diary that he had drunk a wine called “Ho Bryan”, with a taste he had never come across before.

The fifth arrived 118 years later. Mouton, placed at the head of the *deuxièmes crus* in 1855, had been in the hands of Nathaniel de Rothschild for barely two years, and the slight rankled for generations. The house motto put it with aristocratic pride: “Premier ne puis, second ne daigne, Mouton suis”, first I cannot be, second I do not deign to be, Mouton I am. After decades of lobbying by Baron Philippe de Rothschild, promotion came in 1973, and the motto was rewritten: “Premier je suis, second je fus, Mouton ne change”, first I am, second I was, Mouton does not change.

That vintage has a special label too. Since 1946 Mouton has commissioned an artist for each year’s label, and the 1973 was dedicated to Picasso, who had died in April. It remains the only promotion in the classification’s history. The other official adjustment dates from 1856, when Cantemerle was added to the fifth growths, apparently after an oversight. Every other change has come from within: estates divided, as with Pichon and Léoville, mergers, one disappearance. Today there are 61 reds: five firsts, fourteen seconds, fourteen thirds, ten fourths and eighteen fifths.

## Sauternes, the other half of the list

It is often forgotten that in 1855 the brokers also classified white wines, and that the whites in question were sweet. Sauternes and Barsac, where the mists of the Ciron encourage noble rot (we covered it in our piece on botrytis), received a list of their own, split into first and second growths. Above them all, with a title created for the occasion, stood Château d'Yquem: *premier cru supérieur*, the only wine in the entire classification to sit a rung higher than even the great reds.

In 1855 the sweet wines numbered 21; here too, later divisions of estates have increased the number of labels. It is a precious snapshot of an age when sweet wine was the ultimate luxury, far more than it is today. If the price yardstick still applied, the Sauternes list would probably look rather different, and not only towards the bottom.

## What an 1855 ranking is worth today

This is where things get interesting, because the classification is both a monument and an anachronism. It works like a brand: a fifth growth remains a *cru classé* even if its vineyard has since grown, shrunk or shifted, because what was classified was the estate, not a plot of land. It is the exact opposite of the Burgundian logic, and of the one Italy has chosen for Barolo with its *menzioni geografiche aggiuntive*, the officially mapped named vineyards, where the land is ranked rather than the producer.

The market, meanwhile, has long since rewritten the list in its own way. Some second and third growths have traded for years well above their station, to the point that English-speaking merchants coined a term for them, “super seconds”. Every so often analysts of the secondary market amuse themselves by redoing the 1855 exercise with current prices, and the result never matches the official list. The picture is incomplete, too: Pomerol has never had a classification, and in Saint-Émilion, which has its own, revised roughly every ten years, three of its top names, Cheval Blanc, Ausone and Angélus, walked out between 2021 and 2022.

There is a lesson here even for those who never buy Bordeaux. A hierarchy fixed once and for all protects those at the top and discourages those below, but it gives drinkers a reference point that no annual score can shift. Rankings that are regularly updated are fairer, but they become a battleground, as Saint-Émilion shows.

## A list to read, not to worship

My view is that the 1855 classification should be read as an extraordinary historical document, not as a buying guide. It tells us what the Bordeaux trade held precious in the mid-nineteenth century, and it tells us how much longer the power of a name can last than the reasons that created it. That after 170 years the only real correction came through the stubbornness of a baron, rather than any assessment of the vineyards, says it all. And that is precisely its strength: Bordeaux understood before anyone else that a hierarchy nobody touches ends up becoming a myth.
