Few Italian grapes have had such an odd career. For centuries Ribolla Gialla was the most celebrated wine in Friuli; then it all but vanished; then it came back in the hands of a few stubborn growers who turned it into a cult wine; and finally, in recent times, it has become a supermarket label with bubbles. Today the same shelf can hold three wines that bear the same name and resemble each other in no way at all. It is worth understanding how we got here.

## A local grape, not a Greek one

For a long time the story went that Ribolla had come from Greece, a relative of Robola from Kefalonia on the strength of the similar names. DNA analyses published in 2007 and 2008 settled the matter: no relationship. The most widely accepted hypothesis today is that Ribolla was born precisely where it is grown, between the Friulian Collio and Slovenia’s Goriška Brda.

The paperwork, after all, has placed it there for a very long time. A notarial contract from the late 13th century already concerns Ribolla vineyards in Friuli, and in the following century Boccaccio mentions it among the pleasures of gluttony. In 1402 Udine passed a law against the adulteration of Ribolla, which is the most convincing proof of its prestige: nobody bothers to fake a wine that is worth nothing. We have already covered its early history, including the Slovenian side, in our article on the great whites of the Collio.

Then came phylloxera, and when the vineyards were rebuilt many owners preferred Merlot and Sauvignon. The decline was a long one: according to the most widely cited accounts, by the 1990s Ribolla went into less than 1% of Friuli’s white DOC wines. A historic grape reduced to a curiosity.

## The first life: still, taut, from the hills

Still Ribolla is the starting point, and also the version most at risk of being forgotten. It is a light-bodied white with high acidity, floral and citrus aromas rather than tropical fruit, and a finish that, given a few years, can take on notes of dried fruit and nuts. It is not an aromatic grape and does not draw attention to itself: it asks for poor soils and well-exposed slopes, such as the marl of the Collio or the hills around Corno di Rosazzo, in the Colli Orientali.

The rules of the Collio DOC give an idea of the philosophy: at least 4,000 vines per hectare, maximum yields of 11 tonnes and the exclusion of flat land. These are numbers designed for a hillside wine, which has to gain its concentration in the vineyard rather than the cellar. The trouble is that this Ribolla is also the least showy of the three, the one that seems almost too simple at first sip. It only makes sense later, at the table, with fish from the Adriatic or a *frico*, the local cheese-and-potato cake.

## The second life: on the skins

Ribolla’s second life is the one that made it known to the world: maceration on the skins, as for a red wine, which gives it an amber colour, real tannin and a structure capable of long ageing. Its thick skins make it especially suited to the technique, which is why Oslavia and the hills between Gorizia and Slovenia have become its point of reference. It is a chapter we have told at length in our article on orange wines, to which we refer you for the names and dates. Here one thing will suffice: without that season, Ribolla would probably still be a footnote today.

## The third life: the bubbles

The third life is the most recent and, in terms of bottles, the largest. In recent years Ribolla has been made into sparkling wine in growing quantities, mostly by the Charmat method in pressurised tanks, which is quick and cheap and can bring a wine to market in a matter of weeks. The grape’s natural acidity lends itself to this perfectly, and the name, exotic yet pronounceable, struck many as Friuli’s answer to Prosecco. The Friuli DOC, the broadest regional denomination, has rules designed for the plains and for volume, with yields of up to 14 tonnes per hectare.

Success came at a price, quite literally. A university study reporting figures from a large local cooperative describes a grape market that went from over 100 euros per quintal in 2017 to about 33 in 2019: a fall of roughly two thirds in two harvests, a sign of plantings that had grown faster than demand. It is a single-source figure and should be taken as indicative, but it captures the dynamics of every gold rush rather well.

Anyone drinking a sparkling Ribolla should know this: it is not a wrong wine, it is often fresh and pleasant, but it tells the story of the grape by subtraction. The subtler notes of hillside Ribolla, the ones that develop with time, have no time to appear in a simple Charmat. There are also bottle-fermented versions, more ambitious, but they remain a minority.

## The border in the glass

In Slovenia the same grape is called Rebula, and according to several sources that is where most of it is grown, in the Brda and the Vipava valley. The border drawn in 1947 runs through the hills and sometimes through the holdings of the same families, as we described when writing about the Collio. But today the line that truly divides Ribolla is not the one between Italy and Slovenia. It is the one between hill and plain, between a grower’s wine and a mass-market product, between those who use it to tell the story of a place and those who use it to fill a category.

Seen this way, a grower in Oslavia and one in Brda have far more in common with each other than with whoever bottles sparkling Ribolla a hundred kilometres away. That is Ribolla’s paradox: the political border, which split it in two for decades, is now almost invisible, while a border of style has appeared, within the same country and sometimes within the same cellar.

## Three wines, one name

My view is that Ribolla does not need to choose a single life, but that its three lives cannot go on sharing the same name without explaining themselves. An easy-going sparkler does no harm to anyone, until it becomes the grape’s only image. The risk is one we have seen elsewhere: a variety with more than seven centuries of documented history reduced to a byword for cheap aperitivo fizz. Still hillside Ribolla is the most fragile commercially and the most precious culturally. If there is one label to look for next time, it is that one, ideally with a few years under its belt. That is where you understand why, six centuries ago, Udine took the trouble to protect it.
