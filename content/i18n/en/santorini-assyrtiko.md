First-time visitors to Santorini go looking for the vineyards and fail to find them. Instead of the tidy rows of Burgundy or the Langhe, Piedmont's Nebbiolo hills, the plateau above the caldera is scattered with low, dark rings, set on the ground like forgotten baskets. These are vines. Some, it is said, have roots older than the United States, and they grow in a place where, by any reasonable logic, grapes should not grow at all.

## An island made of ash

Santorini is what remains of a volcano. Around the 17th century BC a colossal eruption hollowed out the island, leaving the caldera that now draws the cruise ships and burying the town of Akrotiri under ash more than a thousand years before Vesuvius did the same to Pompeii. The soil it left behind, known locally as *aspa*, is a jumble of pumice, ash, basalt and sand, almost devoid of clay and poor in potassium. It holds little water and offers next to nothing.

Rain is scarce, a few hundred millimetres a year, falling mostly in winter. In summer the moisture comes chiefly from the sea, in night-time mists that the porous soil soaks up. The wind blows hard and without let-up. To an Italian grower used to the hills of Tuscany it is an almost hostile environment; to Assyrtiko, the white grape that rules here, it is home.

The result is minuscule yields: according to figures reported in the specialist press, the average hovers around 10 hectolitres per hectare, a fraction of what an ordinary vineyard produces. In 2019 the harvest came in at less than half the average.

## The basket against the wind

The best-known training system is the *kouloura*. The grower weaves the vine's canes into a ring that grows year after year until it forms a basket at ground level. The bunches ripen inside, sheltered from the wind that would otherwise tear them off and from the sun that would scorch them. There is also a lower variant, shaped like a small bush vine, similar to the *alberello* seen in Italy on Pantelleria or on Etna.

Then there is a habit that astonishes every outsider. When the visible part of the plant ages and loses vigour, after some 75-80 years, it is cut off at the base and a new shoot is allowed to spring from the same roots. The trunk is young; the root system is not. In some cases there is talk of roots centuries old, with estimates reaching 300 or 400 years, hard to prove but plausible.

## The vines phylloxera never touched

All this is possible because Santorini's vines are almost all ungrafted, on their own roots. Phylloxera, the insect that destroyed Europe's vineyards in the late 19th century and forced the grafting of nearly every vine onto American rootstock, never took hold here: it needs soils with clay to travel from one root to the next, and in loose pumice its tunnels collapse. This is one of the few places in Europe where you can still drink wine from European vines on their own roots, as people did before the catastrophe.

The rules of the protected designation of Santorini, created in 1971 and also covering the neighbouring islet of Thirasia, require at least 85% Assyrtiko for the dry whites, with Athiri and Aidani as possible partners. Assyrtiko gives wines of searing acidity, with a savouriness many describe as saline and a structure that holds for years in bottle. The comparison that springs to an Italian mind is with the whites of Etna: the same volcanic tension, the same sense of stone rather than fruit. The Nykteri style, whose name harks back to harvesting at night to avoid the heat, spends at least three months in wood and is broader and more structured.

## The Vinsanto that came before Vin Santo

For Italians there is an extra twist. Santorini's Vinsanto, written as one word, is a sweet wine from grapes dried in the sun for about two weeks, with at least 51% Assyrtiko and the rest mostly Athiri and Aidani. It ages for at least 24 months in barrel, though many labels state far longer, up to 20 years.

It should not be confused with Tuscan Vin Santo, made from Trebbiano and Malvasia dried indoors and aged in small casks called *caratelli*. And yet the two stories touch. From the 13th century, when the island passed to Venetian lords, and later under the Ottomans, who arrived in 1579, Santorini's sweet wines travelled all over the Mediterranean, prized because sugar and alcohol protected them on long voyages. The Russian Orthodox Church adopted them as its liturgical wine. And among the legends about the origin of the Tuscan name, one of the most often told links it to Santorini itself. A legend, admittedly, but a telling one about how much the island weighed in the wine trade.

## The enemy is no longer the insect

Santorini's problem today is not nature but success. In the early 1960s the island had more than 4,000 hectares of vineyard. Current estimates put it at around 1,100-1,200 hectares: according to reports in the specialist press, since the 1980s the island has lost half its vines to hotels, villas and tourist infrastructure. A few years ago, a plot of just over half a hectare could be worth €350,000, a sum no harvest could ever repay.

Add to that an ageing generation of growers, children who would rather work in tourism, and an ever drier climate shrinking yields that were already tiny. Planning rules, according to several observers, have not been enough to curb the loss of land.

## Why it matters beyond the island

Santorini is not just a curiosity for enthusiasts. It is a living archive: vines that show how grapes were grown before phylloxera, and a way of adapting to wind and drought that other regions of the world, Italy included, may have to study as the climate changes. Assyrtiko, meanwhile, has shown that it can keep its acidity even in hot climates, which is why it is now planted elsewhere in Greece too.

My view is that the price of Santorini's wines, so often debated, should be read in this light. You are not paying only for a taut, saline white, but for the survival of a landscape that tourism devours a little more each year. Every bottle is, in the end, a vote to keep the vines where they are. The volcano protected them for centuries; it is up to the visitors not to finish the job that phylloxera never managed.
