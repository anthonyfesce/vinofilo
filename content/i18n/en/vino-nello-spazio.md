For centuries we have asked wine to age in the dark, in the cool and, above all, in peace. Then someone decided to do precisely the opposite: put twelve bottles of one of the world’s most celebrated reds on a rocket and leave them for fourteen months circling the Earth at an altitude of 400 kilometres, at a speed that makes a house move by lorry look like a stroll. This was not a stunt dreamt up by bored collectors. It was a serious experiment, with curious results and a few questions still hanging in the air.

## Twelve bottles and three hundred and twenty canes

The story begins in November 2019, when twelve bottles of Pétrus 2000 set off for the International Space Station. Pétrus is the most mythologised name in Pomerol, the small appellation on Bordeaux’s Right Bank where Merlot rules: for readers who know Italy better than France, it carries the kind of fame and scarcity that certain historic labels from Barolo or Bolgheri enjoy there, but with auction prices from another galaxy.

The mission was organised by Space Cargo Unlimited, a young European company set up to study how microgravity changes organic matter. One detail matters: it was not the owners of Pétrus who chose to send their wine into orbit. According to Decanter, the company bought the bottles from a *courtier*, one of the brokers of the Bordeaux trade, and picked them for their structure and the clear dominance of Merlot. The name was revealed only once the mission was over.

The bottles travelled sealed in steel cylinders. Alongside them went 320 vine canes, 160 of Merlot and 160 of Cabernet Sauvignon, because the wine was, in the end, the most photogenic pretext: the stated aim of the programme, called Mission Wise, is to understand how plants respond to extreme stress, with an eye to farming that can withstand a changing climate. The return came in January 2021 aboard a SpaceX Dragon cargo capsule.

## The blind tasting in Bordeaux

In March 2021 the scene shifted to the ISVV, the University of Bordeaux’s institute of vine and wine science and one of Europe’s most authoritative centres of oenological research. Twelve tasters each received two samples of about 30 ml: the Pétrus that had come back from space and a bottle of the same vintage that had stayed in the cellar. Tasted blind, as it should be.

The two wines were not the same. Jane Anson, then Decanter’s Bordeaux correspondent, described the earthbound bottle as exactly what she expected: younger, more closed, its tannin still to the fore. The space bottle struck her as more evolved, by two or three years on her estimate, with more floral aromatics and softer tannins. She added, honestly, that she had tasted only one space bottle, so simple bottle variation could not be ruled out.

Professor Philippe Darriet, who heads the institute’s oenology research unit, summed up the panel’s view more cautiously: both great wines, with differences perceived in aroma, taste and colour, but read differently according to each taster’s sensitivity. The canes had a surprise in store too: according to the organisers, they grew faster than their twins left on the ground, despite less light and less water.

## Why orbit might age a wine

Here it pays to be clear about what we know and what we don’t. The underlying idea is fascinating: bottle ageing is a slow chain of chemical reactions, from tannins binding and softening to aromas transforming, and microgravity might alter their pace. But in orbit the variables are not just one. There are the vibrations of launch and re-entry, radiation, the swings in temperature and pressure during transfers, the months of travel between one laboratory and another. Separating the effect of reduced gravity from everything else is the real scientific problem.

At the time of the tasting, detailed chemical analyses were announced, along with the intention of publishing the results in an international scientific journal. I have found no verifiable trace of a definitive publication, and until shown otherwise the firmest evidence remains sensory: twelve expert palates, a tiny statistical sample, a difference perceived but not explained. Anyone who tells you that “space makes wine age faster” is running ahead of the data.

There is also a paradox every wine lover will spot. For decades we have been told not to move our bottles, to keep them away from light and vibration, and more than one home-cellar manual still insists on it. The space Pétrus, which took more knocks than any bottle ever laid down in a cellar, came out of the ordeal not ruined, but different. That perhaps says more about the resilience of a great wine than about the perils of space.

## At auction, with a meteorite corkscrew

In 2021 Christie’s offered one of the bottles back from orbit by private sale, with an estimate the press put at around a million dollars. The lot was conceived as a story to tell: a bespoke trunk, a decanter, glasses, a corkscrew fashioned from a meteorite and, above all, a bottle of Pétrus 2000 that had stayed on Earth, so the buyer could repeat at home the comparison made in Bordeaux. The proceeds were earmarked to fund Space Cargo Unlimited’s future missions.

Whether the bottle actually sold, at what price and to whom, I have not been able to confirm from reliable sources: reports at the time describe the offer, not the outcome. I flag this because it is easy to find figures online presented as certain. Anyone who enjoys auction stories will find in our piece on the most expensive bottles ever sold the context to see how much such sums depend on the narrative, as well as on the wine.

## A serious experiment with a cover-star label

My view is that the orbital Pétrus was, first and foremost, an extraordinary piece of storytelling, and there is nothing wrong with that. A cult wine drew the world’s attention to research that concerns the vine rather than the glass: how a plant reacts to stress, and what we can learn about growing it in ever more difficult conditions. As for the wine itself, the most honest lesson is that fourteen months in orbit changed it in a way that was perceptible but not yet explained. Until the chemistry has its say, the cellar beneath your house remains the best place to age a bottle. And it costs considerably less than the ticket.
