On 1 September 1730, on the island of Lanzarote, the earth split open and did not close again for six years. The eruptions that followed, until 1736, raised dozens of new volcanic cones along a fissure several kilometres long and buried villages and fields beneath metres of lapilli. Anyone looking at that black landscape would have written off farming on the island. The people of Lanzarote, instead, started digging. From that gesture, stubborn and brilliant in equal measure, came one of the most photographed and least understood vineyards in the world.

## Pits in the ash

The landscape is called La Geria, and first-time visitors struggle to believe it is a vineyard at all. Instead of rows there are thousands of hollows dug into the *picón*, the layer of lapilli and volcanic ash that blankets the old fertile soil. These are the *hoyos*: pits a few metres across and two or more deep, at the bottom of which the vine reaches the earth that survived beneath the lava. Around each one, a semicircular wall of lava stone, the *zoco*, shelters the plant from the trade winds that scour the island without respite.

The logic is subtler than it looks. Lanzarote gets very little rain, less than 200 millimetres a year according to the most widely cited sources, packed into a handful of days. The picón acts as a mineral mulch: at night it holds on to the moisture carried in on the Atlantic air, by day it stops what little water the soil holds from evaporating. The pit gathers that meagre resource around a single plant, and the wall does the rest. The result is viticulture at extraordinarily low density, entirely by hand, in which every vine is a small feat of engineering.

For readers who know Europe’s wine map, the nearest comparison is Santorini, where Assyrtiko is coiled into a basket to defend itself against the wind. Or Pantelleria, the Italian island between Sicily and Tunisia, where Zibibbo (the local name for Muscat of Alexandria) is trained as low bush vines in hollows scooped out of the ground, a practice UNESCO has recognised as intangible heritage. Three islands, three different seas, one conclusion: where wind and drought are in charge, the vine crouches and hides.

## Malvasía, an island’s own grape

On Lanzarote the queen grape is Malvasía Volcánica, which accounts for the great majority of the island’s vineyards. Genetic studies point to a natural cross between Malvasía Aromática and Marmajuelo, a white grape typical of the archipelago: a variety, in other words, born here. It makes dry or off-dry whites with a saline, floral profile and a freshness that is startling in so warm a climate. The Lanzarote denomination of origin dates from the mid-1990s and, by the most common estimates, counts around 1,800-2,000 hectares of vines; the precise figure varies with the source and the year.

The name Malvasía is, after all, an umbrella covering different grapes across the Mediterranean, from Greece to Sicily’s Malvasia delle Lipari. It reached the Canaries with the Portuguese at the end of the 15th century and is the grape with the longest history in the archipelago. It is also the one that made the islands famous in England, but more on that shortly.

## Listán, and the insect that never came

Widen the view to the other islands, above all Tenerife with its five denominations, and the most planted grapes are two: Listán Blanco and Listán Negro, found throughout the archipelago. The first is the same variety that in southern Spain is called Palomino and forms the basis of Sherry; here, far from flor and the casks of Jerez, it gives taut, savoury whites. The second is the signature red of the Canaries, capable of light, spicy wines, sometimes with an almost smoky note that many put down to volcanic soils and that it would be more honest to call cellar style. In Tenerife’s old vineyards the plants are often jumbled together in mixed plots, the *vidueño*, where no one ever thought of separating one variety from another.

Then there is a fact that makes these vineyards almost unique in Europe: phylloxera, the aphid that devastated the continent’s vineyards in the 19th century and whose story we have told on Vinofilo, never reached the Canaries. Isolation in the middle of the Atlantic acted as a barrier. The vines therefore grow on their own roots, without American rootstock, and some century-old plants are living archives of varieties lost elsewhere. A 2019 study catalogued dozens of local cultivars.

## Falstaff’s wine

Which brings us to England. In the 16th and 17th centuries Canary Malvasía, above all from Tenerife, was among the most sought-after sweet wines in London’s taverns, where it was known simply as *canary*, or *canary sack*. Shakespeare names it repeatedly: in “Henry IV” the hostess Mistress Quickly scolds a guest for having drunk too much canaries, “a marvellous searching wine, and it perfumes the blood”; in “Twelfth Night” Sir Toby suggests that what his friend lacks is precisely a cup of canary. In fairness to the philologists, Shakespeare never writes “malvasía”: the identification with the grape is the historians’ deduction, however sound, because Malvasía was the wine the islands shipped to England. And Falstaff’s sack, when it is called *sherris sack*, comes from Jerez, not the Canaries.

The trade ended in spectacular fashion. In 1665 Charles II granted a group of London merchants a monopoly on Canary wine, the Canary Company, which forced rock-bottom prices on growers. In July 1666, at Garachico, the port on Tenerife, hundreds of growers broke into the cellars and staved in the casks destined for the company: according to contemporary accounts, Malvasía ran through the streets in rivers. The company was dissolved in 1667, but the English market never returned to what it had been, and in the decades that followed British taste drifted towards Madeira and Port. The “derrame del vino”, the spilling of the wine, is still remembered in Garachico as an act of peasant pride.

## A future written in ash

Today the Canaries are one of the liveliest frontiers in Spanish wine. A generation of producers has stopped chasing international taste and set to work on old vineyards, local grapes and light extractions. The wines that result are often pale, nervy and low in alcohol for a subtropical latitude, and they have found a place on the most inquisitive wine lists in Europe and America.

My view is that the real value of the Canaries lies not in the fashion for “volcanic”, a label now spent far too freely, but in something rarer: a heritage of ungrafted vines and native varieties that the rest of Europe lost in the 19th century. The pits of La Geria are not a postcard curiosity; they are proof that a vineyard can be born from disaster. A glass of Lanzarote Malvasía tastes of three centuries of obstinacy. Falstaff, who knew more about wine than he let on, would have approved.
