Salmon is the most misunderstood fish on the Italian table. We treat it like any other fish, and so we pour the usual white beside it, the light, slightly timid one that goes with sea bream. Then we wonder why the glass vanishes. Salmon is not sea bream: it is fatty, rich, sometimes smoked, sometimes almost meaty. At the table it behaves less like a fish than like something halfway between fish and pork. And the wine has to take that into account.

The good news is that the solutions are few and clear. The bad news is that almost all of them depend on how the salmon reaches the plate. Smoked, raw, baked or grilled are four different animals, and it pays to treat them as such.

## Party canapés

Smoked salmon is December’s permanent guest: on buttered bread, on blini, with a dab of sour cream or a sprinkling of dill. Here there are three problems to solve all at once. The fat, which coats the mouth. The salt, which dries it. And the smoke, which covers anything delicate.

The most sensible answer is also the most festive: a traditional-method sparkling wine. The acidity cuts the fat, the carbon dioxide cleans the palate with every sip, and the bread-crust and yeast aromas that long ageing on the lees gives the wine converse with the butter and the bread of the canapé. A low dosage, brut or extra brut, is preferable: the salt in the fish makes any residual sugar more obvious, and an overly soft sparkler becomes cloying by the third crostino.

Those who want to go further can choose a traditional-method wine with a good share of Pinot Nero, or even a rosé. The extra structure stands up better to the smoke, and the colour in the glass next to the pink of the fish looks the part. It is not a rule, it is a pleasure.

The still alternative is a dry Riesling. Its taut acidity and citrus profile, sometimes with a mineral hint that in more evolved wines veers towards almost petrol-like notes, seem made for smoked salmon. Here it is best to stay dry: a Riesling with residual sugar can work with a very salty version, but on a buttered canapé it risks weighing things down.

To avoid: whites with plenty of new oak. Vanilla plus smoke is a sum, not a pairing.

## Raw, tartare and carpaccio

Raw salmon is another matter. Without smoke and without cooking the fat remains, but the texture is melting and the flavour subtler. It often arrives dressed: lemon, oil, soy sauce, ginger, avocado, sesame. The wine has to respect the delicacy of the fish and stand up to the dressing, which is sometimes the noisiest part of the dish.

Here dry Riesling is perhaps the most precise choice of all, especially if there is a citrus element. With a Japanese-style tartare, dressed with soy and ginger, a Riesling with a touch of sweetness also works well, balancing the salt of the sauce. Traditional-method sparkling remains an excellent card, in a leaner, less vinous style dominated by Chardonnay.

And then there are rosés. A dry, pale rosé with good acidity and low alcohol accompanies raw fish with a naturalness that surprises those who associate it only with summer. It has the fruit to stand beside the fat and the freshness not to smother it. Better to steer clear of those too heavy in colour and sugar: on raw fish they turn into jam.

## Baked

When salmon cooks in the oven it changes character. The flesh firms up, the fats become rounder, and the dish brings along whatever is in the tray: potatoes, herbs, sometimes a butter sauce or a pistachio crust. Here the wine can raise its voice.

With simple baked salmon, with herbs and lemon, a medium-bodied white with good acidity works well, and a dry Riesling with a few years behind it becomes interesting: its aromatic evolution stands up to a cooking method that raw fish did not demand. With richer preparations, butter or cream sauces, you need more structure, and a traditional-method sparkler with long ageing comes back into its own, because the carbon dioxide lightens the sauce.

But it is with the oven that the door to red opens. And here a small scientific detour is needed.

## The question of red

The old saying goes: fish with white. It has a real basis, though not the one people think. In 2009 a group of Japanese researchers published a study in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, carried out on a few dozen red and white wines tasted with scallops. The conclusion was that the unpleasant, almost metallic, fishy aftertaste that sometimes appears when pairing wine and seafood depends above all on the iron contained in the wine. More iron, more aftertaste. And reds, on average, contain more of it.

Colour, then, is not the point. The point is to choose a red that does not make things worse: fine tannins, lively acidity, red fruit and no intrusive oak. That is the identikit of Pinot Nero. With baked salmon, and even more so with grilled, a fresh, not overly extracted Pinot Nero is one of the most successful pairings salmon knows. Its light texture does not quarrel with the fish, and the red fruit leans on the sweetness of the cooked flesh. It should be served a little cooler than usual, at around 14 degrees, so that acidity and fruit stay in the foreground.

What is best avoided, on the other hand, are tannic, muscular reds. With fish, tannins turn rough and can accentuate precisely those metallic notes the study described.

## Grilled

The grill is the freest territory. The crust, the charred notes, the flesh that stays tender inside: grilled salmon has a personality halfway between fish and white meat. Pinot Nero is its natural companion, and becomes even more convincing if there are mushrooms, lentils or a red-wine sauce on the plate.

Rosé plays its best game here. One a little more structured than the one chosen for raw fish, with more decisive fruit, handles both the smoke of the embers and the fat of the fish, and has no tannin problem. It is the choice for a long summer table, but it holds its own in winter too.

Those who stay loyal to white can still bet on Riesling, as long as it has substance, or on a full-bodied traditional-method sparkler, perhaps a blanc de noirs. Bubbles with the grill seem a gamble and yet they work: the carbon dioxide cleans the char from the palate as a beer would, with more elegance.

## An opinion, to finish

If we had to keep a single bottle in the cellar for all the year’s salmon, it would be a brut traditional-method sparkler, perhaps with some Pinot Nero in it. It covers canapés, raw fish, the oven and even the grill, and almost never gets it wrong. But the real advice is something else: stop thinking of salmon as a light fish. It is a rich dish, and it deserves wines with nerve and a little courage. The timid white of fish dinners, the one that bothers nobody, simply vanishes beside salmon. And a wine that vanishes is never a pairing, only a missed opportunity.
