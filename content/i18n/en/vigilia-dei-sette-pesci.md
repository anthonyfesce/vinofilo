Ask a Neapolitan what they eat on the evening of 24 December and you will get a list: *capitone* (the big female eel), baccalà, a fry-up, spaghetti alle vongole. Ask whether they celebrate the “feast of the seven fishes” and you will get the look normally reserved for a tourist ordering a cappuccino after carbonara. And yet in Philadelphia, in Brooklyn, across New Jersey, the Feast of the Seven Fishes is one of America’s best-loved and most photographed Christmas traditions, filling restaurant menus, food magazines and family arguments every December. It is an Italian ritual with an American name. Which is precisely why it deserves telling from the Italian side of the ocean.

## A name born in Philadelphia

The substance is old and Catholic. For centuries the Church treated Christmas Eve as a day of fasting and abstinence from meat, with the fast broken in the evening. Hence the meatless *cenone di magro*, the “lean” Christmas Eve dinner, which exists in some form all over Italy but which in the South, with the sea close by and fish affordable, took the shape of a parade of seafood dishes. The Church relaxed its rules on abstinence in the 1960s, but by then the table had long since found its own way.

That way, quite literally, was emigration. Between 1880 and 1924 some four million people left southern Italy for the United States, taking their Christmas Eve dinners with them, each one different according to the village it came from. In America, where Italians were treated as a single undifferentiated bloc, those regional traditions blended until they became one thing, something that had never existed as such in Italy.

The name came much later. A piece of research by Mental Floss traces the first appearance in print of the phrase “Feast of the Seven Fishes” to a 1983 advertisement for a Philadelphia restaurant. The anthropologist Michael Di Giovine of West Chester University, who has studied the phenomenon, confirms that the expression is not heard in Italy: there the evening is simply *la Vigilia*, the Eve, or the *cenone*, the big dinner. As for the number seven, nobody knows where it comes from for certain. The most quoted explanations are religious (the seven sacraments, the day of rest in Genesis), though some suspect, not without mischief, the hand of a restaurateur. Plenty of families serve nine, twelve or thirteen dishes anyway, and nobody is counting.

## What is on the table

The Italian-American menu is a collage of the South. Baccalà, salt cod, is all but compulsory: fried in batter, dressed as a salad or stewed. Fried calamari with lemon and marinara sauce is the course that wins over even the children. Then there are clams, baked with breadcrumbs and oregano or tossed through spaghetti; mussels; smelts, small fish fried whole that recall the Italian *latterini*; and scungilli, an Italian-American word drawn from Neapolitan for sea snails, served as a salad with celery and lemon. The more lavish versions bring in lobster fra diavolo, shrimp, branzino, sole and, in some households, eel, the last memory of the Neapolitan capitone.

Seen from Italy, the most striking difference is one of tone. Christmas Eve at home is regional and stubborn: in Vicenza it is baccalà alla vicentina, in Naples it is capitone, and woe betide anyone who changes it. The American version is more inclusive and noisier, a celebration of identity more than of liturgy, in which the fish also says where you come from. We have already written about Italy’s own Christmas Eve fish dinner and its pairings region by region: what matters here is what changes when the same spirit crosses the Atlantic.

## The wine problem: many dishes, one evening

From the glass’s point of view, the Feast of the Seven Fishes is a gentle minefield. Fried food, salt, garlic, tomato, lemon, sometimes chilli, all on the same night and often on the same plate. The neutral, anonymous white chosen “because there’s fish” vanishes by the second calamaro. What you need is acidity, salinity and a minimum of character.

The good news for anyone laying the table in the United States is that Italy offers exactly that, and has been exporting it for a long time. For the opening, the raw dishes and above all the fried ones, the rightest answer is a dry sparkling wine. A Prosecco Brut is perfectly good as a welcome; an Italian traditional-method sparkler, a Franciacorta or a Trento DOC (the sparkling wines of Lombardy and Trentino, made like Champagne with a second fermentation in bottle), has broader shoulders and copes better with batter and oil.

Clams, mussels and scungilli call for a white that tastes of the sea without being slight. Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi, from the Marche, has the faintly bitter-almond finish that shellfish love. Vermentino, Sardinian or Ligurian, brings Mediterranean herbs and salt. And if you want to stay loyal to the South where it all began, Fiano di Avellino and Greco di Tufo, the two great whites of Campania, are almost a family choice: the first rounder, the second more nervy.

## Baccalà, tomato and the courage of red

Baccalà is where the white-wine rule starts to creak. Stewed with tomato, olives and capers, as many Italian-American households make it, it asks for something with a little colour. Here a serious rosé is the secret weapon: Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo, made from Montepulciano grapes, has the structure of a light red and the freshness of a white; a rosé from Salento made from Negroamaro does the same job with a warmer accent.

For lobster fra diavolo and spaghetti in red sauce, do not be afraid of a young, low-tannin red served cool: a Sicilian Frappato, a Lacryma Christi del Vesuvio, an Etna Rosso in its lighter guise. High tannin is the only real enemy, because with fish it leaves a metallic taste in the mouth; colour, on the other hand, never hurt anyone.

One practical tip that holds on both sides of the ocean: three bottles are enough. A sparkler to start and for the fried dishes, a white with structure for the shellfish and pasta, a rosé or light red as the wild card. The rest is temperature: whites and sparklers in a bucket of water and ice, not left to warm up next to the oven.

## A tradition Italy should envy

It would be easy to dismiss the Feast of the Seven Fishes as an invention, a picture-postcard Italy built by emigrants and restaurateurs. It would also be unfair. Traditions are not created by decree, and this one was born the way they all are: from people who wanted to remember who they were, in a country that did not understand them. My view is that Italian Americans have given us a small gift. They took our regional Christmas Eve dinners, fused them into a single feast and gave it a name anyone can remember. The least we can do is fill those glasses with Italian wine worthy of the occasion and perhaps, just this once, count the fish ourselves.
