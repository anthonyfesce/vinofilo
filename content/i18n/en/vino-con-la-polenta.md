Asking which wine goes with polenta is like asking what music to play in an empty room: it depends on who walks in. Polenta on its own is maize cooked in salted water, sweet, neutral, almost mute. The wine is chosen for the dish that sits on top, and a change of topping means a change of bottle. Anyone looking for “the polenta wine” has already asked the wrong question.

The good news is that geography is on our side. Polenta is food of the mountains and the foothills, and those mountains have produced, over the centuries, the wines their kitchens needed: acidic, nervy, often slender. All you have to do is stay in the same valley.

## Taragna: the polenta with character

Taragna is not the yellow polenta you might picture. In Valtellina and the Bergamo valleys it is made with buckwheat flour in clear majority and maize only as a supporting player. The name, so the story goes, recalls the *tarel*, the stick used to stir the cauldron. Then come butter and cheese: Bitto, Casera, Formai de Mut or Taleggio, depending on the valley and the family, since almost every household guards a secret variation.

Buckwheat tastes rustic, earthy, faintly bitter, and the melted cheese brings fat and salt. You need a red with acidity and tannin, not muscle. A few kilometres from where the dish was born stands Valtellina’s Nebbiolo, known locally as Chiavennasca: a young Rosso di Valtellina, or a Valtellina Superiore without too many years on it, has tannin that cuts through the richness and acidity that cleans the palate with every mouthful. It is the valley’s own pairing, and hard to improve on.

## Concia: the cheese is in charge

Polenta concia, at home in the kitchens of Valle d’Aosta and Piedmont, is dressed with butter and stringy, melting cheeses, fontina first among them in the Aosta version. It is the richest of all polentas. Here a round, soft wine produces the Sunday-lunch effect: an hour after coffee it already feels like evening.

What you need is freshness and a little grip. A lively Barbera, with high acidity and low tannin, cuts the fat without a quarrel. A young Nebbiolo does the same with more severity. And if you want a white, one from altitude with the right backbone, such as Valle d’Aosta’s Petite Arvine, stands up to the cheese better than you would expect. Avoid only the broad, fruit-sweet whites: against melted butter they become one caress too many.

## Baccalà alla vicentina: the dish that deceives

Here the Veneto has a surprise in store. The *bacalà* alla vicentina of the Sandrigo tradition and its Confraternita is not salt cod but stockfish, cod dried in the open air and soaked for days. It cooks slowly with onion, oil, salted anchovies, milk and a little grated cheese, and is served with polenta. The dish, say its keepers, improves after a night’s rest.

It is no dish for a powerful red: fatty and savoury, yes, but delicate in substance, and firm tannin would flatten it. A white from those hills works better, such as Breganze based on Vespaiolo, with its taut acidity, or a Gambellara or Soave from Garganega grapes, full-bodied and carrying the note of almond this fish likes. A dry sparkling wine, say a Durello from the Lessini hills, is a regional choice and less obvious than it seems: the acidity does the job that elsewhere a squeeze of lemon would do.

## Mushrooms, stew, sausage: the red gets serious

With mushrooms, in autumn, polenta takes on the smell of the forest floor, and that is where a Nebbiolo with some years in bottle, or a mountain Pinot Nero, finds its place: earth and dry leaves call out to earth and dry leaves. A wine that is too young and powerful covers the dish instead of accompanying it.

Stew and sausage are another story. They mean meat, fat and gravy, with the polenta acting as a sponge. Teroldego from the Piana Rotaliana in Trentino, with its dark colour, dark fruit and fine acidity, seems made for it. The South Tyrolean Lagrein, softer and spicier, is the alternative. For sausage, a fresh Marzemino is the choice of those who want to drink without thinking too hard, which is a respectable ambition.

## One rule only

If I had to boil it all down to a single rule, it would be this: acidity. Polenta and cheese, polenta and gravy, polenta and dried fish: there is always fat on top, and the right wine is the one that lightens it. The rest is detail, or local pride, which here is a more reliable guide than it sounds.

Let me add a preference, and call it an opinion. Slightly angular, unaccommodating mountain wines are more honest with polenta than the full, polished reds sold as “for meat”. Polenta does not need to be softened: it needs a companion that can hold its own without crushing it. And if that companion comes from the same valley, it has usually already understood everything else.
