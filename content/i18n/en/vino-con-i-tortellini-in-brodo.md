Capon broth is the dish that most resents being paired. It has no tannin, no fat floating on top, not even any real aggression: it is hot, savoury, almost obvious. Yet it is the moment when many Christmas tables make their biggest mistake, uncorking the important red that has been waiting for months. The result is an uneven duel, and the broth always loses.

## The rule of liquid with liquid

The principle is simple and works better than many a treatise. A dish whose star is a hot, delicate liquid calls for a wine on the same wavelength: fluid, fresh, light on its feet. The broth already has its structure, and it lives entirely in flavour: the meat, the bone, the time. The filling adds a fatty, spicy note (in tortellini, meat, ham, nutmeg, cheese), but in tiny amounts, wrapped in a thin sheet of pasta.

So you need three things, in this order: acidity, to cleanse the palate of the filling's fat and keep the savouriness alive; bubbles, or at least liveliness, which refresh with every sip without weighing anything down; and almost no tannin, because against a dish like this tannin has nothing to fight and ends up being the only thing you taste. Temperature helps: a wine served cool next to a steaming bowl creates a contrast that wakes up both.

It holds for all three great cousins of filled pasta in broth. The tortellini of Bologna and Modena, the cappelletti of Romagna, Reggio and thereabouts, the anolini of Parma and Piacenza have different fillings and broths (in anolini, for instance, a deeper, sweeter note of braised meat dominates), but the logic of the pairing stays the same. The nuances change, not the method.

## Red wine in the broth: a real custom, to be handled with care

Is there really a habit of putting red wine in the bowl? I looked for evidence and found only one source, and a partial one. A collection of Emilian recipes reports that in the Bassa, the flat lowland part of Emilia, it is the custom to serve as a starter, in a small bowl, a few cappelletti cooked with a little broth and a little Lambrusco. One testimony, not a survey. Other sources I consulted on capon broth do not mention wine, and I found no solid documentation on how widespread the practice was or since when.

So the honest version is this: it is a local custom, attested, whose extent I cannot tell you. It is neither a restaurant rule nor a dogma. If you want to try it, the logic is consistent with everything else: Lambrusco is fresh, acidic, lightly fizzy and low in tannin, which is exactly the profile the broth welcomes. Pour little, and do it at the last moment: red wine changes the colour of the dish irreversibly.

## Lambrusco: this is precisely its job

This is the simplest case. Lambrusco beside tortellini is a pairing of place before it is one of taste, and fortunately taste confirms it. But you have to choose with care, because the family is wide.

In the Modena area the appellations include, among others, Lambrusco di Sorbara DOC and Lambrusco Grasparossa di Castelvetro DOC. The first is the lightest and most elegant, pale in colour, with a floral profile and a cutting acidity: the natural candidate for a bowl of tortellini. The second is darker and more substantial, with tannin you can really feel: I would leave it to cotechino and bollito, not to the broth.

As for residual sugar, for tortellini stay with the dry style or close to it. A sweet Lambrusco asks for something sweet, not something salty.

## Pignoletto and Albana: the home whites

Those who prefer a white have, in Bologna, their ace. Colli Bolognesi Pignoletto has been a DOCG since 2010, and its rules require a large majority of Grechetto Gentile, in still, frizzante and spumante versions. A dry frizzante or spumante is the best fit: light, savoury, with a pleasant bitter edge on the finish that links well to the broth without covering the spice of the filling.

Albana di Romagna has a curious destiny: in 1987 it became the first Italian white to receive DOCG status, a decision still debated by those who consider it more political than qualitative. It exists in dry, amabile, sweet and passito versions. With broth only the dry works, and only if it is not too dense: Albana has body and an almost tannic note, and with a minimal dish it can feel cumbersome. It is better young and well chilled. For Romagna's cappelletti, with their richer cheese filling, it is a sensible choice.

## Sangiovese: yes, but with judgement

To speak of cappelletti in Romagna without naming Sangiovese would be an omission. The region's red is the one many people expect to see on the table, and the pairing can be done, as long as you stay with young, everyday, medium-bodied Sangiovese, served a little cooler than usual. No Riserva, no marked oak, no years in bottle: tannin and wood would smother the broth like a heavy blanket over a candle.

Sangiovese is the riskiest of the four options, and also the one that gives the most pleasure when it works. If other courses follow, perhaps a roast arriving right after, a Romagna red acts as a bridge between the two halves of the meal.

## My position

If I had to choose a single glass for a bowl of tortellini, I would open a dry Lambrusco di Sorbara, in a simple bottle, without hunting for a star. A wine that costs what it should and knows it is there to serve the dish, not steal the scene. Broth is not a pretext for showing off the cellar: it is the one dish of the year where the best wine is the one you notice least.
