In 1899 two Piedmontese arrived in the Nurra, the plain north of Alghero, with an idea that must have seemed reckless to many: to reclaim a difficult land and turn it into a great vineyard. They were Erminio Sella, nephew of the statesman Quintino Sella, and the lawyer Edgardo Mosca. This is the tenth and last portrait in the "Italian Excellence" series, on an island that has made wine for millennia but learned late how to tell its story.

## Two Piedmontese in the Nurra

The story of [Sella & Mosca](https://www.sellaemosca.com/) begins with pioneers' work: clearing, reclaiming, planting. Erminio Sella belonged to a family from Biella that gave Italy a statesman, bankers and also a great mountain photographer, Vittorio Sella; their photographs of the company's early decades are now kept in the winery's museum.

In 1903, on the estate's land, the necropolis of Anghelu Ruju came to light: thirty-eight tombs cut into the rock, the *domus de janas* of Sardinian tradition, the largest prehistoric necropolis on the island. Few producers in the world can claim such a coincidence: the vines grow beside a prehistoric burial site. The house took its name for its most unusual wine.

## The largest vineyard, the rarest grapes

Today the company has about 520 hectares around the estate, plus small plots in Gallura and in the south of the island: a single block that Forbes Italia, echoing the house, describes as the largest in Europe. Since 2016 it has been part of the Terra Moretti Vino group.

Size alone would not make Sella & Mosca a benchmark. A precise choice of grapes does. Alongside Cannonau and Vermentino, Sardinia's two emblematic grapes, which we wrote about in [Sardinia, Cannonau and Vermentino](/en/articles/2026.09.24/sardinia-wine-cannonau-vermentino/), the house has since the 1980s revived Torbato, an almost vanished white grape that in Italy is grown mainly here, around Alghero. Torbato also gives Oscarì, a traditional-method sparkling wine that the house presents as the first made from this grape. And there is Marchese di Villamarina, a pure Cabernet Sauvignon, proof of how well the Nurra can host grapes from far away too.

## Anghelu Ruju, the wine of the necropolis

The wine bearing the necropolis's name is the house's most singular. Anghelu Ruju is an Alghero Rosso DOC *liquoroso*, a fortified wine made from dried Cannonau grapes and aged for a long time in cask: an intense garnet red, sweet and strong, which according to retail technical sheets reaches around 18.5% alcohol. It is an end-of-meal wine, little known off the island, a reminder that Sardinia has a tradition of sweet and fortified wines that is often forgotten.

What remains, in the end, of a house like this? An industrial idea born of two pioneers, which managed to become a guardian of rare grapes. It is an unusual path for a company of this size, which usually tends to simplify its range rather than complicate it.

## What to taste first

A path that runs through every colour:

- **Torbato Terre Bianche**, Alghero DOC, pale straw with greenish glints, to meet the grape the house saved;
- one of the house's **Cannonau di Sardegna** wines, ruby red, for the island's character;
- **Anghelu Ruju**, garnet red, to drink at the end of a meal.

Prices vary by vintage and channel: Torbato, Vermentino and Cannonau sit among good everyday wines, while Anghelu Ruju, with its long ageing, is often sold as a gift bottle and costs considerably more.

## How to visit

The winery is at I Piani, along provincial road 42, a few kilometres from Alghero. The [Sella & Mosca museum](https://www.sardegnacultura.it/en/articles/alghero-museo-sella-e-mosca), also described by the Sardinian Region's cultural portal, has two sections: an archaeological one, devoted to the Anghelu Ruju necropolis with copies of the finds, and one on the company's history from 1899 to 1938, with photographs by Erminio and Vittorio Sella and old winemaking tools. Visits run all year round by booking, in Italian or English, with tastings and sales.

So the tour of the ten houses ends in Alghero. Ten different stories, one constant: in each, at some point, someone chose the less comfortable road. Two Piedmontese on a plain waiting to be reclaimed, in 1899, are perhaps the most extreme example.
