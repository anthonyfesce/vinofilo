The name comes from a novel, and not just any novel. In Giuseppe Tomasi di Lampedusa's The Leopard, Donnafugata is the Prince of Salina's estate, and the place name preserves the memory of a queen in flight: Maria Carolina, who at the start of the nineteenth century left Naples ahead of Napoleon's troops and found refuge in Sicily. According to the Rallo family, that refuge lay in the very lands where its vineyards grow today. This is the fifth portrait in the "Italian Excellence" series: a young house, founded in 1983, that changed the image of an entire island.

## Marsala, 1851

[Donnafugata](https://www.donnafugata.it/) is young only on paper. Giacomo Rallo was the fourth generation of a family that had been making wine in Marsala for over a century and a half: the family cellars, with their underground barrel room carved out of tufa, date from 1851. In 1983 Giacomo and his wife Gabriella, a pioneer of quality viticulture in Sicily, founded a new company starting from those cellars and from the vineyards of Contessa Entellina, in the hinterland of western Sicily. In the early 1990s their children, José and Antonio, joined; today they run the company.

The timing was right and the choice was brave. Sicily in the 1980s sold mostly bulk and blending wine; the Rallos bet on bottles with an identity, illustrated labels (since 1994 drawn by the illustrator Stefano Vitale) and names taken from literature. The style of their communication is still recognisable today, and it is no detail: it was one of the ways Sicilian wine learned to tell its own story.

## Pantelleria, son of the wind

The turning point came in 1989, when the family landed on Pantelleria. On this island, closer to Tunisia than to Palermo, Zibibbo grows as low bush vines in hollows dug into the ground to shelter them from the wind: a practice UNESCO inscribed in 2014 on its list of [intangible cultural heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-agricultural-practice-of-cultivating-the-vite-ad-alberello-head-trained-bush-vines-of-the-community-of-pantelleria-00720). The [Pantelleria estate](https://www.donnafugata.it/en/estates/pantelleria/) looks after more than forty kilometres of dry-stone walls in lava rock, and the family has donated to FAI, Italy's national trust, a *giardino pantesco*, the round stone enclosure that protects a single citrus tree.

From here comes Ben Ryé, a Passito di Pantelleria DOC that the house subtitles "son of the wind". We told the story of the island and its sweet wine in [Pantelleria and Zibibbo](/en/articles/2026.11.05/pantelleria-zibibbo-passito-bush-vines/): Ben Ryé has become its best-known ambassador, a sweet wine that managed to escape the niche of meditation wines.

## Four Sicilies, one idea

Today the house works in four territories: Contessa Entellina, Pantelleria, Etna, with vineyards on the northern slope at Randazzo, and Vittoria, where Cerasuolo is made. It is a map that tells the story of contemporary Sicilian wine well, made of very different zones. The house's emblematic red, Mille e una Notte, was born with the 1995 vintage from a collaboration with the oenologist Giacomo Tachis, the same man behind the Tignanello we discussed in the second chapter.

What makes it a benchmark? Donnafugata proved that in Sicily one could build a brand recognised worldwide without giving up the bond with place. The possible criticism is the other side of the coin: such a carefully crafted image sometimes risks speaking louder than the wine. But Ben Ryé, put to the test of the glass, carries the weight of its fame.

## What to taste first

A three-step path, with very different colours:

- **Anthìlia**, the white from Contessa Entellina, pale straw, to understand the starting point;
- **Mille e una Notte**, an intense ruby red, for the house's most ambitious side;
- **Ben Ryé**, deep gold with amber glints, from Zibibbo: for the 2019 vintage the technical sheet gives 14.5% alcohol, with a full sweetness.

Prices vary by vintage, format and channel; Ben Ryé also comes in half bottles, the most practical size for a sweet wine.

## How to visit

According to the house, the cellars at Marsala, at Randazzo on Etna and at Vittoria are open to visitors all year round, while the Pantelleria winery opens in summer; visitors number over twenty-four thousand a year. Bookings go through [visit.donnafugata.it](https://visit.donnafugata.it/). If you can choose, Pantelleria is the visit that explains everything: the wind, the black stone, the toil of a viticulture that cannot be mechanised.

One is left with the impression that Donnafugata has won the hardest bet, that of being modern without being generic. For a house named after a queen in flight, that is no small thing: it found a way to stay.
