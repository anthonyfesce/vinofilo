Burgundy has a rare talent: it manages to seem simple and baffling at the same time. Two grapes, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, do almost all the work on the Côte d'Or. Yet open any wine list and you face hundreds of names, many of which look like the same thing spelled two ways. The good news is that the system has a logic, and once you grasp it you never forget it. The bad news is that the logic is not about producers. It is about pieces of land.

## A pyramid built of places

In Bordeaux, what gets classified is the château, which is to say a business. In Burgundy, what gets classified is the ground. That difference explains everything else, and an Italian reader can grasp it by thinking of Barolo: there, since 2010, labels may carry one of 181 *menzioni geografiche aggiuntive*, or MGAs, which are in effect official vineyard names. With one fundamental difference: in Piedmont the MGAs set no hierarchy, while in Burgundy the hierarchy is written down in black and white.

There are 84 appellations, most of them born from 1936 onwards, when France built its AOC system. They fall into four tiers.

At the bottom sit the **regional** appellations, such as Bourgogne or Bourgogne Côte d'Or: grapes that may come from a broad area. They account for the bulk of the wine made, more than half of the total according to the latest five-year averages. Above them come the 44 **village** appellations, which carry the name of a commune: Pommard, Meursault, Gevrey-Chambertin. Then the **premier crus**, which are not appellations in their own right but specific vineyards within a village: across the region there are 662 of them. At the top, the 33 **grand crus**, each an appellation of its own.

## The label that reads backwards

Here comes the trick that trips up everyone. The higher you climb the pyramid, the less the label tells you. A village wine gives the name of the commune. A premier cru gives the commune followed by the name of the vineyard, a Meursault, say, with the name of the parcel beside it. A grand cru, on the other hand, carries only the vineyard name: Montrachet, Corton, Chambertin. No commune, no explanation. It is taken for granted that the buyer already knows.

To complicate matters, many villages long ago adopted the habit of hitching their most celebrated vineyard to their own name. So Gevrey-Chambertin is a village wine, while Chambertin on its own is a grand cru. The longer name, against every instinct, signals the more modest wine. It is a system devised by insiders for insiders, and it has a faintly snobbish charm that there is no point denying.

## The climats, or a thousand names for a thousand plots

In 2015 UNESCO added the Climats du vignoble de Bourgogne to its World Heritage list. In Burgundy the word *climat* has nothing to do with the weather: it means a delimited vineyard plot with a name of its own and a character recognised over centuries of observation. There are roughly 1,247 of them, and the listing also takes in the historic centre of Dijon, where for centuries these plots were recorded and regulated, and the town of Beaune, the heart of the trade.

What makes the listing interesting is that it did not reward a picture-postcard landscape but an idea: that a few metres apart the same grape gives different wines, and that each difference deserves a name. Sources trace this fragmentation partly to the monks, with the spread of the Cistercian order from the 11th century, and then to the burghers who bought vineyards in the Middle Ages. Anyone who knows Etna, where the *contrade*, the local named vineyard areas, do a similar job, will recognise the same obsession with detail. Here, though, it has had a thousand years to become law.

## Côte de Nuits and Côte de Beaune, two halves of a ribbon

The Côte d'Or is a thin strip of east-facing hills that begins just south of Dijon and runs southwards. It splits in two.

The **Côte de Nuits**, to the north, is Pinot Noir country. This is where you find the villages that anyone who has leafed through a grand restaurant’s wine list will have seen at least once: Gevrey-Chambertin, Chambolle-Musigny, Vosne-Romanée, Nuits-Saint-Georges. The great majority of the region’s red grand crus are concentrated in this half.

The **Côte de Beaune**, to the south, runs for about 20 kilometres from Ladoix-Serrigny to the hills of the Maranges, with vineyards that rarely exceed a few hundred metres in width. This is the land of the great whites: Meursault, Puligny- and Chassagne-Montrachet, and the Montrachet family, home to some of the most famous Chardonnay grand crus in the world. Red is not lacking, from Volnay to Pommard, but the Côte de Beaune’s only red grand cru is Corton.

Beyond the Côte d'Or, Burgundy carries on: Chablis to the north, with a single grand cru divided into several climats and some forty premier cru climats, then the Côte Chalonnaise and the Mâconnais to the south. But it was on that narrow ribbon of the Côte d'Or that the legend was built.

## What it means for those not born in Beaune

Faced with so much precision, the temptation is to think that climbing a rung is enough to drink better. It is not that simple. A carelessly made grand cru is still a disappointing wine with an important name, and a good village wine from a meticulous grower can say more than a tired premier cru. The hierarchy describes the potential of the land, not the hand of whoever farms it. That is also why in Burgundy the producer’s name matters as much as the vineyard’s, even if it often takes up less room on the label.

My view is that Burgundy should be learned like a foreign language: not all at once, but one village at a time. The rest of the wine world, Italy included, has looked to it as a model for decades, and Barolo’s MGAs and Etna’s contrade are proof that the idea is catching. But borrowing the vocabulary is easy. The hard part is having a thousand years of patience to fill it with meaning.
