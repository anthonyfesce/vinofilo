There is an Italian wine that is drunk at cellar temperature, that tastes of toasted hazelnut and the sea, that spends years in casks deliberately left half empty and that, to anyone tasting it for the first time, brings Andalusia to mind. It is not an imitation and it is not a museum curiosity: it is Vernaccia di Oristano, and the fact that many Italians have never drunk it says more about us than about the wine.

## An ancient wine, a pioneering DOC

Vernaccia di Oristano comes from the lower Tirso valley, on the west coast of Sardinia, in some twenty communes around Oristano. It was the island’s first DOC: the decree of recognition is dated 11 August 1971, five years after Vernaccia di San Gimignano had inaugurated Italy’s DOCs. Two wines with the same name and no kinship, incidentally: DNA analysis shows that the Sardinian Vernaccia is a variety distinct from the other grapes called “vernaccia” on the mainland, a name that, according to the most common etymology, goes back to the Latin *vernaculus*, “local”.

The roots run deep. In the Cabras area, in a Bronze Age well, vine seeds dated to around three thousand years ago have been found, one of the finds that point to a very ancient Sardinian viticulture. And the first written mention of the wine is traced to a legal document of 1327, the Breve di Villa di Chiesa, today’s Iglesias. The grape, according to ampelographers, may well have originated in the Tirso valley itself.

## Flor, the veil that protects

The heart of the matter lies in the cellar. The wine is put into wooden casks, often chestnut as well as oak, which are never filled to the brim. On that surface exposed to the air, instead of turning to vinegar, the wine grows a veil of yeasts: flor. In Sardinia it goes by dialect names, and the aroma it produces, somewhere between noble mould and hazelnut, has its own Campidanese term, *murrai*.

The veil does two things at once. On the one hand it shields the wine from too rapid oxidation; on the other it slowly transforms it, consuming part of what it contains and leaving aromas of dried fruit, almond, bread crust and salt air. Over the years, as the veil thins, oxidation takes over and the colour slides towards deep gold and amber.

This is where anyone familiar with Sherry recognises something. In Jerez flor is the star of Fino and Manzanilla, and there too the work is done in part-filled casks, with ageing systems in which wines of different ages are blended over time: the solera. Sardinia uses similar systems of topping up between wines of different ages, which make Vernaccia, like Sherry, a wine that often carries more than one vintage into the bottle.

## A relative, not a copy

The stylistic kinship with Sherry is real, but it pays to be precise about where it ends. Sherry is made from another grape, Palomino, on white albariza soils, and it is fortified with added spirit. Vernaccia di Oristano, in its most classic form, is not fortified: Sardinian grapes ripen under the Campidano sun to high natural sugar levels, and the wine reaches considerable strength on its own. There is also a fortified version, the *liquoroso*, dry or sweet, but it is not the archetype.

There is another difference, less technical and more cultural. Sherry was an export product for centuries, shaped by the taste of English merchants. Vernaccia remained a wine of the home, tied to feast days, to almond pastries, to Sardinian hospitality: the small glass offered to a guest before the coffee has even arrived. Anyone looking for a closer comparison in terms of method might also think of the vin jaune of the Jura, which matures under a similar veil, but Vernaccia has a Mediterranean salinity found nowhere else.

## How to read a bottle

The production rules provide for a basic version, a Superiore and a Riserva, as well as the Liquoroso, with minimum ageing periods in wood that lengthen as you climb the categories and that are measured in years, not months. The rules have been revised over time, so it is worth reading the label carefully: the word Riserva signals the longest stay. Many producers also keep the wine in cask well beyond the minimum, and it is not unusual to find Vernaccias with a decade or more behind them.

At the table it works surprisingly well. With grey mullet bottarga, which comes from the very lagoons of the Stagno di Cabras, it makes an almost tautological pairing, salt with salt. It stands up to aged cheeses, smoked fish and robust soups. And of course it goes with Sardinian almond sweets, even in its dry version, because its dried-fruit note speaks to almond paste better than many sweet wines do. Serve it cool but not chilled, in a small glass; an open bottle holds for days without suffering, as befits a wine already used to air.

## A heritage at risk of indifference

Vernaccia di Oristano is a hard wine to sell, and there is no point pretending otherwise. It demands time, wood, cellar space and a public willing to taste something that resembles nothing else on the shelf. There is frequent talk of abandoned vineyards and a shrinking area, but there are no recent, verifiable figures to say by how much: a point on which the consortium and the region would do well to shed some light.

My view is that Vernaccia does not need to be explained as “Sardinian Sherry”, a convenient but reductive formula. It needs to be drunk by people who live in Italy before it is drunk by foreigners. Every year we celebrate grapes with thirty years of commercial history and ignore a wine that has had a DOC since 1971, a method unique in the country and a bond with its land that few can match. If there is an end-of-year present to give a curious friend, this is it.
