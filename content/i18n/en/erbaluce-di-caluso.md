Think of Piedmontese wine and you almost always look south, towards the Langhe and Monferrato. Yet half an hour from Turin, heading up towards the Aosta Valley, lies a basin shaped by a glacier that has given Italy one of its most singular whites. It is called Erbaluce, it grows on pergolas as tall as a courtyard arbour, and it has an acidity that apologises to no one. The paradox is that some foreign enthusiasts know it better than many people in Turin.

## A basin carved by ice

The landscape has to be understood before the wine. During the Quaternary glaciations the great Dora Baltea glacier flowed down from the Aosta Valley and, spreading out onto the plain, pushed vast quantities of debris ahead of it and along its flanks. When it retreated it left the morainic amphitheatre of Ivrea: a semicircle of hills, lakes and peat bogs that reads clearly even on a map.

Its most spectacular rim is the Serra d'Ivrea, a lateral moraine some twenty kilometres long, as straight as a line drawn with a ruler, running from the slopes of Mombarone to Lake Viverone. It is often described as the largest formation of its kind in Europe. The appellation's rules speak of a “morainic basin” enclosed between the ridges of Ivrea and Caluso, with the vineyards on the south-facing slopes.

This is where the soils get their character: sandy, stony, poor and naturally acidic. It is not a detail for geologists only. Soils like these, combined with a cool climate at the foot of the Alps, explain why Erbaluce reaches harvest with an acidity that elsewhere would merely make it taste unripe, and that here is its backbone.

## A thick-skinned grape with a luminous name

Erbaluce is an ancient and very local white grape. The first mention considered reliable dates from 1606, in a text produced at the court of Duke Charles Emmanuel I of Savoy, which already describes its thick skins and bunches able to hang on the vine for a long time. Theories of older origins, Roman or even pre-Roman, have circulated for years but remain conjecture.

There are two schools on the name. The prosaic one links it to the colour of the ripe bunches, which in the autumn sun turn golden, almost copper-toned: a grape that seems to “give off light”. The poetic one tells of the nymph Albaluce, daughter of Dawn and the Sun, from whose tears the vine was born. Take your pick; the wine stays the same.

What does change is how it is grown. The traditional system is the Canavese pergola, the *topia* in local dialect, with very widely spaced rows, more than five metres according to the rules, and around a thousand vines per hectare. This is not folklore: Erbaluce bears little fruit on the first buds of the cane and needs long pruning, which the pergola accommodates. Walking beneath it in September, with the bunches overhead, is one of those agricultural landscapes that are becoming rare in Italy.

## One DOCG, three wines

Erbaluce di Caluso became a DOC in 1967, among the first in Italy, and a DOCG in 2010. The zone takes in a few dozen communes across the provinces of Turin, Biella and Vercelli (the rules themselves waver between 36 and 37), for an area that sources put at around 200 hectares: a drop in the ocean of Piedmont's vineyard. The appellation can also be labelled simply “Caluso”, and it covers three very different styles.

The **dry** wine is the most common: a taut, savoury white with scents of white flowers, herbs and citrus that can seem almost angular when young. Curiously, its success is recent: for a long time the grape was thought of mainly for passito, and the still white only really took off from the late 1990s. Among the key moments in its story is the Cantina della Serra, backed in the post-war years by Adriano Olivetti, a reminder that in Ivrea industry and countryside talked to each other more than you might think.

The **sparkling** wine must be traditional method, with at least 15 months on the lees, and it makes sense for obvious reasons: anyone who needs high-acid grapes for bubbles finds them ready-made here. Some cuvées stay on the lees for years, showing how well Erbaluce can gain complexity without losing its nerve.

The **passito** is the historic style and the most demanding. The best bunches are laid on racks to dry for months, until sugars exceed 29%; the wine must then age for at least 36 months, 48 for the Riserva, and cannot be released before the third or fourth autumn after the harvest. The result, with at least 70 grams of residual sugar per litre, is sweet but far from cloying, because that famous acidity keeps it upright. We have already met it on our tour of Italian sweet wines: Caluso Passito deserves its place in that company.

## At the table, in Canavese and beyond

The dry wine is a white for regional cooking: Piedmontese antipasti, herb frittatas, lake fish, the fresh cheeses of the valleys. Its acidity also makes it an excellent partner for fried food and slightly rich dishes, where a softer white would slump. The sparkling version does its job at aperitivo time and beyond. The passito calls for dry biscuits and pastries, blue cheeses and, for those who want to stay local, the traditional sweets of the Canavese.

A practical tip, not a commercial one: dry Erbaluce is not afraid of a few years in bottle. Many drink it the summer after the harvest, which is perfectly fine, but those with the patience to wait discover broader notes, almost of honey and dried fruit, without the freshness slipping away.

## Why it is worth the detour

My view is that Erbaluce is one of the most underrated Italian whites relative to what it can do. It has a unique landscape, a history documented for more than four centuries, and three styles that show genuine versatility rather than marketing gimmicks. Its limit is size: with so few hectares it can never be a mass-market wine, and perhaps that is just as well. But anyone who lives in Piedmont, or passes through, has no excuse: before reaching for yet another fashionable white, it is worth heading up towards Ivrea and letting yourself be surprised by a wine the ice prepared a very long time ago.
