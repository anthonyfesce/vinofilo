Take wine out of Italian literature and almost everything would still stand, but the air would be colder. It is not the hero of our classics, and it is rarely an object of worship as it is for the Persian poets or the 19th-century French novelists. It is more a unit of measurement: it tells you who is rich and who is not, who works and who celebrates, where we are and in which century. Following it from Dante to Soldati means reading another history of Italy, written in the margins.

## Dante and Boccaccio, the sun and the river

The first truly memorable wine in the Italian language is never drunk. It sits in the twenty-fifth canto of *Purgatorio*, where Statius explains how the soul joins the body and, to make himself understood, reaches for a farmer’s image: “Guarda il calor del sol che si fa vino, / giunto a l’omor che de la vite cola” (see the sun’s heat that becomes wine when joined to the sap that flows from the vine). The sun enters the vine’s juice and becomes something else, just as the soul enters matter. It is theology, but made with the harvest. And it says a great deal about the 14th century that a poet could explain the highest mystery through a process every reader had seen with their own eyes.

Boccaccio, half a century later, takes wine to the tavern and to the practical joke. In the *Decameron* (eighth day, third tale) Calandrino’s friends describe to him the land of Bengodi, where there is a mountain of grated Parmesan and there flows “un fiumicel di vernaccia, della migliore che mai si bevve” (a little river of Vernaccia, the best ever drunk). It is a prank, and Calandrino falls for it. But the choice of wine is no accident: Vernaccia was then a name that spelt luxury, a wine to long for. The poor man’s paradise, for Boccaccio, is a place where good wine costs nothing and has not been watered down.

## Redi and the king of Montepulciano

Then comes the 17th century, and wine becomes a literary genre. Francesco Redi, physician and naturalist at the Medici court, published *Bacco in Toscana* in 1685, a dithyramb in which the god, drunk and ever more enthusiastic, reviews the wines of the region with Ariadne at his side. The line everyone remembers is the final verdict: “Montepulciano d’ogni vino è il re” (Montepulciano is king of every wine). We have written about it in our piece on Vino Nobile, and that line has done more for the fame of an appellation than any advertising campaign.

The beauty of the dithyramb is that it reads like a breathless wine list. Redi was a rigorous scientist, and here he allows himself the luxury of disorder. Yet even in the disorder there is a modern idea: wine carries the name of a place, and places can be ranked. It is a thought that the *disciplinari*, the appellation rulebooks, would formalise almost three centuries later.

## Pascoli, Pavese, Fenoglio: wine as toil

In the 19th and 20th centuries the tone changes. Wine comes down from the court and returns to the fields, but not to celebrate them. Pascoli, who wrote the countryside as few others have, leaves it on the sidelines: in his world of hedges, ploughs and birds, wine appears more as a domestic, convivial gesture, the tavern, the table, than as a subject. Anyone looking for a bard of wine in his verse comes away slightly disappointed, and that is a valuable clue: for rural Italy at the end of the 19th century, wine was something obvious, not a theme.

With Cesare Pavese and Beppe Fenoglio we are in the Langhe, and the contrast with today’s image is almost brutal. Pavese was born in 1908 in Santo Stefano Belbo, and in *La luna e i falò* (The Moon and the Bonfires, 1950) he brings his narrator back to hills where vineyards mean work, debt, seasons and sharecropping. The harvest is no picture-postcard festival but a calendar that rules people’s lives. Fenoglio, a native of Alba, digs deeper still with *La malora* (1954): a poor Langhe, where people work other men’s land and good wine is seen going past more often than drunk.

Anyone arriving in Alba today for Barolo should pack one of these two books. Not to feel guilty, but to understand that the prestige of those hills is very recent, and that it is built on a hardship literature recorded when nobody else did.

## Soldati, the first reporter

And then there is Mario Soldati, who turned wine into an investigation. As early as 1956 he made *Viaggio lungo la valle del Po alla ricerca dei cibi genuini* for RAI, Italian state television, one of the first Italian programmes in which food became a story about place. In the 1970s came the three journeys of *Vino al vino*, the third published by Mondadori in 1976, later gathered into a single volume. Soldati walks into cellars, talks to growers and flags wines on the verge of disappearing: we met him in our piece on the reds of Friuli, on the trail of a red with only a handful of producers left.

His core idea is summed up in a sentence often quoted in his name: “La nobiltà del vino è proprio questa: che non è mai un oggetto staccato e astratto” (the nobility of wine is precisely this: it is never a detached, abstract object). For Soldati, wine cannot be judged from a glass poured far from where it was born. It has to be sought on the spot, together with the people and the landscape. Today we would call it terroir, and we have turned it into a slogan. He practised it, with a notebook and a great deal of patience.

## A lesson for today’s drinkers

Read in sequence, these writers say something simple: Italian wine was for centuries an everyday matter, and that is precisely why our writers used it more as a lens than as an object. Dante explains the soul, Boccaccio hunger, Redi the pleasure of the list, Pavese and Fenoglio poverty, Soldati the value of place.

My view is that we should go back to reading them before the guides. Guides tell us what to buy. These books tell us why, in Italy, a glass of wine is never just a glass of wine. And they remind us that the real rarity is not the unobtainable bottle, but the ability to tell where it comes from.
