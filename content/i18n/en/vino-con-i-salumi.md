A board of *salumi*, Italian cured meats, is the one Italian dish that everyone agrees on and that almost nobody really thinks about matching. You open whatever is around, you pour, you raise a glass. Yet between a slice of sweet prosciutto and a spoonful of 'nduja lies the same distance that separates a sorbet from a chilli pepper, and asking a single wine to referee the lot is a little naive. The good news is that the rules are few, and nearly all of them are common sense.

## The basic rule: fat calls for acidity and bubbles

Almost every cured meat has two main ingredients: fat and salt. Fat coats the mouth; salt dries it and makes you thirsty. The right wine has two jobs: to cleanse and to refresh. Acidity does this, cutting through the oily sensation, and so does carbon dioxide, which has an almost mechanical effect, like a cloth wiped across the palate.

The enemy, by contrast, is heavy tannin. A very structured red, alongside the salt in cured meats, tends to turn more bitter and harder; and the fat, though it softens it, is not enough to compensate. That is why big age-worthy reds are rarely the best choice, unless the salume is very savoury and long-aged. It is a rule that applies to cheese too, which we have already written about: a mixed board only makes it more pressing.

Then there is temperature. A red served cool, around 14-16 °C, works far better with cured meats than the same wine at living-room temperature: the alcohol recedes, the fruit and acidity come forward.

## Lambrusco, or the Emilian answer

If any region solved this problem centuries ago it is Emilia, where cured meats and Lambrusco grow up on the same plain. A dry, frizzante Lambrusco, with its violet foam and lively acidity, does exactly what is needed with mortadella, salame and cotechino: it refreshes, cuts the fat and keeps company without taking over. We have already defended Lambrusco against its bad reputation; here it is enough to say that, faced with a board of salumi, it is hard to do better.

The practical rule is to choose the style according to the meat. A Lambrusco di Sorbara, paler, sharper and more floral, gets along with the more delicate flavours. A Grasparossa, darker and with a thread of tannin, stands up better to aged salami and fattier preparations. And the *amabile* Lambruschi, those with some residual sugar, are best kept for something else: with the salt of cured meats the effect can turn cloying.

## Prosciutto, culatello, mortadella: the gentle ones

Sweet **prosciutto crudo**, such as Parma or San Daniele, both DOP, has a delicate flavour that is easily lost under an overly intense wine. Dry sparkling wines work well here: a traditional-method Franciacorta, Trento or Alta Langa, or a Prosecco or a lighter Lambrusco di Sorbara. A fresh, unoaked white, such as a Friulano in the case of San Daniele, is also a regional pairing that makes a lot of sense.

**Culatello**, a DOP speciality of the Bassa parmense, the low-lying country along the Po near Parma, is the noblest of Emilian cured meats, made from the finest part of the leg and aged in damp cellars among the river fogs. It is more intense, softer and more fragrant than prosciutto. It suits the local wines, sparkling and dry: a well-made Lambrusco, a dry Malvasia from the Colli di Parma, or Fortana, the area’s frizzante red. More structured traditional-method sparklers, with a few years on the lees, are the luxury alternative.

**Mortadella**, whose most famous version is Mortadella Bologna IGP, is sweet, spiced and just fatty enough. Above all it wants freshness: a Lambrusco, a frizzante Pignoletto from the Colli Bolognesi, a lively rosé. Anyone who wants a still red should choose one that is young, low in tannin and served cool.

## Salame, lardo and 'nduja: when flavour raises its voice

**Salame** is the broadest and most variable category in Italy: from Felino to the Hungarian-style *ungherese*, from Tuscan finocchiona to wild-boar salami, grind, spices and ageing all change. In general, the more aged and spiced it is, the more it can ask for a red with a little body: a young Barbera, a current-vintage Chianti, a frizzante Bonarda from the Oltrepò, a Rosso di Montalcino without too much oak. With finocchiona, Sangiovese from the same land remains a natural reference point.

**Lardo di Colonnata**, an IGP, comes from a small village among the marble quarries near Carrara, where it is salted and left to cure with aromatic herbs and spices in marble basins. Sliced paper-thin over warm bread, it practically melts on its own. It is all fat and fragrance, and it needs a wine that cuts through it decisively: a Vermentino from the Colli di Luni, just next door, or a dry, taut sparkling wine. A tannic red here would be a mistake.

**'Nduja**, the spreadable Calabrian salume linked above all to Spilinga, in the province of Vibo Valentia, is a case apart: fat and chilli in generous quantities. Heat amplifies alcohol and tannin, so very alcoholic or structured wines are best avoided, as we saw when writing about spicy food. Better choices are a southern rosé served well chilled, a Cirò rosato, a young, juicy red served cool or, for those who like surprises, an aromatic white with a touch of residual sugar, which softens the burn.

## And if the board is mixed?

In real life a board is almost never single-themed. In that case the safest wine is the one that can do a bit of everything: a dry Lambrusco, a traditional-method sparkler that is not too slight, or a light, fresh red served at cellar temperature, such as a Grignolino, a young Dolcetto or a Bardolino. If you want to do things properly, two bottles are better than one: a sparkling wine for the gentler meats, a fresh red for the more savoury ones.

## My view

I think the most common mistake with cured meats is deference: people assume a great salume deserves a great red, and open the most important wine in the cellar. It is almost always the wrong choice. Cured meats ask for cheerful, fresh, often sparkling wines that aren’t afraid of seeming simple. It is no coincidence that local traditions, from Lambrusco’s Emilia to Vermentino’s Liguria, got there long before the sommeliers did. At the table, now and then, it pays to trust your grandparents.
