Grignolino is the wine that proves wrong anyone who judges a red by its colour. In the glass it looks almost like a deep rosé, a light ruby that turns orange-tinged with age. Then you drink it, and in comes a dry tannin, a bitter twist, an acidity that does not ask permission. It is not an obliging wine, and it has never done much to become one. Perhaps that is why Piedmont, after loving it for so long, nearly lost it along the way.

## A name made of pips

The most widely accepted etymology leads to the Piedmontese *grignole*, grape pips. Grignolino berries are small, with little pulp and up to five seeds each, and it is the seeds that release tannin during fermentation. There is another, more evocative theory: the name would come from *grigné*, the tight-lipped grimace the tannin draws from whoever tastes it. True or not, it describes the effect perfectly.

The roots run deep. The most often cited reference dates from 1249, when a wine called *Barbexinus* appears in the archives of the cathedral chapter of Casale Monferrato; Barbesino is still one of the grape’s synonyms today. For centuries Grignolino was a prestigious wine in the hills between Asti and Casale, and for a long time one of Piedmont’s most admired reds, before Barolo and Barbaresco took the stage.

## A grape that refuses to be tamed

The difficult character is not confined to the glass. In the vineyard Grignolino is temperamental: it suffers from millerandage, bunches with berries that ripen unevenly, a problem that varies from vintage to vintage and weighs on both quality and yield. It also mutates easily, and its clones can give very different wines, from vegetal and slightly herbaceous to raspberry. And it is acutely sensitive to site: the same grape, a few kilometres away, can change register.

Little wonder that plantings are small. The estimates in circulation speak of barely a thousand hectares, a figure best taken as an order of magnitude. Outside Italy it is a curiosity: a few rows in California, mainly in the Napa Valley and the Santa Clara Valley.

## Asti and Casale, two DOCs for the same awkward temper

There are two main denominations, among the oldest in Piedmont. Grignolino d'Asti DOC has its production rules set by a decree of 29 May 1973; Grignolino del Monferrato Casalese DOC, in the hills of the province of Alessandria, by a decree of 26 June 1974. The basic rules are similar: at least 90% Grignolino, with Freisa allowed up to 10%, and a minimum of 11% vol. Many of the most careful producers, however, make it as a single varietal. There is also Piemonte DOC Grignolino, which extends the area to the whole region.

For much of the twentieth century, though, the denominations counted for little. Until the 1970s Grignolino was rarely bottled where it was grown: grapes and bulk wine often went to the large houses of Alba, which bottled it themselves. The territory produced; others signed. The revival began when a handful of estates in the Casale and Asti parts of Monferrato decided to bottle their own and to take seriously a wine many saw as nothing more than a snack-time red.

## Anarchic, by definition

The adjective that has followed Grignolino for decades is “anarchic”, and it is attributed to Luigi Veronelli, the critic who did more than anyone to tell the story of Italy’s farmhouse wines. Some sources give the phrase as *anarchico testadura*, “anarchic and pig-headed”. A *testa balorda*, “crackpot”, also circulates, but there is no documented trace of it in Veronelli’s writings, and today it is mainly the name of a wine label: better to treat it as a nickname born elsewhere. The sense remains, and it is apt: this is a wine that obeys neither the rules of reds nor those of rosés.

The debate over oak proves it. In Monferrato it was long said that “Grignolino and wood don’t get along”, and classic Grignolino is a young wine, to be drunk within a few years, without time in barrel. In 2015 a group of producers set out to test the proverb with Monferace, a project named after the ancient name of Monferrato: 100% Grignolino, at least 40 months of ageing, 24 of them in wood. It is a bet on ageing a wine that traditionally did not age, and only time will tell how well it holds.

## How to treat it at the table

Grignolino needs food. Its tannin and acidity work well with fried dishes, white meats and the less opulent side of Piedmontese cooking, and local tradition also pairs it with fish and antipasti. On temperature, what we wrote about chilled reds applies: the pale colour invites you to cool it, but too much cold accentuates precisely the tannin. Cellar-cool is better than ice-cold.

## The value of an awkward wine

My view is that Grignolino is one of Italy’s most contemporary reds, precisely because it has never been fashionable. Today people look for wines that are light in colour, low in alcohol, fresh and food-friendly: it was all of that back when those adjectives were used to downgrade a wine. The risk now is the opposite: softening it to please everyone. That would be a mistake. A Grignolino without its edges is just another pale red. Its charm lies in the grimace it pulls from you at the first sip, and in the smile that follows right after.
